AI-empowered and algorithm-driven automated ﬁnancial advisory systems, also known as Robo-advisors, have
been rapidly implemented by service providers and customers in ﬁnancial service markets. Yet, few empirical
studies investigate customers’ experience interacting with fully functional Robo-advisors in real-life scenarios.
Also, it is still unknown how the design of the automated system can affect customers’ perception and adoption of
this new technology. To mitigate these gaps, 24 participants with different levels of experience and understanding
of ﬁnancial investment were asked to use a Robo-advisor from a retail bank and perform the tasks. By conducting
observations and retrospective post-test interviews, we ﬁnd that participants do not fully perceive the social
aspects supposed to be provided by Robo-advisors. The overarching problems are, among others, a lack of
transparency and incomprehensible information. This results in distrust of the results generated by this system,
which negatively affects customers’ adoption of the investment advice provided by the Robo-advisor. The po-
tential of interactive data visualization is also detected. This work contributes to the understanding of customers
regarding their perception and adoption based on their use of a functional Robo-advisor and proposes design
takeaways for transparent and comprehensible automated advisory systems in ﬁnancial service contexts.

1. Introduction

AI-empowered and algorithm-driven automated systems have been
rapidly implemented and are commonly used in people’s everyday life.
This type of technology enables the automation of services by eliminating
human intervention; and has been claimed to provide disruptive poten-
tial and is widely used across service sectors and industries, including
managerial decision-making (Jarrahi, 2018; Shrestha, Ben-Menahem, &
Von Krogh, 2019), customized recommendations (Jameson, Konstan, &
Riedl, 2002; Namjun, Hosoo, Sangman, & HWANG, 2019), medical care
(Cai, Winter, Steiner, Wilcox, & Terry, 2019; Holzinger, Biemann, Pat-
tichis, & Kell, 2017; Yang, Steinfeld, & Zimmerman, 2019), and auto-
mobiles (Azaria, Rosenfeld, Kraus, Goldman, & Tsimhoni, 2015). In the
personal ﬁnance context, a type of algorithm-driven automated ﬁnancial
advisory service on the mobile terminal, also known as “Robo-advisor” or
“Robo-advisory” in business management and ﬁnancial communities, has
become popular among customers of retail banks and FinTech com-
panies, especially during the COVID-19 when people have become more
open for digital and automated service (Ben-David & Sade, 2020; Gan,
Khan, & Liew, 2021; Guo & Polak, 2021; Insider Intelligence, 2021).

The emergence and development of Robo-advisors (RAs) were
initially driven by ﬁnancial service providers (e.g., retail banks) to
replace human advisors’ services to cut labor costs and transfer tradi-
tional services from ofﬂine to online (Fisch, Labour(cid:2)e, & Turner, 2019).
Also, service providers want to attract more customers who are different
from those in conventional advisory services by promoting RAs’ low cost,
ﬂexibility, accessibility 24/7, and the same service quality as a human
advisor. Robo-advisors have shown their potential for mitigating biases
and conﬂicts of interest in human advisory encounters (Bolton, Freixas, &
Shapiro, 2007; Burke, Hung, Clift, Garber, & Yoong, 2015; Faloon &
Scherer, 2017) and have proven their commercial values in markets. UP
to January 2022, two independent leading RA companies in the world
reported double-digit increases in account openings during the pandemic
(Insider Intelligence, 2021).

Despite RAs’ rapid growth, compared with the conventional advisory
service, the perception of RAs is unclear, and their adoption is still low
among customers in the ﬁnancial service market (Brown, 2017; Erin EI,
2020; Jung, Dorner, Weinhardt, & Pusmaz, 2018). In Information Sys-
tems and Business Studies, research mainly focuses on customers’

Transparency and trust are complex and particularly signiﬁcant topics
when AI-empowered systems are contextualized in customers’ ﬁnancial
decisions. Financial decisions can be high-stake behaviors and customers
face the possibility to lose their money. Customers in ﬁnancial services,
unlike users in other automated systems, tend to be “vulnerable” (Mogaji,
Soetan, & Kieu, 2021). Any unfair or untrustworthy perception will
adversely and negatively affect customers’ adoption. Customers’ under-
standing of the information provided by RAs is also essential. Due to the
lack of human experts’ assistance or explanation, customers highly
depend on their own understating when using RAs, in which, most in-
formation is in written form or data visualization (Salo & Haapio, 2017).
Design research in the internet-based era came up with solutions as
collaborative interfaces or platforms that enhance perceived trans-
parency and comprehensibility (Kilic, Heinrich, & Schwabe, 2015;
Nueesch, Puschmann, & Alt, 2014, 2016; Nussbaumer, Matter, Reto (cid:3)a;
Nussbaumer & Matter, 2011; Porta, et al., 2012). However, it is difﬁcult
for these design solutions to adapt to the AI-empowered system. Without
human intervention, the “black-box” nature of the algorithm embedded
in this system makes it difﬁcult for users to understand why and how this
decision (i.e., recommended investment portfolio) is made and claimed
to be “optimized” for them. This might lead to distrust and low adoption
even if the AI’s decision is accurate (Dikmen & Burns, 2022). Two ap-
proaches are detected by researchers in Human-computer/robot Inter-
action (HCI/HRI). One of them is the anthropomorphic design which
aims to compensate for the lack of human advisors in the automated
process (Adam, Toutaoui, Pfeuffer, & Hinz, 2020; Deng & Chau, 2021;
Hildebrand & Bergner, 2021; Morana, Gnewuch, Jung, & Granig, 2020).
But the requirement of human-like design has not been theoretically
problematized in empirical studies. Even though those studies have
proved that the perceived social presence can delegate more trust, these
studies have not shown whether a human-like robot is useful for
achieving customers’ goals in ﬁnancial service contexts. The other
approach is transparent and responsible AI systems in ﬁnancial services
(Anshari, Almunawar, Masri, & Hrdy, 2021; Stefanel & Goyal, 2019;
Zheng, Zhu, Li, Chen, & Tan, 2019). Yet, existing research rarely illus-
trates which type of transparency is lacking and what is needed based on
users’ lived experiences and how to incorporate users’ demands into
existing systems. Moreover, RAs are multi-stakeholder autonomous sys-
tems (Sonboli, Smith, Cabral Berenfus, Burke, & Fiesler, 2021), in which
the customer’s proﬁts are integrated with providers’ business goals and
ﬁnancial regulations. All these factors make it a complex but urgent issue
to ﬁgure out the relationship between RAs’ system design and customers’
perception and adoption of RAs.

The research gaps identiﬁed in previous literature are: (1) few
empirical studies investigate customers’ experience interacting with fully

functional RAs in real-life scenarios; (2) the relationship between the
design of the automated system and customers’ perception and adoption
of RAs is still unknown. This study aims to understand customers’
experience of using a RA, particularly to shed light on how the system
design can affect their perception and adoption of RAs. Perception fo-
cuses on customers’ holistic experience and understanding of the RA
system and service based on their interaction throughout all aspects of
the customer’s journal, from onboarding to the ﬁnal generated portfolio.
Adoption addresses their intention and willingness to use RAs in the
future after they review the ﬁnal recommendation generated by RAs
based on their real-life situation (e.g., personal income, risk appetite,
loans and savings, etc.). The 24 participants with different levels of in-
vestment experience and ﬁnancial understanding joined the lab experi-
ment, and their demographics were also collected and considered in the
analysis. This paper discusses fully automated systems on the mobile end,
so a hybrid system is not within the scope. The user test approaches from
HCI include pre-test surveys, assigning scenarios and tasks, observing
performance, and collecting feedback from retrospective post-test in-
terviews. The research questions include: (1) how are customers able to
interact with a retail bank RA system; and (2) how does this experience
affect their perception and adoption of this RA system?

This article brieﬂy introduces the RA system and its implementation
in ﬁnancial service sectors. First, we present an interdisciplinary litera-
ture review of the state-of-art research that is relevant. Then, the
empirical user study is introduced regarding the test procedure, partici-
pant recruitment, data collection, and analysis. Because most RAs in
ﬁnancial markets have similar interfaces and features. The selected RA in
the empirical study is a representative RA application that enjoys a large
number of users and is produced by a well-known ﬁnancial group in
Scandinavia countries. Its name is anonymous. Last, ﬁndings are pre-
sented and discussed. This work ﬁnds the lag in customers’ perception of
RA compared with its business ambitions. Also, the perceived non-
transparency and incomprehensible information in the RA system
cause distrust, and those experiences negatively affect their adoption of
the portfolio generated by RA. However, the visualization tool is prom-
ising to ﬁll these gaps.

This work extends the knowledge of system design in customers’
perception and adoption of AI-empowered decisions in ﬁnancial services
by contributing empirical evidence and proving the user test as an
effective method. Also, it elicits the existing problems that can be
improved in the design of AI-empowered automated systems. Most
importantly, it articulates the demand from customers for a RA system,
improving transparency and comprehension. This compensates for prior
RA empirical studies by asking participants to use a well-functional retail
bank RA and view the result generated by AI.

2. Literature review

Starting with an introduction to RAs’ implementation, this part
summarizes existing research regarding RAs from interdisciplinary per-
spectives (e.g., information systems, service markets, design science) and
their challenges that can beneﬁt from this study. This section also in-
cludes the key concepts recurring in this study, such as transparency and
trust.

2.1. The implementation of RAs in ﬁnancial service

RAs use algorithms to provide customers with ﬁnancial advisory
services and support their investment decisions. This implementation is
standardized in the markets but might be slightly different according to
laws and regulations. Normally, these systems include four stages: (i)
personal data collection (customer’s savings, incomes, and preferences),
(ii) risk assessment (using a standardized survey to categorize customers
into different risk-apatite groups), (iii) wealth management advice
(providing an investment portfolio based on collected data and algo-
rithms), and (iv) continuous investment maintenance (Fisch et al., 2019;

automatically classiﬁed into different groups according to individual

other algorithm-based systems, this step is a “black box” (Litterscheidt &
Streich, 2020). Data visualization is commonly used in this step to convey
investment planning and the estimated performance of the recommended
portfolio. In Maintenance and Rebalancing, the system automatically
monitors and rebalances the portfolio to meet the optimal model.

2.2. Studies to understand RA customers

Because RAs are still emerging phenomena, it is of great value for
developers and service providers to understand the user demographics,
motivations, attitudes, and expectations. Studies revolving around users’
perception and adoption are mainstream objectives in Information Sys-
tems and Business Studies in Financial Market. These user studies
collected survey data from users to establish trust and adoption models of
RAs (e.g., uniﬁed theory of acceptance and use of technology) across
temporal (e.g., at COVID-19 crisis) and regional scopes (e.g., Germany
and Malaysia.) (Lourenço et al., 2020; Seiler & Fanenbruck, 2021; Todd
& Seay, 2020; Wu & Gao, 2021). Their models help to explain the
inﬂuential factors that lead users either to adopt the RA’s ﬁnal recom-
mendation or invest money by accepting this service. In those models,
some factors regarding customers’ experience of RAs were mentioned but
have not been deeply discussed by connecting them with RA system
design. Speciﬁcally, those factors, including the perceived usefulness and
performance expectations, were collected from survey data and were not
based on their real experience using RAs. To compensate for this, a
user-centered design study was conducted for risk-averse and low-budget
customers (Jung, Dorner, Weinhardt, & Pusmaz, 2018). Their work
contributes principles to RA design: ease of interaction, work efﬁciency,
information processing, cognitive load, and advisory transparency. These
principles, especially the last two regarding the user’s cognitive load and
transparency, are essential design takeaways for RA system design.
However, these design principles were limited by focusing on one spe-
ciﬁc customer group, and because the tested system was still in devel-
opment and iteration, the ﬁnal recommendation generated by the RA was
not discussed in this paper.

2.3. Anthropomorphic robot to delegate trust

Financial and investment decisions have traditionally and historically
been about trust. Trust can be deﬁned as “the willingness to take actions
of another entity based on the expectation that the other will perform a
particular action, regardless of the ability to monitor or control that other
part” (Bedu(cid:2)e & Fritzsche, 2022, p. 533). Thus, in this case, trust is about if

RAs can provide the expected beneﬁts that customers feel entitled to. To
maximize trust, human advisors have been involved in this process in the
conventional ﬁnancial service encounter, while they have been increas-
ingly augmented with AI-empowered technology over time. In tradi-
tional service encounters, the social interaction and relationship between
human advisors and customers signiﬁcantly affect customers’ perception
of advisor credibility and willingness to follow their advice (S€oderberg,
2013). With the AI replacement, the higher trust towards the Robo
advisor, the more likely consumers will adopt its recommendation
(Bruckes, Westmattelmann, Oldeweme, & Schewe, 2019). Thus, the so-
cial and anthropomorphic design could be alternative solutions to dele-
gate trust to compensate for the lack of human intervention in the RAs
(Bruckes et al., 2019). These studies usually articulate the perceived
social presence as anthropomorphism in RAs. For example, researchers
investigated verbal and visual cues (Adam et al., 2020), social cues in
conversational chat (Day, Lin, & Chen, 2018; Hildebrand & Bergner,
2021), visually human-like design (Deng & Chau, 2021), and varying
degrees of a human-like chatbot (Morana et al., 2020). These have
proven the possible inﬂuence of perceived social presence towards a
higher trust and adoption of RAs and the ﬁnal recommendation. How-
ever, the human-like design in business implementation and ﬁnancial
services is not a one-size-ﬁts-all solution because other factors dynami-
cally inﬂuence trust (Cheng et al., 2019). Also, trust delegated by
human-likeness robots is vulnerable to being disrupted by factors such as
the unsatisﬁed experience of using chatbots or a lack of understanding
(Dikmen & Burns, 2022). An empirical study shows that compared to
responsiveness, customization, and such traits, likability is not signiﬁcant
to customers’ intention to use chatbots in ﬁnancial services (Lee & Park,
2019).

2.4.

Information system in ﬁn-tech

RAs are automated decision-making systems, fundamentally disrup-
tive compared with other digital ﬁnancial services provided by banks or
other retail ﬁnancial institutions. Information systems are also applied in
human-advisory encounters, namely, a collaborative platform as a tool
for human advisors to advise customers. Prior research addressing IT-
support (web-based) systems in digital ﬁnancial services has detected
that transparency and information symmetry are important principles
(Kilic et al., 2015; Nueesch et al., 2014, 2016; Nussbaumer, Matter, Reto
(cid:3)a; Nussbaumer & Matter, 2011; Nussbaumer, Matter, & Schwabe, 2012;
Porta, et al., 2012). These studies also indicate that the system design of
digital advisory services is as important as the performance of the rec-
ommended ﬁnancial products. Compared with these digital systems
where human intervention still exists, an automated system is supposed
to promise more transparency regarding cost and the advisory process
(Tertilt & Scholz, 2018). However, these studies in ﬁnancial service
systems focused on web-based services rather than a fully-automated
system, i.e., RA.

According to Turilli and Floridi (2009), transparency is the process of
making information explicitly and openly available so that users can
exploit the disclosing information to make decisions. In information
systems and design science, the challenge of information transparency
can be addressed by Explainable AI (XAI). According to Eschenbach
(2021), transparency is necessary for trust and determining if AI is
trustworthy. Based on this philosophy, XAI can be a lens for researchers
to uncover users’ acceptance and trust in AI (Shin, 2021b). XAI has been
implemented and examined in varying settings [e.g., mental health (Wolf
& Ringland, 2020), recommendation system (Shin, 2021a)], as well as in
the RA context (Ben David, Resheff, & Tron, 2021). These studies
discovered the potential of using XAI for designing a RA that builds trust
and adoption, even though most of them were not contextualized in
ﬁnancial contexts.

knowledge to comprehend and interpret the explanation information

3. Methodology

3.3. Procedure

This user test includes an introduction, pre-survey, scenario setting,
task performance by using the RA application, and a post-test retro-
spective interview. The survey data, interview transcriptions, and screen
recordings of participants’ interaction with the interface were analyzed
accordingly.

3.1. Participants

To complement the participant groups of prior empirical studies that
focus on either speciﬁc user groups (Jung, Dorner, Weinhardt, & Pusmaz,
2018; Morana et al., 2020) or people who are actively in investment
activities (Hildebrand & Bergner, 2021), here we aimed to recruit par-
ticipants coming from more diverse demographic groups in terms of ages
and different levels of ﬁnancial understanding and investment experi-
ence. This choice also considers that the customers inclined to use RAs
are changing over time (Fulk, Grable, & Kruger, 2018; Gan et al., 2021;
Pradhan & Wang, 2020). This study does not aim to systematically
compare differences between participant groups, but some patterns
found between groups were observed and will be reported in the ﬁnding
section.

Two participants performed the pilot test, and 24 participants with
stable incomes by convenience sampling joined the formal user test. The
participants in this study have never used the kind of automated advisory
service investigated in this study before. All participants have stable in-
comes (MED. ¼ €300 to € 400 per month). The participants either had
experience with the ﬁnancial investment before or were interested in
trying this service to see the possibility of ﬁnancial investment in the
future. According to their self-reported ﬁnancial knowledge and experi-
ence, they were divided into two groups: non-expert customers (P2, P3,
P5, P6, P8, P10, P12, P13, P14, P16, P18, P19) and expert customers (P1,
P4, P7, P9, P11, P15, P17, P20, P22, P23, P24). The division into two
groups was not made to rigorously compare the groups but to identify
patterns within different groups by analyzing the screen recordings. The
professions of participants varied from researchers (including PhD stu-
dents), engineers, data analysts, administrators, physicians, librarians,
etc.

3.2. Design of the user test

Inspired by the retrospective think-aloud approach (van den Haak, De
Jong, & Jan Schellens, 2003), a combination of that method and a
semi-structured post-test interview method was used in this study. This is
a retrospective post-test interview. Participants were not asked to “think
aloud” during the test but talked about their performance and intentions

All the user tests were conducted in a quiet room in a lab environ-
ment. The complete user test contained three sessions: (1) pre-survey, to
collect participant’s demographic information, self-reported experience
and knowledge in ﬁnancial investment, and frequency of using tech-
nology and AI system; (2) performing three tasks by using the RA (video
is recording at the same time); and (3) post-test retrospective interview,
answering the questions in the interview, in parallel with describing and
explaining their behaviors by referring to the video playback.

Before the user test, each participant was informed of the aims and
process of this test; and was asked to sign the consent form regarding
information usage and privacy protection.

3.3.1. Tasks and scenarios

One task with scenarios was presented to the participants in a written
format before and while they were using the RA: (1) think about a sce-
nario in which you want to invest your savings for a long- or short-term
target; (2) try this RA as one of your investment options and explore this
automated service by yourself; (3) stop before the system asks for your
ﬁnal decision and keep in mind whether you want to use it or not in the
future and if you want to adopt the ﬁnal recommendation.

One researcher stayed in the lab as a moderator to offer help if the
participant had any problems. This moderator did not interact with the
participant during the task performance part of the test to simulate the
automated self-service process and encourage participants to ﬁnish the
service themselves. However, if the participant had problems that pre-
vented them from proceeding with the test, this researcher would offer
some assistance regarding operations to ensure that the test could pro-
ceed. This helps participants to complete the advisory process to have a
holistic understanding of the RA service, starting from the onboarding
experience until the ﬁnal recommended portfolio.

3.3.2. Post-test interview

The ﬁrst author constructed the ﬁrst draft of the interview protocol;
then, it was reviewed by the co-authors specializing in socio-robotics and
ﬁnancial services. Some texts were reworded, and a few questions were
added. The ﬁnal interview protocol contains a list of questions encom-
passing three parts. The ﬁrst part concerns participants’ prior investment
channels and how they differ from the RA they experienced in the test. In
this part, a semi-structured interview method was used. In the second
retrospective interview part, the questions focused on participants’
experience when using and interacting with the RA. Here, the researcher
re-played the video recording and asked participants for feedback
regarding each step and the overall process. For example, any problems
they encountered when using it, their expectations of the system, and

according to their input. In the fourth part, participants were asked
whether they thought a virtual client service or chatbot is more helpful
and supportive in this type of product compared to a human advisor.

3.4. Apparatus

3.4.1. Test hardware and software

In terms of the test hardware, researchers pre-installed the RA
application in a test smartphone (iPhone 6s Plus). And the software is a
selected RA by a retail bank.

The RA selected for this study was developed by a bank, one of
Sweden’s largest ﬁnancial groups. The selected RA is also one of the
earliest launched automated ﬁnancial services, among other similar
products. Using a RA service provided by a big bank can help to recruit
participants much easier due to its trustworthiness and authority
perceived by investors, and testing a mature application with accurate
technological support can help to reduce the consumers’ uncertainty
caused by inaccurate algorithms that might affect the users’ perception
and trust of the RAs.

The system design of most RAs on the mobile end is rather simple and
linear (see Fig. 1). The selected RA has the same usage ﬂow. Users can
navigate by pressing the “back” and “next” buttons, scrolling up and
down to view the interface, inputting numbers and sliding the bar to
change numbers when interacting with data visualizations. Additionally,
supplementary documents, such as fact sheets of ﬁnancial products, risk
disclosure, and purchase agreement, can be reviewed as attachments by
pressing the icon, which directs users to a PDF document.

3.4.2. Documentation

Each interview audio was recorded on an iPad for future transcription
and analysis. An iOS video recording software was used to record the
participants’ interaction with the interface during the user test. Due to
ethical considerations, participants were asked to use an unidentiﬁed test
account to log in to the RA system, but the ﬁnal recommended portfolio
was based on their inputting data. Their information would not be
identiﬁed by the bank, and researchers erased their inputs manually after
each test.

3.5. Data collection and analysis

This study collected three types of data: pre-survey data, a video
recording of the interaction with the RA (AVG. ¼ 19.8 min), an audio
recording of post-interview (AVG. ¼ 30 min), and a retrospective
description is included in the post-interview. The interview recordings
were transcribed into text and were imported into the software NVivo for
analysis. The coding is analyzed inductively by ﬁnding themes with
similar meanings. Three authors coded four interviews in parallel to
validate the coding items and make the coding consistent within our
research group. After the discussion, the initial coding metrics (an excel
sheet with codes, sub-codes, and their deﬁnition) were conﬁrmed, and

the rest of the interviews were coded by one of the authors. This author
added codes inductively if new themes emerged in the following 20
interview transcriptions. The three authors synthesized and discussed
these codes and annotations based on transcriptions.

In parallel with the interview transcription analysis, the video re-
cordings were used as supportive data material when participants
retrospectively discussed their behaviors and intentions when interacting
with the RA. Also, each step of participants’ interaction data with the RA
system was transcribed into an Excel spreadsheet. These data included
each participant’s buttons, scrolling pages, documents being reviewed,
and information ﬁlling in. For example, they checked “detailed infor-
mation” of recommended funds/stocks, opened additional documents
such as “factsheet”, reviewed “detailed cost”, and their input number
indicating their investment willingness. This aims to determine if par-
ticipants trust the AI-empowered recommendation by comparing the
suggested amount of money and their actual input. This data can also
reveal potential differences in patterns between participants with
different levels of investment experience or ﬁnancial understanding,
namely, the expert and non-expert customers.

Because ten participants were not proﬁcient with the language used
in the RA, an automated translation (i.e., Google Translate) tool was used
to help them perform the tasks. The translation accuracy was also
checked by bilingual participants, proving to work well for conveying
textual information. Due to the time lag for auto-translation, the time
they spent was not accounted for in the data analysis.

The data from pilot tests (N ¼ 2) were excluded from the analysis.
Thus, the data of 24 participants (14 females, 10 males; median age ¼
31.5 years) were brought into the analysis phase.

4. Results and ﬁndings

This section shows that the participants’ perception of RA does not
match their expectations. Non-transparency and insufﬁcient information
about the system make it difﬁcult for participants to adopt the results
generated by RA. However, data visualization showed its promise to
convey comprehensible information to participants. The ﬁndings are
synthesized from interview transcriptions and video recording analysis.

4.1. The perceived social role of RAs lags behind the participants’
expectation

4.1.1. Participants do not think they get advice when using the RA

As mentioned before, RAs are developed and implemented by service
providers to partially replace human-resource costs. RAs also promise to
provide the same quality of human-advisory service to customers with
relatively low service and transaction fees. However, participants did not
recognize that they were provided professional advice such as ﬁnancial
planning or wealth management. On the contrary, they felt forced to
complete the purchase of certain ﬁnancial products from the bank. The
RA was perceived as a sales-centric service by the bank. P20: “… the bank
wants to make a product that is easy for selling and make it easy for
people to click the contract that they want me to sign.”; P18: “I was forced

4.1.2. Participants do not think they are interacting with a “robot.”

In the post-test interview, participants reported that they did not
think the RA had any “robotic” features, and participants did not even
perceive some signiﬁcant features. The expectations they got from the
name “Robo-” and the introductory information about the RA service
shown on the onboarding pages did not match their experience with the
system when using it. For example, some thought the service was a
simple matching system: “very little robotic (…) a simple recommenda-
tion system (P20)”; it “just tries to match my proﬁle with existing
products (P21)”. Participants thought it was not intelligent because of the
lack of conversational interactions like one expert with a customer: “I
could not talk to, wouldn’t say more to the robots (P21)”; “the questions
it asked me are very standardized, I don’t think it is my advisor. I would
like my advisor to show more professionalism than this (P17).”

Participants also thought the service was “not intelligent” because it
just stopped and did not give any information to help users recover from
errors when they input unexpected or contradictory answers to the
questions in the risk assessment phase. Three participants (P3, P11, P13)
selected contradictory options when answering risk-related questions
according to the analysis of video recordings. As per the regulations in
ﬁnancial services, customers must correctly answer these questions by
themselves before they are allowed to proceed to the next step. Thus, RA
systems cannot tell customers the correct one. However, in this test,
participants were stopped from using the system. When this “contradic-
tory” was detected, the service just “gave up” and asked participants to
call the bank’s customer service. This unsatisfactory experience made
participants feel that the system was not advanced. P13: “It (is) not the
way that humans talk about ﬁnance. (…) It is unintelligent because it
could have dynamically told me which answers are inconsistent.” P3: “I
don’t know where I am wrong, and I don’t know how to ﬁx it.” P11 went
back to the previous stage and did the risk assessment again to proceed
with this service and said, “In real life, I will quit the service, and I will
not call them.” This unpleasant experience also made participants feel
ashamed and caused dissatisfaction. P13 thought the system blamed him:
“It told me that I did something wrong”.

Moreover, the features regarding automated maintenance and reba-
lancing of investment were not perceived by participants, even though
this information was shown on the introduction webpage. It might be
that the participants did not pay attention to the after-sale service
because the test moderator asked them to terminate the test session
before they had to perform the real purchase actions. However, after the
researcher explained to participants that the system would perform
automated maintenance and rebalancing after the investment, most
participants said that they had not recognized this function of the RA
when using the system.

4.1.3. Participants do not think a social robot can solve their problems but
expect more human intervention

In the interview, participants were asked if they thought that a virtual
customer service robot or chatbot would be able to give more support
compared to what the RA did that they just used. Almost all answered no

because they had not had any good experience talking with virtual
customer services (P4, P6, P9; P26); based on their understanding of
current technology, they thought that chatbots could only answer certain
questions, and they preferred checking FAQ by themselves (P18, P24).
Most importantly, P5 and P17 thought that it did not matter if humans or
robots provided the service; what they cared about was that the service
was performed professionally and rigorously and could be perceived as
trustworthy.

Participants thought this automated service could be “pre-stage”
before they went for human counseling because they still needed guid-
ance and tutorial from a human advisor (P13). P16 thought a professional
human could help to mitigate “the risk of miscommunication”, which
might be caused by their misinterpreting the conﬁguration questions
from the system. Besides, P24 preferred to “have a combination between
some person you can talk to and this automatic service” because he
thought that talking to someone could make banks “know more about my
economy, my goals and how I should act when I need an investment or
loan”. P17 proposed that the bank should set a threshold for when a
human advisor must do the ﬁnal review: “For big investments that exceed
a certain amount of money, a human advisor must get involved” because
“investment can be a big decision, I cannot give my money to something
based on a few clicks.” But P17 also said she might try this service by
starting with a small amount of money. Thus, human involvement is
expected by participants for different reasons, but mainly for calibration
of the possible “miscommunication” and more personalized advisory
suggestions.

4.2. Non-transparency is the main concern for decision making

Non-transparency indicates the information required by participants
that they do not think they get when using the system. This includes
undisclosed information by the service and disclosed information but
unperceived by the participant when using the service. Non-transparency
mainly exists in two stages: risk assessment and ﬁnal recommendation.

4.2.1. Non-transparency problems in conﬁguration questions

In the conﬁguration phase, RAs are supposed to “understand” cus-
tomers regarding their personal preferences, ﬁnancial situation, and risk
appetites based on surveys and answers ﬁlled by participants. This phase
is fundamental because it not only gets necessary inputs from customers
for the system to make complex computing; it also helps customers un-
derstand which factors construct the ﬁnal decision (i.e., investment
portfolio) made by this expert system. However, as the reasoning process
is not adequately revealed, participants know neither the differences
between each option, in particular, “high”, “minor”, and “intermediate”,
nor where these answers will lead them to. This is not a survey that
recommends any TV program but will lead to a high-stake decision on
their real money.

4.2.1.1. Participants want to know the logic behind the proﬁling ques-
tions. The reasoning behind conﬁguration questions and proﬁling stra-
tegies is unknown to participants. In the conﬁguration phase, customers
are required to provide information, so they will be categorized into
different groups based on the risk assessment and other customized in-
formation, namely, overall savings, the income left after their compul-
sory monthly expenditure,
loan, mortgage, etc. The standardized
questions for categorizing the customers into risk-appetite groups are
usually multiple-choice questions. Human advisors will help interpret
and explain these questions and options and check for accuracy by
regularly examining if customers understand them. In this user test,
participants were much more aware and cautious of the logic behind
these questions because they understood that these questions were to
proﬁle and match them with the appropriate ﬁnancial recommendation.
Thus, many participants were concerned about the issue of non-
transparency because they asked why the RA asked these questions but

4.2.2. Transparency problems in the ﬁnal recommended portfolio

After conﬁguration, the algorithm will automatically match the cus-
tomer’s proﬁle to a certain group and show them the ﬁnal recommen-
dation, which is usually a portfolio of ETFs (exchange-traded funds) that
is a basket of investments such as assets, stocks, bonds, etc. This is a
passive investment and is not fully transparent because the manager’s
trading strategies are normally unpublished. However, in the test, most
RA participants were unaware of the portfolio and how ETFs work in the
ﬁnancial market. Based on the information provided by RA, participants’
confusion is two folds: lack of information about the mechanism in the
system that creates the suggested portfolio; and the lack of information
that they think is necessary for their decision-making.

4.2.2.1. Lack of transparency of the recommendation system mechanism. -
After reviewing the recommended portfolio, most participants asked for
the result’s source and principles. This non-transparency revolves around
the AI-empowered algorithm and its mechanism for advising customers
on the optimal and personalized portfolio. P9: “I don’t know how and
what the criteria are for picking up the stocks for me.” P10: “I want to
know more speciﬁcally where these numbers suggest my monthly deposit
comes from.” This lack of transparency negatively affects participants'
trust as the service claims that the recommendation is “tailored” for them

and “adopted” to their needs; however, participants did not perceive
those features. P18: “I do not understand what kind of models or
mechanism the recommendation is based on.”

The participants were aware that they were categorized into different
risk groups, and they would like to know the ﬁnal recommendation in
other groups, even though the participants did not explicitly explain the
reasons for this requirement. P20: “why cannot I review all (plans)
choices at the end of the process? And that is also a factor where I would
lose trust in this service.” P17: “I know they might have seven groups in
total. I was classiﬁed into ﬁve; I want to see what the products in other
groups are.” P5 makes an analogy with personality tests: “This could be,
okay, our (banks’) options are this and this, just like the personality test,
you know. Then you can see other people (’s distribution) as well.” This
ﬁnding is in line with previous work regarding AI’s use in the medical
domain (Aoki, 2021); humans show more trust in AI’s decisions if they
feel that they are still involved in the decision-making by communicating
with the automated system. This type of “communication” is lacking in
RA service.

4.2.2.2. More transparent managerial strategies are needed regarding the
components of the recommended portfolio. Participants tended to show
more consciousness as they requested more detailed information about
the companies of bonds and securities, which were about the investment
targets in the portfolios. P13: “So 22% of the investment will go into the
IT sector, and 13% here will go into the health (industry), but it doesn’t
show me what’s the company?” Those participants who selected “sus-
tainable preference” were more ethically concerned investors, and they
thought it was important for their decision-making to know the criteria
for screening sustainable investments and what companies the bank
would invest in. P12: “I’m surprised it’s (sustainable) going; the majority
is going to IT. I didn’t expect that. Like, I thought it would go more to-
wards energy and sustainable energies.” P21: “Because if I’m an ethical
investor, I want that information to be transparent. If I know what they
are, I can track the companies and see what they are doing.”

Insufﬁcient information is the second issue that hinders participants’
adoption. Generally, non-expert customers with limited investment
experience or ﬁnancial understanding need expert systems like RAs to
make ﬁnancial decisions. In the interview, when asked, “would you
adopt the recommendation or use RAs in the future” most participants
were uncertain and could not make decisions based on their experience
with RAs. Only four gave certain answers like “yes” (P1 & P14) and “no”

want to use and try it several times and then make decisions”, expert
participants were more conﬁdent regarding their ﬁrst trial and believed
that the result would be the same if they tried the whole procedure again
(e.g., P4, P9, P17, P20, P24). At the same time, non-expert participants

that is transparent but also explanative and understandable for them.

4.3.1. Non-expert participants require more understandable information to
support their adoption decision

Participants said they needed more information to understand the
ﬁnal recommendation and to support their decision-making. The infor-
mation is two-fold: information disclosure regarding the RA service; and
ﬁnancial understanding, which is basic domain knowledge to support
interpretations and comprehensions of those disclosures. Differences
exist between expert and non-expert participants. The latter felt over-
whelmed when so much information appeared at the last stage. Even
though some can ﬁnd these documents, it was still hard for them to
understand the content, including ﬁnancial terms and numbers, not to
mention how to utilize this information to support their decision-making.
Generally, non-expert participants expect to learn basic ﬁnancial market
and investment knowledge through words they can easily comprehend.
P10: “I open the fact sheet and realize; I don’t understand the terms.”
P12: “I expected it could tell me what the stock market is like, such as the
current index of the whole stock market. I hope that someone can guide
me with more basic information.” P6: “They should have a good editor to
create understandable texts. Or they have links to some knowledge where
I could learn more.” In short, extra guidance in the system is expected to
reach three aims: (1) to help customers navigate the information they
need; (2) to guide them to understand this information, and (3) to tell
them which are the most important criteria for evaluating and predicting
the performance, of a fund for example.

4.3.2. Participants expect RAs to have a holistic understanding of their
personal information

Participants were skeptical that the RA could provide a holistic and
personalized portfolio based on the limited information currently
collected from them through the system. They also expected to spend a
long time using this service rather than getting a plan within a few mi-
nutes. P17: “Before using (the RA), I thought it would ask me more
questions that allow me to see the value of this service. These questions
are so standardized I don’t think they can give me good suggestions on an
individual level. I doubt it.” P7: “It is a short (the time), honestly. It can be
more patient with me. It’s a big decision. It’s not something I would like
to have done in 3 min”

Thus, some participants would like that the system could understand
more about users by asking more personalized questions such as

everyday ﬁnance and any long- or short-term plans in an individual’s
real-life scenarios, for instance, pensions (P21), buying a car or an
apartment (P8), or other plans in the future. P21 thought the RA skipped
important questions about customers’ horizons and intentions for saving
and investing. This also makes them feel that the advisor is not person-
alized as they see from the introduction page, and this unmet expectation
negatively affects their experience.

4.3.3. Data visualization is perceived as an effective tool to convey
comprehensible information

In the interview, most participants gave positive feedback in terms of
the interactive data visualization in the RA. Data visualization in-
struments show great potential in three aspects: visualizing ﬁnancial
understanding (especially risk assessment), interpreting the performance
of the recommended portfolio, and envisioning growth and loss.

In the selected RA, two types of data visualizations are utilized. One
visualizes risk assessment by showing concrete numbers in discrimina-
tive visual cues (i.e., two colors) (Fig. 2). The other visualization presents
more information and interactive parameters by which customers can
envision the growth and loss of the money they plan to invest in one-time
or monthly deposits (Fig. 2).

Compared with the vague terminology in the option such as “low”,
“middle”, and “high” in risk assessment mentioned before in this paper,
the data visualization (Fig. 2 Left panel) is claimed to be “more concrete
(P12)” and “much more tangible (P5)” in helping participants understand
risk, even though some design problems exist (e.g., most participants did
not realize the bottom round button is interactable). All participants said
that the interactive line diagram (Fig. 2 Right panel) provided sufﬁcient
information for them to understand the performance of their portfolio
and how the possible growth and loss of their money could change over
time. First, the interactive line diagram is self-explanatory and compre-
hensible for participants without much knowledge about this type of
service. P10: “it can provide me the estimation of the lowest and highest
(…) It can give you a general conception, like the rate, percentage. Then I
know the highest money loss I can bear, concretely and practically.” P21:
“This (Fig. 2 Right panel) is more comprehensible regarding the time
horizon and related risk”. From the video analysis, every participant
interacted with the tool by inputting different variables in Fig. 2. P4
thought the input in time horizon helped to associate risk with the con-
crete amount of money, making the risk more understandable.

4.4. An untransparent system and incomprehensible information: where
the distrust comes from

Participants showed distrust and suspicion of the result if the RA
system was not transparent or incomprehensible to them. According to
the interviews, participants’ decision to adopt the RA was affected by the
system and other factors, such as unsatisﬁed fees and initial intention of
using RA. However, for both expert and non-expert participants, trans-
parency and sufﬁcient comprehensible information are crucial for their
willingness to use RA or not. Besides, it is worth noting that most par-
ticipants distrust the system from their interviews and the screen
recording. After reviewing the ﬁnal recommended portfolio, participants
were asked to input their investment plan to ﬁnish the task performance.
In the video recordings, most participants did not follow the RA’s sug-
gested amount of money. Only four (P6, P7, P11, P18) inputs exactly
matched the amount of money the RA suggested. This can be interpreted
as participants not taking the RA’s suggestion as their best ﬁt, thus rep-
resenting distrust of the risk grouping and the mechanism behind the
recommended portfolio. Most participants showed that they trusted the
bank and the technology, being conﬁdent that there would be no fraud.
However, their experience of the whole system makes it difﬁcult to trust
the result (P3, P5, P6, P7, P12, P13, P21). According to the interview
data, this distrust negatively affects participants’ willingness to adopt the
advice generated by the RA.

5. Discussion

This holistic user-centered study of a functional retail bank RA aims to
understand how the AI-empowered system can impact customers’
perception and adoption. Methodologically, the approach of asking
customers to use a functional RA compensated for the previous empirical
studies by simply conducting surveys without considering customers’
experience of interacting with a RA in real-life situations. Theoretically,
this study extends the knowledge of RA perception and adoption research
by articulating the negative effects of the lack of transparency and
comprehensibility in a RA system. In particular, this paper claims that
non-expert customers request more comprehensible domain knowledge
to use and adopt RAs. Besides, the perception of RA lags behind both
customers’ expectations and the service providers’ ambitions. Overall,
the signiﬁcance and the role of RA’s system design have been levered up,
and some design takeaways, current limitations, and future work are also
discussed.

5.1. A transparent and comprehensible AI-empowered system is signiﬁcant
for RA perception and adoption

In the context of ﬁnancial investment, transparency and compre-
hensibility are important because the investment decision is relatively
high-stake, and investors are “vulnerable customers” (Beltramini, 2018;
Mogaji et al., 2021), which means their perception of RA can be nega-
tively affected by lack of transparency. They are not resilient or tolerant
of unmet expectations. Moreover, unlike other AI systems, investment is
not a “one-time” behavior, but a long-term and dynamic process. The
whole service provided by RA ranges from portfolio design and main-
tenance, which is difﬁcult for customers to understand. Customers are
highly dependent on written texts and visualized data without human
intervention when using RA systems. This also increases the threshold to
adopt RA. Thus, a good RA system should be transparent and
comprehensible.

By extending prior studies in the web-based Fin-tech (Kilic et al.,
2015; Nueesch et al., 2014, 2016; Nussbaumer, Matter, Reto (cid:3)a; Nuss-
baumer & Matter, 2011; Nussbaumer, Matter, & Schwabe, 2012; Porta,
et al., 2012), the result has levered up the role and importance of a
transparent and comprehensible system perceived by customers, which
works together with other multiple factors affecting customers’ percep-
tion and adoption of RAs. The mechanism behind consumers’ behavioral

intention is complex, especially when AI meets with ﬁnancial decisions.
This interdisciplinary paper bridges the previous fragmented studies in
different ﬁelds (Belanche, Casal(cid:2)o, & Flavi(cid:2)an, 2019; Ben David et al.,
2021; Ben-David & Sade, 2020; Cheng et al., 2019; Gan et al., 2021; Shin,
2021a; Wu & Gao, 2021), by compensating for the lack of customers’ real
use of a functional RA in previous studies.

5.1.1. Transparency issues running through the whole system

Transparency has long been an issue in ﬁnancial advisory services
(Kilic et al., 2015; Nueesch et al., 2014, 2016; Nussbaumer & Matter,
2011) and AI-empowered customer experience design (Ben David et al.,
2021; Shin, 2021a). This empirical study also revealed different trans-
parency issues embodied in AI-empowered advisory and ﬁnancial sys-
tems. Customers in ﬁnancial service encounters are more aware of issues
because the investment decision is high-stake compared with other
AI-empowered services. This study articulates these issues, and it in-
dicates that transparency should be a principle that runs through every
detail of a RA system ranging from the texts on the introductory page, the
reasoning behind survey questions and options, the mechanism of the
recommended portfolio, the performance and components of ﬁnancial
products, the service fees, to the service-related terms, such as termina-
tion. Customers using RA are more rational than those lay investors in
conventional ﬁnancial services with the popularization of AI-empowered
technology and its wide use. They would like to make more informed
decisions even though they trust the service providers and the technol-
ogy, namely the algorithm behind the system. Similar to the ﬁnding of
previous internet-bank research (Nussbaumer, Matter, & Schwabe,
2012), transparency in the AI era intensively requests the system not only
provide the necessary information, but also prioritize the importance of
different information and convey these to customers. This can help to
balance the information asymmetry between an AI-empowered expert
system and customers in ﬁnancial service encounters. Otherwise, the
non-transparency will hamper the relationship established on the trust-
worthiness between customers and service providers.

than expert customers. Also, non-expert customers with little knowledge
might feel embarrassed and anxious when conversing with a human

5.1.3. Potential of interactive data visualization

The interactive data visualization in the RA service has proven its
potential to help customers understand risk, envision the growth and loss
of their assets, and associate information with the time horizon. By using
different colors as discriminative visual cues to indicate different situa-
tions, this visualized design synthesizes parameters from different sour-
ces (e.g., AI-driven models, input from customers), then embodies the
complex mechanism into an interactive diagram that can easily ﬁt with
customers’ cognitive ability. Thus, this user study can treat interactive
data visualization as an explainable AI approach. Moreover, this design
shows the recommended deposit plan and still grants customers auton-
omy to input and adjust their money to be invested. This ﬁnding extends
previous research regarding interactive data visualization in making
judgments (Eberhard, 2021), understanding information (Perdana et al.,
2018), and ﬁnancial decision-making (Tang et al., 2014) by uncovering
how well it is used and its potential in automated ﬁnancial advisory
contexts.

5.2. The discrepancy between RAs’ social design and customers’ perception

It is a disruptive ﬁnding that customers’ perception of the RA service
is not in line with the intentions and ambitions of the service providers.
Findings in this study have extended the knowledge regarding RAs’
weaknesses (Jung, Dorner, Weinhardt, & Pusmaz, 2018; Jung et al.,
2019). Most importantly, these ﬁndings have questioned in a disruptive
way the assumption that RAs’ anthropomorphism can compensate for the
lack of human intervention in RAs (Adam et al., 2020; Deng & Chau,
2021; Hildebrand & Bergner, 2021). According to our user study, cus-
tomers need a human advisor to double-check their comprehension of
aspects of the service, primarily due to the possibility of “miscommuni-
cation” in the conﬁguration phase. Here, a more transparent and un-
derstandable information-sharing mechanism between customers and
the system regarding the interpretation of the questions and each option
connected to them is lacking. Without knowing why these questions are
asked and what their answer can lead them to, customers need and
expect a human advisor as a bridge to conﬁrm if customers understand
the question the same way as was intended by the developers of the
system; and if the system interprets participants input in exactly the way
that the participants meant. Different levels of human-likeness design
might affect consumers’ behavior and decision-making in certain ways;
however, based on the ﬁndings in this study, that is not what customers
need nor expect.

5.3. The dilemma between system design and business implementation:
practical takeaways

Here we propose takeaways for the practitioners and service pro-
viders of RA. In practice, the implementation of RAs is not simply user-
centered due to multiple stakeholders' involvement, because RA de-
velopers and designs are only part of the business strategies. In behav-
ioral ﬁnance studies, customers’ ﬁnancial decisions tend to be biased
(Chira, Adams, & Thornton, 2008; Costa, de Melo Carvalho, de Melo
Moreira, & do Prado, 2017). Some customers would only invest in those
with high returns and ignore other factors, and lay investors with limited
ﬁnancial literacy would not actively acquire any information but still
make investment decisions. If conventional ﬁnancial advisory service is
sales-centered, then RAs are supposed to be result-driven. It is designed
to encourage customers to start in a very small amount by showing them
the results quickly, so customers can decide based on the return rate or
service fee. Thus, it is understandable that existing RAs seems to be easy
to use and their “smooth” and “simple” interface makes customer rapidly
check the ﬁnal result because they do not want to let customers leave
halfway without seeing the ﬁnancial products, which are their core
selling points of the service.

According to the ﬁndings of this study, customers are more self-aware
and patient when interacting with AI-empowered systems in making
ﬁnancial decisions. They are willing to spend more time and get sufﬁcient
information from RA systems, but service providers underestimate this.
The system design is supposed to be more user-centered, and the decision
mechanism and reasoning process should be transparent and under-
standable for both expert and non-expert customers. Most importantly,
data visualization can be further explored to convey sufﬁcient and
explainable information as an effective instrument for the interface.

5.4. Limitations and future studies

This study did not employ standardized assessments to evaluate
participants’ ﬁnancial literacy. Instead, this information is incorporated
in the pre-test survey, and participants were asked to self-report ﬁnancial
knowledge on a 1 to 5 scale (scale 1 stands for very little knowledge; 5
stands for expertise knowledge). Possible biases exist in participants who
are overconﬁdent or underestimate their ﬁnancial knowledge. Further
work regarding the comparison between user groups with different
ﬁnancial literacy would be a very interesting avenue. Also, future work
could be extended from focusing on behavioral intention to including
long-term outcomes, for example, customers’ retention or after-adoption
behaviors.

6. Conclusion

This study conducts a user study regarding a fully functional AI-
empowered automated ﬁnancial advisory system and its provided ser-
vice. The ﬁndings show that the social aspects that are supposed to be
provided by the RA are not perceived as such by participants. The lack of
