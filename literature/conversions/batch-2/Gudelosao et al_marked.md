Abstract. Cooperatives play a vital role in promoting economic growth and 
financial inclusion, especially in communities with limited access to formal 
financial  institutions.  Despite  this  importance,  many  cooperative  members 
still exhibit low financial literacy, which may affect their financial decisions 
and the organization’s overall performance. This study utilized a quantitative 
descriptive-correlational research design with mediation analysis involving 
one  hundred  members  from  four  registered  multi-purpose  cooperatives. 
Data on members’ financial literacy were gathered through a questionnaire, 
while  financial  performance  indicators  were  obtained  from  cooperative 
records.  Results  revealed  that  financial  knowledge  significantly  increases 
financial  attitude  (β  =  0.525,  p  <  .001),  and  financial  attitude  significantly 
increases  financial  behavior  (β  =  0.592,  p  <  .001).  However,  financial 
knowledge has no significant direct effect on financial behavior (β = 0.024, p 
=  .797),  demonstrating  that  financial  attitude  fully  mediates  this  pathway 
(indirect effect β = 0.311, p < .001). Furthermore, regression analysis showed 
that  members'  financial  literacy  had  no  significant  predictive  effect  on 
cooperative  financial  performance  (β  =  0.048,  p  =  .632),  with  the  model 
explaining only 0.2% of the variance (R² = 0.002). These findings indicate that 
improving  members’  financial  literacy  alone  is  insufficient  to  enhance 
cooperative  outcomes;  organizational 
factors  such  as  governance, 
management, and internal controls likely exert a more substantial impact.

Keywords: Cooperatives; Financial literacy; Financial performance; Multi-purpose 
cooperatives

C

ooperatives  are  more  than  just  businesses;  they  are  vital  engines  of  economic  development  and  social 
cohesion, particularly in regions where formal financial services are scarce. In Bohol, a landscape shaped 
by agriculture, tourism, and micro-enterprises, cooperatives serve as a financial lifeline. For instance, the

organizations' structural strength is inherently tied to the financial competence of their constituent members.

personal asset but a form of collective human capital that drives the organization’s operational success. Financial 
literacy serves as the essential structural link between individual behavior and institutional health (Perez & Lopez, 
2020). Viewed through the lens of the Resource-Based View (RBV) theory, prominent literacy gaps represent a 
clear failure to leverage internal human resources—the members themselves—which are necessary for building a 
competitive, sustainable organization.

However, a significant gap remains between this ideal and global reality. The Economic and Social Survey of Asia 
and the Pacific (2022) highlights an ongoing literacy crisis, noting that only about one-third of adults in the region 
meet basic financial literacy standards. Furthermore, the International Cooperative Research Group (2022) points 
to misinformation and a lack of awareness as primary barriers to cooperative growth. While leaders must naturally 
assess growth and operational health (Jamaluddin et al., 2023), the downstream influence of the financially literate 
member on these metrics is often overlooked. Financially savvy members are better equipped to avoid debt traps 
and contribute to the cooperative’s capital (Ermawati et al., 2019), yet the direct link between this knowledge base 
and institutional performance remains under-researched. For example, Mwatondo and Wekesa (2020) found that 
literacy levels alone failed to fully explain growth dynamics in certain financial sectors, and Pratiwi et al. (2020) 
called  for  a  deeper  investigation  into  how  member-level  knowledge  translates  into  institutional  performance. 
Empirical research has yet to fully map how individual member competencies translate into institutional success 
within the local context of Bohol.

Methodology  
Research Design 
This study employed a quantitative research design to examine the relationship between cooperative members' 
financial literacy and the financial performance of large cooperatives. The researchers applied specific measures 
to  assess  both  the  members'  financial  literacy  levels  and  the  cooperatives'  financial  performance.  Data  were 
obtained  through  the  distribution  of  structured  questionnaires  and  the  collection  of  financial  statements.  The 
gathered data were then systematically described and interpreted to derive substantive conclusions.

Research Environment 
The  research  was  conducted  in  Tagbilaran  City,  Bohol,  Philippines,  focusing  on  selected  multi-purpose 
cooperatives.  These  cooperatives  play  an  essential  role  in  supporting  local  livelihoods  and  contributing  to  the 
province's economic development. Tagbilaran City is home to a well-established network of cooperatives across 
various sectors, making it an ideal setting for this study.  According to the CDA, Tagbilaran City has 71 registered 
cooperatives, 43 of which are multi-purpose cooperatives located in the City of Tagbilaran, Bohol. As the capital 
of the province, Tagbilaran serves as the economic hub of Bohol, playing a significant role in local trade, business, 
and community development.

Research Instrument 
The study utilized a structured questionnaire and financial ratio analysis as the primary research instruments to 
assess the financial literacy of cooperative members. The questionnaire consisted of 30 items equally distributed 
among three foundational constructs: financial knowledge, financial attitude, and financial behavior (10 items per 
dimension).  The  instrument  was  adapted  from  validated  global  instruments  and  structured  in  line  with  the 
OECD/INFE  (2023)  guidelines  to  ensure  cross-contextual  relevance  and  standardized  measurement.  Financial 
knowledge  included  both  objective  components  (measured  via  multiple-choice  and  mathematical  problem-
solving items scored as correct/incorrect) and subjective components. Subjective knowledge, financial attitude, 
and financial behavior were assessed using a uniform four-point Likert scale ranging from strongly disagree to 
strongly  agree.  To  establish  the  instrument’s  validity  and  reliability,  a  pilot  test  was  conducted  prior  to  data 
collection. The data yielded acceptable Cronbach’s Alpha reliability coefficients above the standard 0.70 threshold 
for all subscales, confirming strong internal consistency.

The study also involved document analysis, specifically focusing on the cooperative’s financial statements for the 
year 2024. These documents, along with related records and reports, were reviewed to extract relevant data. To 
evaluate the extent of financial performance, the researchers utilized financial ratios based on the STEPS (Stability, 
Turnover, Efficiency, Profitability, and Structure of Assets) method established by the Cooperative Development 
Authority (2021). These ratios provided the necessary financial data and allowed for the calculation of the selected 
indicators.  The  results  were  then  used  to  determine  the  adjectival  rating  of  the  cooperative’s  financial 
performance.

Research Participants 
The  study  involved  members  of  multi-purpose  cooperatives  in  Tagbilaran  City,  selected  through  purposive 
sampling.  This  non-probability  method  was  used  to  target  cooperatives  that  met  specific  study  requirements, 
particularly those with established systems, larger memberships, and complex financial operations. Cooperatives 
were chosen based on three criteria: (1) being classified as large, (2) having a headquarters or branch in Tagbilaran 
City, and (3) operating as a multi-purpose cooperative. From a PCDO-provided list of qualified cooperatives, five 
were identified, and four agreed to participate.

After selecting the cooperatives, individual respondents were chosen using eligibility criteria to ensure relevance 
and accessibility. Respondents had to be active, verified members of a registered multi-purpose cooperative in 
Tagbilaran City. They also needed to be of legal age and have sufficient membership duration to be familiar with 
the cooperative’s financial programs and operations. Purposive selection ensured that participants could provide 
informed, reliable data aligned with the study’s objectives.

The sample size was justified based on standard rules for multiple linear regression and Ordinary Least Squares 
(OLS)  path  modeling.  This  framework  dictates  that  for  a  model  evaluating  up  to  three  concurrent  predictor 
pathways, a minimum sample size of 74 is required to achieve adequate statistical power. The selection of 100 
respondents exceeds this baseline, ensuring sufficient power to detect medium effect sizes. The sample was evenly 
divided among the four cooperatives, with 25 respondents each.

Data Analysis 
Data  were  processed  and  analyzed  using  IBM  SPSS  Statistics  and  PROCESS  Model.  Cooperative  financial 
performance was evaluated through document analysis of the 2024 audited financial statements to calculate ratios 
based on the STEPS method. To reconcile data across two distinct units of analysis, the STEPS performance metrics 
of the four cooperatives were disaggregated and assigned directly to the profile rows of their respective twenty-
five  individual  member  respondents.  To  evaluate  the  underlying  mechanisms  of  member  competencies,  an 
Ordinary Least Squares (OLS) regression path analysis was performed to test the mediation model, specifically 
examining  whether  financial  attitude  mediates  the  relationship  between  financial  knowledge  and  financial 
behavior.  This  mediation  analysis  utilized  non-parametric  bootstrapping  with  5,000  resamples.  Finally,  simple 
linear  regression  was  applied  to  the  disaggregated  data  matrix  to  determine  whether  members'  individual 
financial literacy indices significantly predict the overall organizational financial performance of their respective 
cooperatives.

Ethical Consideration 
The study was conducted in strict accordance with ethical standards for research involving human participants. 
The Holy Name University Ethical Review Board granted formal ethical approval. The informed consent process 
was initiated prior to any data collection. During the recruitment phase, each potential participant was provided 
with a written consent form that detailed the study’s objectives, the voluntary nature of their participation, and 
their  right  to  withdraw  at  any  time  without  penalty.  Consent  was  obtained  in  a  one-on-one  setting  to  ensure 
participants could ask questions and fully understand the scope of the research. Regarding data obtained from 
cooperative records, the researchers secured formal authorization from the board of directors of each participating 
cooperative. This included specific permission to access and utilize institutional financial performance data for 
research purposes only. To protect confidentiality, all participants were anonymized using alphanumeric coding. 
All digital data, including survey responses and financial records, was stored in encrypted, password-protected 
files accessible only to the research team.

Financial Literacy of Cooperative Members 
The results show that cooperative members in Tagbilaran City have a generally high level of financial literacy, 
with  a  combined  Financial  Literacy  Index  (FLI)  mean  score  of  3.50,  interpreted  as  High.  These  findings  are 
consistent  with  Perez  &  Lopez  (2020),  who  found  that  school-based  cooperative  members  demonstrated  good 
financial understanding yet still engaged in problematic borrowing and lacked strong financial discipline. Quibra 
(2024)  also  observed  that  members  of  Sustainable  Livelihood  Program  Associations  had  moderate  financial 
knowledge  and  attitude  but  continued  to  show  inconsistent  financial  behaviors,  which  limited  their  overall 
financial well-being. Together, these studies highlight the same pattern seen in our respondents: individuals may 
understand  sound  financial  concepts,  but  consistently  translating  that  knowledge  into  responsible  financial 
behavior remains a challenge.

Relationship Between Financial Literacy Constructs 
The  results  reveal  a  strong  and  statistically  significant  relationship  between  financial  knowledge  and  financial 
attitude (β = 0.525, p < .001), and between financial attitude and financial behavior (β = 0.592, p < .001). These beta 
values  indicate  strong  positive  effects,  meaning  increases  in  knowledge  substantially  improve  attitude,  and 
increases  in  attitude  significantly  enhance  behavior.  In  contrast,  the  direct  effect  of  financial  knowledge  on 
financial behavior was negligible and not statistically significant (β = 0.024, p = .797), showing that knowledge 
alone  does  not  directly  predict  members’  financial  actions.  The  model  further  shows  that  financial  knowledge 
accounts  for  27.6%  of  the  variance  in  financial  attitude,  while  the  combined  effects  of  knowledge  and  attitude 
explain 36.7% of the variance in financial behavior, which are considerable proportions in behavioral research. 
These findings highlight that attitude acts as the key mechanism through which knowledge influences behavior. 
This shows a direct significant relationship between financial knowledge and financial attitude, which supports 
the findings of Shih et al. (2022). Their study explains that individuals with higher financial knowledge are more 
likely  to  develop  positive  financial  attitudes.  The  study  also  found  a  direct  significant  relationship  between

Relationship Between Financial Literacy and Financial Performance 
The  regression  analysis  reveals  that  financial  literacy  does  not  significantly  predict  cooperative  financial 
performance, as shown by a non-significant beta coefficient (β = 0.048, p = .632). The extremely low coefficient 
indicates  that  a  one-unit  increase  in  financial  literacy  is  associated  with  only  a  0.048-unit  increase  in  financial 
performance, which is statistically trivial. The model’s R² = 0.002 means that financial literacy explains only 0.2% 
of the variance in financial performance, far below the threshold of practical significance. This implies that in these 
cooperatives,  financial  performance  is  determined  by  other  organizational  factors  such  as  governance  quality, 
managerial  decisions,  internal  controls,  and  capital  management,  rather  than  the  financial  literacy  levels  of 
individual  members.  Thus,  improving  members’  financial  literacy  alone  is  not  enough  to  enhance  the 
cooperatives’ financial outcomes.

Conclusion  
This finding reveals that financial attitude serves as a full mediator between financial knowledge and financial 
behavior, addressing the crucial underlying mechanism that translates what an individual knows into how they 
actually act. While it is commonly assumed that simply teaching people financial facts will automatically improve 
their habits, our analysis demonstrates that financial knowledge lacks a direct pipeline to behavioral outcomes; 
instead,  raw  information  remains  functionally  dormant  unless  it  successfully  alters  an  individual’s  internal 
mindset and psychological disposition toward managing money. Consequently, we can conclude that knowledge 
alone is a necessary but insufficient condition for behavioral change, as a person may fully comprehend the basics 
of finance but it will fail to yield better financial choices if their attitude remains unchanged.

Moreover,  members'  financial  literacy  does  not  influence  the  financial  performance  of  the  cooperatives.  While 
members remain essential to cooperative operations, since they serve as the primary source of capital through 
share  contributions  and  patronage,  generate  revenue  through  their  patronage,  and  sustain  activities  through 
continuous transactions, their level of financial literacy alone is not enough to have an effect on the cooperative's 
performance. Instead, the cooperative's performance may be shaped more strongly by organizational factors such 
as governance, operations, and management. As a result, improving the financial literacy of members on its own 
does not lead to measurable enhancements of performance instead, cooperative performance depends more on 
how effectively the organization is managed and how actively members participate in its systems and operations.

As a recommendation, cooperative leaders and educators should move away from relying on information-heavy 
lectures about financial management and instead design training programs that connect with people on a personal 
level to shape their mindsets, confidence, and long-term financial attitudes. At the same time, because members’ 
financial literacy alone does not automatically translate into financial performance, leaders must also invest in 
other aspects like improving governance and management, strengthening operations, and building user-friendly 
systems that make it easy and rewarding for members to participate regularly. Future researchers may conduct 
experiments to test whether attitude-focused training actually changes behavior better than traditional methods. 
Additionally, they may also look into specific cooperative aspects such as governance, operational workflows, 
and management systems as predictors of financial performance.

C ooperatives are more than just businesses; they are vital engines of economic development and social
cohesion, particularly in regions where formal financial services are scarce. In Bohol, a landscape shaped
by agriculture, tourism, and micro-enterprises, cooperatives serve as a financial lifeline. For instance, the
Bohol Community Multi-Purpose Cooperative reports that 40% of its members are farmers and fisherfolk, a
demographic that requires specialized savings and lending solutions. As member-owned entities, these
organizations' structural strength is inherently tied to the financial competence of their constituent members.
This study is grounded in Human Capital Theory, which posits that investments in knowledge and skills directly
enhance economic productivity. In the context of a cooperative, a member’s financial literacy is not merely a
This work is licensed under a Creative Commons Attribution-Noncommercial 4.0 International License (CC BY-NC 4.0).

personal asset but a form of collective human capital that drives the organization’s operational success. Financial
literacy serves as the essential structural link between individual behavior and institutional health (Perez & Lopez,
2020). Viewed through the lens of the Resource-Based View (RBV) theory, prominent literacy gaps represent a
clear failure to leverage internal human resources—the members themselves—which are necessary for building a
competitive, sustainable organization.
However, a significant gap remains between this ideal and global reality. The Economic and Social Survey of Asia
and the Pacific (2022) highlights an ongoing literacy crisis, noting that only about one-third of adults in the region
meet basic financial literacy standards. Furthermore, the International Cooperative Research Group (2022) points
to misinformation and a lack of awareness as primary barriers to cooperative growth. While leaders must naturally
assess growth and operational health (Jamaluddin et al., 2023), the downstream influence of the financially literate
member on these metrics is often overlooked. Financially savvy members are better equipped to avoid debt traps
and contribute to the cooperative’s capital (Ermawati et al., 2019), yet the direct link between this knowledge base
and institutional performance remains under-researched. For example, Mwatondo and Wekesa (2020) found that
literacy levels alone failed to fully explain growth dynamics in certain financial sectors, and Pratiwi et al. (2020)
called for a deeper investigation into how member-level knowledge translates into institutional performance.
Empirical research has yet to fully map how individual member competencies translate into institutional success
within the local context of Bohol.
Methodology
Research Design
This study employed a quantitative research design to examine the relationship between cooperative members'
financial literacy and the financial performance of large cooperatives. The researchers applied specific measures
to assess both the members' financial literacy levels and the cooperatives' financial performance. Data were
obtained through the distribution of structured questionnaires and the collection of financial statements. The
gathered data were then systematically described and interpreted to derive substantive conclusions.
Research Environment
The research was conducted in Tagbilaran City, Bohol, Philippines, focusing on selected multi-purpose
cooperatives. These cooperatives play an essential role in supporting local livelihoods and contributing to the
province's economic development. Tagbilaran City is home to a well-established network of cooperatives across
various sectors, making it an ideal setting for this study. According to the CDA, Tagbilaran City has 71 registered
cooperatives, 43 of which are multi-purpose cooperatives located in the City of Tagbilaran, Bohol. As the capital
of the province, Tagbilaran serves as the economic hub of Bohol, playing a significant role in local trade, business,
and community development.
Research Instrument
The study utilized a structured questionnaire and financial ratio analysis as the primary research instruments to
assess the financial literacy of cooperative members. The questionnaire consisted of 30 items equally distributed
among three foundational constructs: financial knowledge, financial attitude, and financial behavior (10 items per
dimension). The instrument was adapted from validated global instruments and structured in line with the
OECD/INFE (2023) guidelines to ensure cross-contextual relevance and standardized measurement. Financial
knowledge included both objective components (measured via multiple-choice and mathematical problem-
solving items scored as correct/incorrect) and subjective components. Subjective knowledge, financial attitude,
and financial behavior were assessed using a uniform four-point Likert scale ranging from strongly disagree to
strongly agree. To establish the instrument’s validity and reliability, a pilot test was conducted prior to data
collection. The data yielded acceptable Cronbach’s Alpha reliability coefficients above the standard 0.70 threshold
for all subscales, confirming strong internal consistency.
The study also involved document analysis, specifically focusing on the cooperative’s financial statements for the
year 2024. These documents, along with related records and reports, were reviewed to extract relevant data. To
evaluate the extent of financial performance, the researchers utilized financial ratios based on the STEPS (Stability,
Turnover, Efficiency, Profitability, and Structure of Assets) method established by the Cooperative Development
Authority (2021). These ratios provided the necessary financial data and allowed for the calculation of the selected
indicators. The results were then used to determine the adjectival rating of the cooperative’s financial
performance.
232

Research Participants
The study involved members of multi-purpose cooperatives in Tagbilaran City, selected through purposive
sampling. This non-probability method was used to target cooperatives that met specific study requirements,
particularly those with established systems, larger memberships, and complex financial operations. Cooperatives
were chosen based on three criteria: (1) being classified as large, (2) having a headquarters or branch in Tagbilaran
City, and (3) operating as a multi-purpose cooperative. From a PCDO-provided list of qualified cooperatives, five
were identified, and four agreed to participate.
After selecting the cooperatives, individual respondents were chosen using eligibility criteria to ensure relevance
and accessibility. Respondents had to be active, verified members of a registered multi-purpose cooperative in
Tagbilaran City. They also needed to be of legal age and have sufficient membership duration to be familiar with
the cooperative’s financial programs and operations. Purposive selection ensured that participants could provide
informed, reliable data aligned with the study’s objectives.
The sample size was justified based on standard rules for multiple linear regression and Ordinary Least Squares
(OLS) path modeling. This framework dictates that for a model evaluating up to three concurrent predictor
pathways, a minimum sample size of 74 is required to achieve adequate statistical power. The selection of 100
respondents exceeds this baseline, ensuring sufficient power to detect medium effect sizes. The sample was evenly
divided among the four cooperatives, with 25 respondents each.
Data Analysis
Data were processed and analyzed using IBM SPSS Statistics and PROCESS Model. Cooperative financial
performance was evaluated through document analysis of the 2024 audited financial statements to calculate ratios
based on the STEPS method. To reconcile data across two distinct units of analysis, the STEPS performance metrics
of the four cooperatives were disaggregated and assigned directly to the profile rows of their respective twenty-
five individual member respondents. To evaluate the underlying mechanisms of member competencies, an
Ordinary Least Squares (OLS) regression path analysis was performed to test the mediation model, specifically
examining whether financial attitude mediates the relationship between financial knowledge and financial
behavior. This mediation analysis utilized non-parametric bootstrapping with 5,000 resamples. Finally, simple
linear regression was applied to the disaggregated data matrix to determine whether members' individual
financial literacy indices significantly predict the overall organizational financial performance of their respective
cooperatives.
Ethical Consideration
The study was conducted in strict accordance with ethical standards for research involving human participants.
The Holy Name University Ethical Review Board granted formal ethical approval. The informed consent process
was initiated prior to any data collection. During the recruitment phase, each potential participant was provided
with a written consent form that detailed the study’s objectives, the voluntary nature of their participation, and
their right to withdraw at any time without penalty. Consent was obtained in a one-on-one setting to ensure
participants could ask questions and fully understand the scope of the research. Regarding data obtained from
cooperative records, the researchers secured formal authorization from the board of directors of each participating
cooperative. This included specific permission to access and utilize institutional financial performance data for
research purposes only. To protect confidentiality, all participants were anonymized using alphanumeric coding.
All digital data, including survey responses and financial records, was stored in encrypted, password-protected
files accessible only to the research team.

Financial Literacy of Cooperative Members
The results show that cooperative members in Tagbilaran City have a generally high level of financial literacy,

knowledge and attitude but continued to show inconsistent financial behaviors, which limited their overall
financial well-being. Together, these studies highlight the same pattern seen in our respondents: individuals may
understand sound financial concepts, but consistently translating that knowledge into responsible financial
behavior remains a challenge.

Relationship Between Financial Literacy Constructs
The results reveal a strong and statistically significant relationship between financial knowledge and financial
attitude (β = 0.525, p < .001), and between financial attitude and financial behavior (β = 0.592, p < .001). These beta
values indicate strong positive effects, meaning increases in knowledge substantially improve attitude, and
increases in attitude significantly enhance behavior. In contrast, the direct effect of financial knowledge on
financial behavior was negligible and not statistically significant (β = 0.024, p = .797), showing that knowledge
alone does not directly predict members’ financial actions. The model further shows that financial knowledge
accounts for 27.6% of the variance in financial attitude, while the combined effects of knowledge and attitude
explain 36.7% of the variance in financial behavior, which are considerable proportions in behavioral research.
These findings highlight that attitude acts as the key mechanism through which knowledge influences behavior.
This shows a direct significant relationship between financial knowledge and financial attitude, which supports
the findings of Shih et al. (2022). Their study explains that individuals with higher financial knowledge are more
likely to develop positive financial attitudes. The study also found a direct significant relationship between

Relationship Between Financial Literacy and Financial Performance
The regression analysis reveals that financial literacy does not significantly predict cooperative financial
performance, as shown by a non-significant beta coefficient (β = 0.048, p = .632). The extremely low coefficient
indicates that a one-unit increase in financial literacy is associated with only a 0.048-unit increase in financial
performance, which is statistically trivial. The model’s R² = 0.002 means that financial literacy explains only 0.2%
of the variance in financial performance, far below the threshold of practical significance. This implies that in these
cooperatives, financial performance is determined by other organizational factors such as governance quality,
managerial decisions, internal controls, and capital management, rather than the financial literacy levels of
individual members. Thus, improving members’ financial literacy alone is not enough to enhance the
cooperatives’ financial outcomes.

Conclusion
This finding reveals that financial attitude serves as a full mediator between financial knowledge and financial
behavior, addressing the crucial underlying mechanism that translates what an individual knows into how they
actually act. While it is commonly assumed that simply teaching people financial facts will automatically improve
their habits, our analysis demonstrates that financial knowledge lacks a direct pipeline to behavioral outcomes;
instead, raw information remains functionally dormant unless it successfully alters an individual’s internal
mindset and psychological disposition toward managing money. Consequently, we can conclude that knowledge
alone is a necessary but insufficient condition for behavioral change, as a person may fully comprehend the basics
of finance but it will fail to yield better financial choices if their attitude remains unchanged.
Moreover, members' financial literacy does not influence the financial performance of the cooperatives. While
members remain essential to cooperative operations, since they serve as the primary source of capital through
share contributions and patronage, generate revenue through their patronage, and sustain activities through
continuous transactions, their level of financial literacy alone is not enough to have an effect on the cooperative's
performance. Instead, the cooperative's performance may be shaped more strongly by organizational factors such
as governance, operations, and management. As a result, improving the financial literacy of members on its own
does not lead to measurable enhancements of performance instead, cooperative performance depends more on
how effectively the organization is managed and how actively members participate in its systems and operations.
As a recommendation, cooperative leaders and educators should move away from relying on information-heavy
lectures about financial management and instead design training programs that connect with people on a personal
level to shape their mindsets, confidence, and long-term financial attitudes. At the same time, because members’
financial literacy alone does not automatically translate into financial performance, leaders must also invest in
other aspects like improving governance and management, strengthening operations, and building user-friendly
systems that make it easy and rewarding for members to participate regularly. Future researchers may conduct
experiments to test whether attitude-focused training actually changes behavior better than traditional methods.
Additionally, they may also look into specific cooperative aspects such as governance, operational workflows,
and management systems as predictors of financial performance.
