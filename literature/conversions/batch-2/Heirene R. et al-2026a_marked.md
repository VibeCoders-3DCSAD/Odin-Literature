Development of lower-risk guidelines 
for online sports and race betting in Australia 
using objective behavioural data

Robert M. Heirene1*, Dilushi Chandrakumar1, Gary Fahey1, Eunice L. Y. Huynh1 and Sally M. Gainsbury1

Abstract
Background  Lower risk gambling guidelines provide clear, actionable advice for reducing the risk associated with 
gambling by specifying behaviour associated with non-harmful use. Here we aimed to develop lower-risk guidelines 
specific to online sports and race betting in Australia that [1] captured behaviours unique to this activity associated 
with high risk of experiencing gambling harms (e.g., depositing frequency, number of sports & races bet on) and [2] 
were based on objective behavioural data from a gambling operator not subject to self-report inaccuracies.

Methods  Customers from two large online wagering sites in Australia owned by the same parent company were 
invited to take part in a survey including the Problem Gambling Severity Index (PGSI) and the Gambling Harm 
Measure (GHM). Survey responses were matched to customer’s account data provided by the operator, including all 
bets and transactions placed six months before and after the survey. The final sample consisted of 1,647 participants 
(84.6% male; mean age = 44.1).

Results  Receiver Operating Characteristic curve analyses were used to identify optimal threshold values for PGSI 
and GHM harm separately across eight behavioural indicators: frequency, spend, percentage of household income 
spent, number of different sports/races bet on, deposit frequency, deposit amount, percentage of household income 
deposited, and number of active wagering accounts (self-reported). All indicators had acceptable Area Under the 
Curve values (AUC; ≥0.6) except gambling frequency. Optimal threshold values were higher for GHM than PGSI harm, 
and higher than those established in studies using self-report data (e.g., ≤6% vs. ≤1% of monthly income spent) 
irrespective of the measure of harm. A dose-response relationship was observed, whereby surpassing more limits was 
generally associated with greater odds of reporting harm.

Conclusions  Our findings provide preliminary support for the following Lower-Risk Guidelines for Online Sports and 
Race Betting in Australia: [1] make no more than four deposits per month, [2] deposit no more than 2% of your pre-tax 
household income per month, [3] bet with no more than two different sites or apps, and [4] stick to betting on four or 
fewer different types of sports or races. We strongly encourage further evaluation and testing of these guidelines to 
confirm their value across samples and over time.

Background
  Originating  in  Canada,  several  research  groups  have 
attempted  to  establish  ‘lower-risk’  guidelines  for  people 
who gamble [1, 2]. These guidelines assume a consump-
tion  or  dose-response  relationship  between  engagement 
with gambling (e.g., time and money spent) and the risk 
and/or  harm  relating  to  it  [3]. The  guidelines  are  analo-
gous  to  those  provided  for  lower-risk  alcohol  consump-
tion  (e.g.,  “Drink  no  more  than  10  standard  drinks  in  a 
week and no more than four standard drinks on any day”, 
4).  While  the  guidelines  do  not  endorse  any  ‘safe’  level 
of  gambling,  they  offer  practical  advice  for  people  who 
choose to gamble on how to do so in a lower risk man-
ner. In doing so, they may provide an effective alternative 
to either proposing abstinence or the ambiguous “gamble 
responsibly”  narrative  that  has  historically  dominated 
public  health  messaging  campaigns  despite  evidence  of 
its inefficacy as a harm reduction strategy [5, 6].

The  Canadian  lower-risk  guidelines  [7]  were  derived 
from  cross-provincial  survey  data  and  include  the  fol-
lowing recommendations: [1] gamble no more than 1% of 
household income (pre-tax), [2] gamble no more than 4 
days per month, and [3] avoid engaging with two or more 
types  of  gambling  in  a  month.  Using  population  preva-
lence  surveys  from  eight  different  countries,  Hodgins 
et  al.  [8]  found  some  variation  in  the  most  appropriate 
values  for  these  limits,  although  they  remained  within 
a  restricted  band  (e.g.,  gambling  no  more  than  5  to  8 
days per month, spending no more than 1 to 3% of gross 
household  income).  Other  researchers  have  developed 
similar  guidelines  specific  to  jurisdictions  [9,  10].  For 
example,  the  Australian  population  guidelines  include 
gambling no more than 0.83 to 1.68% of personal income 
per year, spending no more than $380-$615 per year on 
gambling,  gambling  on  no  more  than  20  to  30  days  per 
year,  and  avoiding  regularly  gambling  at  more  than  two 
types of games [11].

Existing  research  has  provided  a  solid  foundation  for 
understanding  appropriate  limits  for  lower-risk  gam-
bling  behaviour.  It  is,  however,  limited  by  at  least  two 
significant  factors.  First,  all  but  one  European-based 
investigation [12] used self-reported gambling behaviour 
to  develop  lower-risk  limits.  That  is,  people  were  asked 
how  much  money  they  had  spent  and  how  many  days 
they  gambled  over  the  past  year  with  all  types  of  gam-
bling.  These  values  were  subsequently  correlated  with 
self-reported  harm  to  identify  appropriate  values  that 
appeared  best  to  differentiate  harmful  and  non-harmful 
gambling.  This  is  problematic  as  research  shows  that 
most people are highly inaccurate in reporting their past

30 day behaviour on one gambling site [13, 14], let alone 
when  asked  to  summarise  past  year  behaviour  for  all 
types of gambling. In the study by Heirene et al. [14], the 
median difference between actual and estimated net out-
come  on  an  online  wagering  site  in  Australia  was  100% 
of the actual value (e.g., someone who thought that they 
lost  $1,000  typically  actually  lost  $2,000,  or  $0).  Stud-
ies also find the accuracy of self-reporting declines with 
increased  betting  and  expenditure,  further  complicat-
ing the relationship between self-reported spend and the 
experience of harm [13–15].

Second,  research  has  almost  exclusively  focused  on 
developing limits that are applicable to all forms of gam-
bling (cf., 16). This, as noted by Currie et al. [1], is prob-
lematic as different types of gambling (e.g., lottery, sports 
betting, electronic gaming machines [EGMs]) are associ-
ated with different levels of risk in terms of the associated 
likelihood  of  developing  gambling  problems  and  harms 
[17].  There  are  parallels  here  with  alcohol  consump-
tion  –  different  types  of  alcohol  bring  different  risks  for 
consumers and therefore the guidance for one type (e.g., 
spirits) should not be the same for others (e.g., beer, 18) 
given differences in ethanol concentration (referred to as 
alcohol  by  volume  [ABV]  and  often  expressed  as  a  per-
centage of total volume). However, while alcohol can be 
standardised  to  single  units,  thus  permitting  cross-type 
recommendations, this cannot be achieved for gambling 
where the behaviour varies from type to type (e.g., maxi-
mum bet size, speed of play, bet-outcome delay, and the 
expected  rate  of  return).  Few  studies  have  attempted  to 
develop  limits  for  specific  forms  of  gambling  [16,  19], 
and  even  fewer  have  focused  on  developing  guidelines 
that target key risk behaviours unique to the activity (e.g., 
depositing  money  into  one’s  account  for  online  gam-
bling,  or  time  on  device  for  EGM  gambling).  Variability 
between the limits identified for an online sample versus 
those  established  for  land-based  gambling  led  Louder-
back et al. [12] to conclude that unique limits might apply 
to  different  gambling  populations.  Consistent  with  this, 
77%  of  gambling  experts  and  73%  of  the  general  public 
surveyed by Dowling et al. [20] believed lower-risk guide-
lines should be developed for each type of gambling.

Developing limits specific to online forms of gambling 
may  be  particularly  beneficial  given  their  rapid  growth 
and  unique  risk  factors.  Online  gambling  is  the  fastest 
growing  form  of  gambling  in  Australia  and  worldwide 
[21] and has been linked to elevated rates of problematic 
gambling [22, 23], particularly among those who gamble 
with  their  mobile  device  [24].  Unlike  traditional  land-
based  or  retail  gambling,  online  gambling  is  accessible

Online  betting  is  one  of  the  most  common  forms  of 
gambling  engaged  in  by  younger  generations  [21,  28], 
who are often the most at risk of experiencing gambling-
related harms [29]. A recent systematic review of 21 stud-
ies from 18 countries found younger adults (from 18 up 
to  34  years)  were  1.51  times  more  likely  than  middle-
aged  adults  to  report  problematic  gambling,  although 
the  criteria  used  to  determine  problem  gambling  status 
varied  between  studies  [30].  Young  people  in  Australia 
and  internationally  are  now  exposed  to  gambling  prod-
ucts and marketing in a variety of novel ways, including 
through  social  media  [31],  affiliate  marketing  via  so-
called “influencers” [32], sponsoring of sports teams [33], 
and TV advertisements during and around events known 
to  attract  young  people  (e.g.,  sporting  events,  [34]). The 
elevated risk of gambling problems among young people 
[22, 35] has been recognised in recent government gam-
bling policy in the Netherlands [36]. This policy requires a 
lower automatic deposit limit for those aged 18–24 (€300 
a month) versus those aged 25 and over (€700 a month). 
Few studies have attempted to determine whether lower-
risk  guidelines  should  differ  for  younger  populations, 
who are likely to have fewer resources to offset the nega-
tive impact of any of gambling they engage with. Currie 
et al. [1] reported that risk curves showing the relation-
ship between behaviours (e.g., gambling spend) and harm 
rates appeared similar for younger and older populations 
after dividing their sample using a median split. However, 
the  absence  of  tailored  lower-risk  guidelines  for  young 
adults—those  most  vulnerable  to  harm  as  they  navigate 
their early exposure to legal gambling—represents a gap 
in the research on this topic.

Lower-risk  gambling  guidelines  have  strong  potential 
to  offer  consumers  clear,  actionable  advice  they  can  fol-
low  to  maintain  lower  risk  levels  of  gambling.  Thus,  we 
aimed  to  develop  lower-risk  guidelines  that  are  [1]  spe-
cific  to  online  sports  and  race  betting  in  Australia1  (the 
only  legal  form  of  online  gambling  other  than  lottery 
in  Australia),  [2]  reflect  the  risk  behaviours  relevant  to 
that  particular  type  of  gambling  (e.g.,  amount  of  money 
deposited  into  one’s  account),  and  [3]  derived  from  a

Procedures

In January 2024, we received a dataset containing unique 
customer  identification  numbers  for  all  24,829  eligible 
participants.  Unique  invitation  links  were  generated

Outcome variables
PGSI  harm  index:  The  PGSI  is  a  commonly  used  prob-
lem gambling screening measure with nine items scored 
on  a  four-point  Likert  scale  from  “Never”  to  “Always” 
(e.g., “How often have you bet more than you could really 
afford to lose?”, 37). Items in our survey focused on expe-
riences  over  the  past  12  months.  Consistent  with  previ-
ous  investigations  of  lower-risk  guidelines  [1,  11],  we 
focused  on  the  seven  items  relating  to  gambling  harms 
within the PGSI (i.e., feeling guilty, losing more than one 
can afford, recognition of the problem, health problems, 
financial problems, criticism from others, and borrowing 
money). The two items that  relate to needing  to gamble 
with  more  money  and  loss  chasing  (typically  ordered  as 
items 2 and 3) were omitted as these focus on behaviours 
that  are  indicative  of  problematic  or  disordered  gam-
bling, but do not necessarily represent experienced harm. 
For  each  of  the  seven  harm-focused  items,  participants 
were  assigned  a  score  of  0  (representing  a  response  of 
“Never”) or 1 (representing a score of “sometimes”, “most 
of  the  time”,  or  “almost  always”).  A  score  of  2  or  more 
across these seven items was used to classify a participant 
as harmed, again consistent with prior research using this 
same index [1, 20].

GHM harm index: For our second harm index, we used 
the Gambling Harm Measure [38], a 16-item scale mea-
suring  the  impact  of  gambling  across  six  life  domains 
(financial,  psychological,  relationship,  physical  health, 
work/study, and legal). Gooding et al. [38] share the full 
list  of  GHM  items  in  the  recent  evaluation  of  the  mea-
sure, which found strong correlations between the GHM 
and other measures of gambling-related harm. Dichoto-
mous  responses  (yes/no)  are  provided  to  all  questions 
which  are  asked  in  relation  to  the  last  12  months  (e.g., 
In the last 12 MONTHS, have you experienced any seri-
ous relationship consequences because of your gambling? 
For  example,  have  you  lost  friends  or  family;  or  expe-
rienced  separation  or  divorce;  or  engaged  in  physically 
violent  arguments?  ).  Questions  are  asked  in  a  graded 
manner  with  three  questions  for  each  domain,  the  first 
asking  about  prioritising  gambling  over  an  activity/
responsibility,  the  second  asking  about  strains  or  pres-
sures  caused  by  gambling  in  that  domain,  and  the  third 
asking  about  serious  consequences  of  gambling  in  that 
domain  (the  third  level  of  questioning  is  only  presented 
to participants if they say they have experienced harm at 
the strain/pressure level in that domain). Any participant 
who  responded  “yes”  to  a  question  at  the  second  level 
of  any  domain  (i.e.,  strain/pressure  level  of  harm)  was 
classed as having experienced harms. Note that the legal 
domain only has one question (“In the last 12 MONTHS, 
have you done anything illegal due to your gambling? For 
example, have you stolen money or valuables, or commit-
ted  fraud  or  embezzlement,  etc.?”).  A  positive  response

to  this  question  also  resulted  in  a  participant  being 
classed as harmed.

Participants’  scores  on  the  PGSI  and  GHM  were 
strongly  and  positively  correlated  (weighted  Spearman’s 
rank test: rs [1,578] = 0.71).

Behavioural indicators used for lower-risk 
guidelines
Behaviours used to derive lower-risk guidelines were gen-
erated  using  a  combination  of  behavioural  account  data 
and  survey  responses.  We  first  generated  the  following 
four behavioural indicators that were analogous to those 
studied  in  other  lower-risk  gambling  guideline  studies 
(i.e., gambling frequency, spend, spend as a percentage of 
income, & breadth of gambling activity):

Gambling  frequency  per  month:  The  average  num-
ber of active betting days (i.e., days in which at least one 
bet  was  placed)  per  month  in  the  six  months  preceding 
participation.

Monthly  spend  on  bets:  The  average  amount  staked/
spent  on  bets  per  month  in  the  six  months  preceding 
participation.  Note,  this  refers  only  to  the  amount  bet, 
and not the amount lost.

Percentage  of  monthly  household  income  spent:  In 
the  survey,  participants  were  asked  to  indicate  which 
household  income  bracket  most  closely  reflected  their 
annual income (e.g., $50,000 - $59,999 per year, $60,000 
-  $79,999  per  year)  and  as  such,  we  did  not  have  exact

for  the  bracket  “$80,000  -  $99,999/yr”,  the  middle  value

was  an  income  of  more  than  $200,000  a  year.  To  deter-
mine  an  appropriate  value  that  would  split  this  popula-
tion  equally,  we  used  ABS  census  data  which  contains 
higher income brackets than we used. Using this data, we 
computed the total number of people in income brackets 
above $200,000 a year and identified the midpoint value 
within these brackets that most closely split those earn-
ing more than $200,000 into two even groups: $257,374.

Data analysis

All  statistical  analyses  were  conducted  using  R  version 
4.4.2  [40]  and  followed  the  five  steps  outlined  in  our 
preregistration.  The  assumptions  underlying  all  statisti-
cal  models—including  multicollinearity,  normality  of 
residuals,  and  the  impact  of  influential  observations—
were  evaluated  using  the  ‘check_model()’  function  from 
the  ‘performance’  R  package  [41].  Our  Analysis  Docu-
ment  presents  our  analysis  code  alongside  all  outcomes 
presented  here  and  supplementary  findings,  including 
full statistical model outputs and assumption testing out-
comes

i n  e s -  f o r  - o n l  i n  e - w  a g e  r i n g  - a  n a l y s i s - d o c u m e n t /).  We  used

Stage  I:  We  first  determined  whether  each  participant 
met the criteria for harm using the PGSI and GHM indi-
ces  and  computed  unweighted  frequency  counts  and 
weighted  percentages  for  participants  in  harmed  and 
unharmed  categories.  Weighted  percentages  accounted 
for  our  oversampling  of  “at-risk”  customers  flagged  by 
the  operator’s  risk  detection  system  by  applying  sample 
weights (adjusted for response rates) that adjusted for the 
relative underrepresentation of “not at-risk” customers.

Stage  II  (H1):  Optimal  cut-off  points  for  each  of  the 
eight behavioural indicators of risk were identified using 
three  different  approaches.  For  all  approaches,  we  used 
`cutpointr`  [42],  an  R  package  designed  specifically  to 
assist with identifying optimal limit values in binary clas-
sification  scenarios.  The  three  approaches  to  identify-
ing cut-offs were [1] balancing specificity and sensitivity 
using the Youden index, [2] maximising sensitivity whilst 
ensuring a minimum specificity of 0.5, and [3] maximis-
ing  specificity  whilst  ensuring  a  minimal  sensitivity  of 
0.5. We  used  bootstrapping  with  replacement  and  2,000 
samples  to  ensure  the  optimal  cut-off  identified  were 
robust  to  variations  in  sampling  procedures  and  pro-
duce  95%  confidence  intervals  (CIs)  for  Area  Under  the 
Receiver Operating Characteristic curve values (hereafter 
“AUC”  values).  AUC  values  reflect  a  behavioural  indica-
tor’s overall ability to discriminate between harmed and 
unharmed  groups  independent  of  any  specific  cut-off 
point.  We  used  conventional  guidelines  (e.g.,  43,44)  to 
interpret  AUC  values  as  failed  (0.50–0.59),  fair/accept-
able (0.60–0.79), good (0.80–0.89), or excellent (0.9–1.0). 
Per  our  preregistration,  we  considered  a  behavioural 
indicator to have acceptable classification ability if it had 
an AUC value of 0.6 or more. It is important to note that 
some studies use 0.70 as a cut-off for acceptable AUC val-
ues, although this varies [44] and we have selected 0.60 as 
our minimum acceptable level as the most similar study

Results
The 1,647 people who completed all PGSI items formed 
our  initial  sample  for  analyses.  Of  this  sample,  84.6% 
were male (1.6% were recorded as having an “unknown” 
gender by the operator) and the mean age was 44.1 years 
(SD = 15.5)4.

Rates of harm

Among the 1,647 people who completed the PGSI items, 
893 met the criteria for experiencing harm (equivalent to 
50% of the weighted sample). Among the 1,580 who com-
pleted  all  items  of  the  GHM,  472  (25%  of  the  weighted 
sample)  met  the  criteria  for  experiencing  harm.  Almost 
all those meeting criteria for harm according to the GHM 
met PGSI criteria for harm; however, approximately half 
meeting  PGSI  criteria  did  not  meet  GHM  criteria,  sug-

Predictive ability of the optimal lower-risk guidelines

The  optimal  cut-off  points  identified  for  behavioural 
indicators  in  the  prior  section  were,  where  appropriate, 
rounded to more easily interpreted and actionable values 
before  including  them  in  analyses  presented  in  this  sec-
tion  (e.g.,  the  cut-off  for  number  of  deposits  per  month 
used  to  classify  PGSI  harm  was  rounded  up  from  3.81 
to 4). Note that being above, rather than at or above the 
cut-offs was used to denote higher-risk gambling to align 
with  the  practical  application  of  lower-risk  guidelines 
(i.e.,  to  suggest  upper  limits  for  behaviour  rather  than 
points at which behaviour becomes harmful).

The  upper  section  of  Table  3  displays  outcomes  from 
16  binary  logistic  regression  models  (eight  when  PGSI 
harm was the outcome and eight when GHM harm was 
the outcome) testing H2.1—that is, individually examin-
ing  the  impact  of  surpassing  each  limit  on  harm  (all  16 
models  included  the  demographic  and  other  gambling 
covariates  specified  in  our  data  analysis  section  [Stage 
III],  though  these  are  not  presented  in  Table  3  for  ease 
of interpretation). Supporting H 2.1, after accounting for 
the  impact  of  demographic  factors  and  other  gambling, 
gambling  above  each  was  associated  with  significantly 
greater  odds  of  harm  on  both  the  PGSI  and  GHM.  The 
limit associated with the largest odds of reporting harm 
was depositing more than 2% of one’s monthly household 
income per month for PGSI harm (odds ratio [OR] = 3.78, 
95%  CIs  [2.70,  5.30])  and  depositing  more  than  10%  of 
one’s  monthly  household  income  per  month  for  GHM 
harm (OR = 7.10, 95% CI [4.50, 11.2]).

The  lower  section  of  Table  3  presents  the  outcomes 
from two models (one each for PGSI and GHM) testing 
H2.2.  These  included  covariates  and  all  limits  simulta-
neously  to  account  for  their  individual  impact  on  harm. 
Contrary  to  H2.2,  the  only  limits  that  remained  signifi-
cantly predictive of PGSI harm were three online wager-
ing  specific  behavioural  indicators—depositing  more

than  four  times  per  month,  depositing  more  than  2%  of 
one’s monthly income per month, and having more than 
two active wagering accounts. For GHM harm, spending 
more  than  9%  of  one’s  monthly  household  income  per 
month, depositing more than 14 times a month, deposit-
ing more than 10% of one’s household income per month, 
and  having  more  than  three  active  wagering  accounts 
remained  uniquely  predictive  of  harm.  Being  younger, 
male, and having recently spent money on other forms of 
gambling were significantly predictive of PGSI and GHM 
harm. Unexpectedly, betting more than six (PGSI harm) 
or 9 days (GHM harm) per month was associated with a 
reduced  likelihood  of  experiencing  harm  in  these  limit-
adjusted models.

Discussion
The  aim  of  this  study  was  to  develop  lower-risk  guide-
lines specific to online wagering in Australia using survey 
responses with matched objective account data. We pro-
vide  a  discussion  of  our  findings,  their  limitations,  and 
practical implications below.

Technical identification of optimal lower-risk limits for 
online wagering

As  per  our  preregistration,  we  selected  the  limit  from 
three  approaches  (maximising  Youden,  sensitivity  or 
specificity)  that  resulted  in  the  highest  overall  accu-
racy.  Maximising  Youden  and  sensitivity  values  led  to 
cut-offs with the highest accuracy levels for PGSI harm, 
whereas  maximising  specificity  and  Youden  values  led 
to the most accurate cut-offs for GHM harm. This likely 
results  from  base  rate  differences  in  harm  between  the 
two outcomes in our sample. As GHM-determined harm 
was only prevalent in just under 30%  of the unweighted 
sample,  increasing  the  limits  and  thereby  not  misclassi-
fying  non-harmed  participants  as  harmed  (i.e.,  increas-
ing the true negative rate) led to the greatest increase in 
accuracy in GHM classification. By contrast, PGSI harm 
was more common, reported by slightly more than half of 
the  unweighted  sample;  in  this  scenario,  overemphasis-
ing either sensitivity or specificity will have a less biased 
impact on accuracy rates (hence why Youden-maximised 
thresholds were more common for PGSI harm classifica-
tion).  This  demonstrates  the  need  to  carefully  consider 
the  metric(s)  by  which  limits  are  selected  and  how  the 
proportion of positive and negative cases in a sample may 
impact outcomes.

All  threshold  values  for  classifying  PGSI  and  GHM 
harm  we  identified  were  higher  than  those  observed  in 
previous  attempts  to  derive  Australian  guidelines  [11, 
19]. The outcomes suggest our proposed limits will result 
in  similar  or  superior  classification  accuracy.  Our  limits 
provide a more even balance of sensitivity and specificity, 
whereas  Dowling  and  colleagues’  [19]  wagering-specific 
limits  have  elevated  sensitivity  rates  and  notably  lower 
specificity  rates  resulting  from  lower  threshold  values. 
Consequentially, when the two sets of limits are applied 
at  a  population  level  there  will  be  a  more  meaningful 
impact on overall accuracy rates where the rates of harm 
are  lower  than  reported  here.  For  example,  in  Dowling 
and  colleagues’  [19]  population  prevalence  survey  sam-
ple,  only  8.34%  and  13.49%  of  horse/dog  racing  bettors 
and  sports/other  events  bettors  reported  harm  on  the 
PGSI,  compared  to  slightly  over  50%  of  the  unweighted

Comparing the clinical and practical utility of each limit

Our  analyses  indicate  that  the  eight  behavioural  indica-
tors and the limits we identified for them varied substan-
tially  in  their  ability  to  differentiate  lower  from  higher 
risk gambling. Starting with the limit with the least value 
in this respect, we found that frequency, operationalised

as  the  number  of  betting  days  per  month,  was  not  a 
strong  indicator  of  risk.  This  was  the  only  behavioural 
indicator for which the AUC value was less than 0.6 (our 
preregistered  minimum  level  of  classification  ability). 
Further,  when  all  limits  were  included  in  a  regression 
model simultaneously, average betting frequencies above 
these values was associated with a reduced likelihood of 
reporting harm. The risk curve for betting frequency fur-
ther  illustrates  its  limited  value  in  this  context,  showing 
the  “flattest”  trendline  of  any  behavioural  indicator—as 
betting days per month increased, there was little rise in 
the  proportion  of  participants  who  reported  harm  (see 
Fig. 3). These findings are consistent with prior research, 
although  they  run  contrary  to  the  inclusion  in  betting 
frequency as a standard lower-risk guidelines. Dowling et 
al. [19] found that exceeding their limit for gambling fre-
quency for sports/other events betting was not predictive 
of  harm  once  other  limits  were  included  in  their  logis-
tic  regression  model.  Louderback  and  colleagues  [12] 
found  that  while  surpassing  their  gambling  frequency 
limit (11 days per month) was associated with a positive 
screen  on  the  Brief  Biosocial  Gambling  Screen  [47],  it 
was associated with a reduction in the other risk indica-
tors  examined,  including  account  closure  and  voluntary 
self-limiting. It is unknown why exceeding the gambling

age  of  income  spent,  and  breadth  of  gambling  activity 
(defined  as  number  of  different  sports  &  races  bet  on 
here)—mostly  performed  worse  than  the  wagering-
specific  indicators  examined  (i.e.,  number  of  deposits, 
deposit  amount,  percentage  of  monthly  income  depos-
ited  per  month,  and  number  of  wagering  accounts), 
highlighting  the  importance  of  considering  activity-
specific  behavioural  indicators  for  lower-risk  guidelines. 
For  example,  when  predicting  PGSI  harm,  none  of  the 
limits  for  the  four  standard  behavioural  indicators  were 
significantly  predictive  of  harm  after  accounting  for  the 
impact of all limits simultaneously. Only gambling above 
the  limit  for  percentage  of  monthly  income  spent  per 
month  remained  significantly  predictive  of  GHM  harm. 
We  argue  below,  that  ‘spend’  is  a  more  difficult  variable 
for consumers and operators to define and calculate, and 
hence this variable, while having some predictive utility, 
is not helpful to include as a lower-risk guideline.

Exceeding limits for deposit frequency, the percentage 
of  monthly  income  deposited  per  month,  and  the  num-
ber  of  active  wagering  accounts  remained  significantly 
and strongly predictive of PGSI and GHM harm in mod-
els  including  all  limits  simultaneously.  The  importance 
of  these  behavioural  indicators  is  further  displayed  in 
Fig. 3 through the slope of each line. These findings have 
important  consequences  for  understanding  risk  in  the 
context  of  online  wagering.  First,  increased  expenditure 
on gambling relative to one’s household income is a sub-
stantially  stronger  indicator  risk  than  absolute  expen-
diture.  Second,  depositing  behaviours  appear  strongly 
linked  to  risk  and  may  reflect  loss  chasing  behaviour, 
gambling  beyond  intended  amounts,  or  loss  of  con-
trol [49]. Third, the number of active wagering accounts 
someone currently holds may be an important risk factor 
in this population. This indicator may be both a cause and 
a consequence of gambling harm. For instance, individu-
als experiencing harm might switch between platforms in 
search of better outcomes or to avoid detection by opera-
tors monitoring risky behaviour. Additionally, being reg-
istered  on  multiple  sites  may  increase  their  exposure  to 
advertisements and promotional offers that contribute to 
harmful gambling.

Strengths and limitations of the current study

Our  study  possesses  several  strengths.  It  is  the  first  to 
focus  on  developing  limits  specific  to  online  wagering, 
including limits for standard and activity-specific behav-
ioural indicators validated against harm according to the 
PGSI and the GHM, and one of only two studies to have 
used objective behavioural account data to derive lower-
risk  guidelines  [12].  We  used  bootstrapping  to  select 
optimal  threshold  values  for  eight  different  behavioural 
indicators  using  three  separate  approaches;  employed 
weighted  logistic  regression  models  to  validate  the  pre-
dictive  ability  of  these  limits  after  accounting  for  other, 
non-wagering  gambling  and  demographic  factors;  and 
present  all  outcomes  transparently  here  and/or  in  our 
online  Analysis  Document  containing  extensive  supple-
mental  outcomes.  We  also  compare  the  performance  of 
our  limits  with  those  most  similar  developed  by  Dowl-
ing  et  al.  [19]  and  demonstrate  the  need  to  potentially 
set  lower  lower-risk  threshold  values  for  younger  popu-
lations. Lastly, our study uses some of the most recently 
available  data  (2023–2024)  to  derive  these  limits.  For 
example, despite all being published in 2021, Louderback 
and  colleagues  [12]  note  that  their  samples  were  “more 
than nine years old” and the studies by Dowling et al. [11, 
19]  used  data  from  surveys  collected  between  2011  and 
2015. Further, Currie et al. [1] used data from a longitu-
dinal survey that ran from 2006 to 2011. Whilst all these 
studies  have  provided  strong  insights  into  the  selection 
and value of lower-risk gambling guidelines, using more 
recent data to derive accurate limits may be particularly 
important  in  the  context  of  the  ever-changing  online 
gambling landscape.

There are several limitations to our study. In a separate 
analysis of the dataset used for this study, Heirene et al. 
[39]  found  the  sample  of  customers  who  completed  the 
survey  were  older  and  more  frequent  bettors  relative  to 
all  of  those  invited,  meaning  our  limits  may  be  skewed 
towards those more involved in online gambling. Further, 
whilst using objective behavioural account data to derive 
limits  avoids  biases  in  recall  or  self-preservation  when 
reporting  expenditure,  it  only  captures  gambling  with 
one  site.  We  attempted  to  account  for  this  by  including 
the  self-reported  number  of  wagering  accounts  some-
body held and their other recent gambling as covariates 
in  statistical  models,  though  these  are  unlikely  to  fully 
capture the variety and extent of gambling outside of the 
site  participants  were  recruited  from.  One  solution  to 
overcoming the limitations of both self-report and single-
operator  data  for  developing  guidelines  may  be  to  use 
open  banking  data. This  can  provide  objective  spending 
data across all operators that an individual gambles with, 
as well as comparisons to income and other expenses [50, 
51].

Practical applications and recommended future research

Like Louderback and colleagues [12], we call for further 
evaluations  of  our  newly  established  limits  for  online 
wagering  in  other  samples  and  against  other  indica-
tors  of  risk  (e.g.,  self-exclusion,  setting,  &  changing/set-
ting  deposit  limits).  One  notable  caution  is  that  online 
wagering  behaviour  is  highly  dynamic  and  influenced 
by  government  policies,  available  gambling  formats  and 
operator  actions  such  as  promotions.  Research  shows 
that  changes  to  how  products  are  provided  are  likely  to 
influence  gambling  behaviour;  for  example,  slowing  reel 
spins for EGMs was trialled in an attempt to reduce gam-
bling losses but resulted in customers wagering more per 
spin [52]. Thus, if policies were introduced such as to cap 
deposit  limits,  this  would  likely  change  this  behaviour 
and the predictive value of depositing in identifying risk. 
Nonetheless,  we  proffer  recommendations  for  how  our 
outcomes could be used to inform harm reduction efforts 
and how future research can support these efforts.

First, comparing the optimal limits identified for PGSI 
and GHM harm revealed that the latter resulted in sub-
stantially  higher  threshold  values,  likely  due  to  a  higher 
perceived level of harm being required for participants to 
endorse  the  items  on  this  measure.  We  initially  suggest 
the limits for PGSI harm are likely to be of greater value 
than those for GHM harm if wanting to ensure adequate 
sensitivity levels for lower-level harms.

Second,  we  recommend  focusing  on  depositing  as 
a  key  behavioural  indicator  as  this  is  an  unambigu-
ous  transaction  with  little  room  for  misinterpretation 
and  our  results  show  that  this  is  a  significant  indicator 
of  risk.  Conversely,  in  the  context  of  online  wagering, 
‘spend’  is  commonly  interpreted  in  different  ways  [53]. 
For  example,  some  consumers  may  consider  spend  the 
amount lost (net outcome), the amount deposited, or the 
total  amount  staked  (as  used  here)7.  Deposits  are  argu-
ably  easier  for  consumers  to  mentally  track  than  spend. 
Online  wagering  increases  the  ability  to  track  these 
spend within accounts, depending on how this is defined, 
however,  deposits  have  the  additional  benefit  of  being 
tracked independently and across operators by consum-
ers  through  payment  providers  and  contrasted  with 
any  withdrawals.  It  has  been  recommended  that  banks 
include gambling as a specific category of spend in sum-
maries  for  customers  to  assist  customers  with  tracking 
their  spend  across  other  expenses  such  as  utilities,  gro-
ceries, and entertainment [50].

Third, our findings may provide an empirical basis for 
income-relative  limit  setting,  whether  voluntary  or  gov-
ernment mandated. Concerns and uncertainty regarding

would have to provide salary data to enable this limit to 
be checked, thus still necessitating value-based levels as a 
cut-point for further assessments.

Our  findings  indicate  that  the  relationship  between 
single  behavioural  indicators  and  harm  is  not  one  of  a 
steep, linear trendline, consistent with previous research 
on behavioural indicators for gambling risk [49]. Yet, the 
odds-ratios presented here (Fig. 2) indicate that exceed-
ing  any  of  the  recommended  levels  increases  the  risk  of 
experiencing gambling harms; therefore, our findings do 
not  suggest  that  breaching  a  single  limit  is  without  risk. 
Rather,  exceeding  multiple  limits  compounds  the  risk 
exponentially,  and  therefore  warrants  prompt  action 
by  both  the  individual  and  operators  if  observed.  Con-
versely,  a  notable  minority  of  individuals  who  gamble 
below limits still report harm (hence why we have opted 
to use the term “lower-risk guidelines” rather than “low-
risk  guidelines”),  and  thus  stakeholders  should  not  con-
sider sub-limit gambling risk-free or ‘safe’.

The  perceived  value  and  impact  of  our  recommended 
limits  remain  unknown.  If  consumers  do  not  find  the 
limits  developed  here  to  be  credible  then  their  value  as 
harm reduction measures may be low. One recent study 
in  Finland  used  surveys  to  assess  the  perceived  feasibil-
ity  and  acceptability  of  the  Canadian  low-risk  gambling 
guidelines  [10],  finding  broad  support  for  their  over-
all  value  as  a  harm  reduction  tool  and  for  the  specific 
threshold values set for the limits. Replicating this work 
in  an  Australian  sample  with  the  limits  developed  here 
will be an important step in evaluating their harm-reduc-
tion  potential  as  well  as  the  practical  utility  in  terms  of 
how to encourage gambling customers to adhere to these.
Considering  all  our  findings,  we  tentatively  propose 
four  lower-risk  wagering  guidelines  which  we  have 
graphically  presented  in  Fig.  5.  The  limits  for  percent-
age  of  monthly  income  deposited  per  month,  number 
of  deposits  per  month,  and  number  of  active  wager-
ing  accounts  were  the  most  consistent  and  empirically 
supported  predictors  of  gambling  harm,  making  them

Conclusions
This study has established lower-risk guidelines for online 
sports  and  race  betting  in  Australia  using  an  exten-
sive  series  of  preregistered  and  exploratory  analyses  of 
objective behavioural account data with matched survey

responses. These guidelines  provide  specific  recommen-
dations  for  those  who  choose  to  gamble  that  can  guide 
them  to  do  so  in  a  lower-risk  manner.  The  proposed 
guidelines  provide  advice  that  is  relative  to  one’s  finan-
cial situation making them more targeted and actionable 
than the common instruction to “Gamble responsibly” or

2% of your pre-tax household income per month, [3] bet 
with  no  more  than  two  different  sites  or  apps,  and  [4] 
stick to betting on four or fewer different types of sports 
or  races.  Exceeding  any  of  these  guidelines  corresponds 
to an increase in the risk of experiencing gambling harms 
and  exceeding  multiple  results  in  an  exponential  risk 
increase. Further research is required to refine and adapt 
these to specific cohorts (e.g., younger adults), assess the 
perceived  acceptance  of  the  guidelines  among  consum-
ers,  and  develop  implementation  practices  to  encourage 
adherence.

Development of lower-risk guidelines
for online sports and race betting in Australia
using objective behavioural data

Robert M. Heirene1*, Dilushi Chandrakumar1, Gary Fahey1, Eunice L. Y. Huynh1 and Sally M. Gainsbury1

Abstract
Background  Lower risk gambling guidelines provide clear, actionable advice for reducing the risk associated with
gambling by specifying behaviour associated with non-harmful use. Here we aimed to develop lower-risk guidelines
specific to online sports and race betting in Australia that [1] captured behaviours unique to this activity associated
with high risk of experiencing gambling harms (e.g., depositing frequency, number of sports & races bet on) and [2]
were based on objective behavioural data from a gambling operator not subject to self-report inaccuracies.

Methods  Customers from two large online wagering sites in Australia owned by the same parent company were
invited to take part in a survey including the Problem Gambling Severity Index (PGSI) and the Gambling Harm
Measure (GHM). Survey responses were matched to customer’s account data provided by the operator, including all
bets and transactions placed six months before and after the survey. The final sample consisted of 1,647 participants
(84.6% male; mean age = 44.1).

Results  Receiver Operating Characteristic curve analyses were used to identify optimal threshold values for PGSI
and GHM harm separately across eight behavioural indicators: frequency, spend, percentage of household income
spent, number of different sports/races bet on, deposit frequency, deposit amount, percentage of household income
deposited, and number of active wagering accounts (self-reported). All indicators had acceptable Area Under the
Curve values (AUC; ≥0.6) except gambling frequency. Optimal threshold values were higher for GHM than PGSI harm,
and higher than those established in studies using self-report data (e.g., ≤6% vs. ≤1% of monthly income spent)
irrespective of the measure of harm. A dose-response relationship was observed, whereby surpassing more limits was
generally associated with greater odds of reporting harm.

Conclusions  Our findings provide preliminary support for the following Lower-Risk Guidelines for Online Sports and
Race Betting in Australia: [1] make no more than four deposits per month, [2] deposit no more than 2% of your pre-tax
household income per month, [3] bet with no more than two different sites or apps, and [4] stick to betting on four or
fewer different types of sports or races. We strongly encourage further evaluation and testing of these guidelines to
confirm their value across samples and over time.

Background
  Originating  in  Canada,  several  research  groups  have
attempted  to  establish  ‘lower-risk’  guidelines  for  people
who gamble [1, 2]. These guidelines assume a consump-
tion  or  dose-response  relationship  between  engagement
with gambling (e.g., time and money spent) and the risk
and/or  harm  relating  to  it  [3]. The  guidelines  are  analo-
gous  to  those  provided  for  lower-risk  alcohol  consump-
tion  (e.g.,  “Drink  no  more  than  10  standard  drinks  in  a
week and no more than four standard drinks on any day”,
4).  While  the  guidelines  do  not  endorse  any  ‘safe’  level
of  gambling,  they  offer  practical  advice  for  people  who
choose to gamble on how to do so in a lower risk man-
ner. In doing so, they may provide an effective alternative
to either proposing abstinence or the ambiguous “gamble
responsibly”  narrative  that  has  historically  dominated
public  health  messaging  campaigns  despite  evidence  of
its inefficacy as a harm reduction strategy [5, 6].

The  Canadian  lower-risk  guidelines  [7]  were  derived
from  cross-provincial  survey  data  and  include  the  fol-
lowing recommendations: [1] gamble no more than 1% of
household income (pre-tax), [2] gamble no more than 4
days per month, and [3] avoid engaging with two or more
types  of  gambling  in  a  month.  Using  population  preva-
lence  surveys  from  eight  different  countries,  Hodgins
et  al.  [8]  found  some  variation  in  the  most  appropriate
values  for  these  limits,  although  they  remained  within
a  restricted  band  (e.g.,  gambling  no  more  than  5  to  8
days per month, spending no more than 1 to 3% of gross
household  income).  Other  researchers  have  developed
similar  guidelines  specific  to  jurisdictions  [9,  10].  For
example,  the  Australian  population  guidelines  include
gambling no more than 0.83 to 1.68% of personal income
per year, spending no more than $380-$615 per year on
gambling,  gambling  on  no  more  than  20  to  30  days  per
year,  and  avoiding  regularly  gambling  at  more  than  two
types of games [11].

Existing  research  has  provided  a  solid  foundation  for
understanding  appropriate  limits  for  lower-risk  gam-
bling  behaviour.  It  is,  however,  limited  by  at  least  two
significant  factors.  First,  all  but  one  European-based
investigation [12] used self-reported gambling behaviour
to  develop  lower-risk  limits.  That  is,  people  were  asked
how  much  money  they  had  spent  and  how  many  days
they  gambled  over  the  past  year  with  all  types  of  gam-
bling.  These  values  were  subsequently  correlated  with
self-reported  harm  to  identify  appropriate  values  that
appeared  best  to  differentiate  harmful  and  non-harmful
gambling.  This  is  problematic  as  research  shows  that
most people are highly inaccurate in reporting their past

30 day behaviour on one gambling site [13, 14], let alone
when  asked  to  summarise  past  year  behaviour  for  all
types of gambling. In the study by Heirene et al. [14], the
median difference between actual and estimated net out-
come  on  an  online  wagering  site  in  Australia  was  100%
of the actual value (e.g., someone who thought that they
lost  $1,000  typically  actually  lost  $2,000,  or  $0).  Stud-
ies also find the accuracy of self-reporting declines with
increased  betting  and  expenditure,  further  complicat-
ing the relationship between self-reported spend and the
experience of harm [13–15].

Second,  research  has  almost  exclusively  focused  on
developing limits that are applicable to all forms of gam-
bling (cf., 16). This, as noted by Currie et al. [1], is prob-
lematic as different types of gambling (e.g., lottery, sports
betting, electronic gaming machines [EGMs]) are associ-
ated with different levels of risk in terms of the associated
likelihood  of  developing  gambling  problems  and  harms
[17].  There  are  parallels  here  with  alcohol  consump-
tion  –  different  types  of  alcohol  bring  different  risks  for
consumers and therefore the guidance for one type (e.g.,
spirits) should not be the same for others (e.g., beer, 18)
given differences in ethanol concentration (referred to as
alcohol  by  volume  [ABV]  and  often  expressed  as  a  per-
centage of total volume). However, while alcohol can be
standardised  to  single  units,  thus  permitting  cross-type
recommendations, this cannot be achieved for gambling
where the behaviour varies from type to type (e.g., maxi-
mum bet size, speed of play, bet-outcome delay, and the
expected  rate  of  return).  Few  studies  have  attempted  to
develop  limits  for  specific  forms  of  gambling  [16,  19],
and  even  fewer  have  focused  on  developing  guidelines
that target key risk behaviours unique to the activity (e.g.,
depositing  money  into  one’s  account  for  online  gam-
bling,  or  time  on  device  for  EGM  gambling).  Variability
between the limits identified for an online sample versus
those  established  for  land-based  gambling  led  Louder-
back et al. [12] to conclude that unique limits might apply
to  different  gambling  populations.  Consistent  with  this,
77%  of  gambling  experts  and  73%  of  the  general  public
surveyed by Dowling et al. [20] believed lower-risk guide-
lines should be developed for each type of gambling.

Developing limits specific to online forms of gambling
may  be  particularly  beneficial  given  their  rapid  growth
and  unique  risk  factors.  Online  gambling  is  the  fastest
growing  form  of  gambling  in  Australia  and  worldwide
[21] and has been linked to elevated rates of problematic
gambling [22, 23], particularly among those who gamble
with  their  mobile  device  [24].  Unlike  traditional  land-
based  or  retail  gambling,  online  gambling  is  accessible

anytime and anywhere via mobile devices, with minimal
physical or social barriers to potentially excessive spend-
ing  [25,  26].  Whilst  there  is  a  need  for  broad  regulatory
measures that attempt to prevent online gambling harms
at the population level [27], there is also a need to equip
all those who gamble online with the knowledge of how
to do so in a way that minimises their risk of experiencing
harm. Identifying levels at which gambling is less likely to
result in harms can also assist regulators and operators to
identify those who may be gambling in a risky manner to
guide proactive intervention.

Online  betting  is  one  of  the  most  common  forms  of
gambling  engaged  in  by  younger  generations  [21,  28],
who are often the most at risk of experiencing gambling-
related harms [29]. A recent systematic review of 21 stud-
ies from 18 countries found younger adults (from 18 up
to  34  years)  were  1.51  times  more  likely  than  middle-
aged  adults  to  report  problematic  gambling,  although
the  criteria  used  to  determine  problem  gambling  status
varied  between  studies  [30].  Young  people  in  Australia
and  internationally  are  now  exposed  to  gambling  prod-
ucts and marketing in a variety of novel ways, including
through  social  media  [31],  affiliate  marketing  via  so-
called “influencers” [32], sponsoring of sports teams [33],
and TV advertisements during and around events known
to  attract  young  people  (e.g.,  sporting  events,  [34]). The
elevated risk of gambling problems among young people
[22, 35] has been recognised in recent government gam-
bling policy in the Netherlands [36]. This policy requires a
lower automatic deposit limit for those aged 18–24 (€300
a month) versus those aged 25 and over (€700 a month).
Few studies have attempted to determine whether lower-
risk  guidelines  should  differ  for  younger  populations,
who are likely to have fewer resources to offset the nega-
tive impact of any of gambling they engage with. Currie
et al. [1] reported that risk curves showing the relation-
ship between behaviours (e.g., gambling spend) and harm
rates appeared similar for younger and older populations
after dividing their sample using a median split. However,
the  absence  of  tailored  lower-risk  guidelines  for  young
adults—those  most  vulnerable  to  harm  as  they  navigate
their early exposure to legal gambling—represents a gap
in the research on this topic.

Lower-risk  gambling  guidelines  have  strong  potential
to  offer  consumers  clear,  actionable  advice  they  can  fol-
low  to  maintain  lower  risk  levels  of  gambling.  Thus,  we
aimed  to  develop  lower-risk  guidelines  that  are  [1]  spe-
cific  to  online  sports  and  race  betting  in  Australia1  (the
only  legal  form  of  online  gambling  other  than  lottery
in  Australia),  [2]  reflect  the  risk  behaviours  relevant  to
that  particular  type  of  gambling  (e.g.,  amount  of  money
deposited  into  one’s  account),  and  [3]  derived  from  a

Procedures

In January 2024, we received a dataset containing unique
customer  identification  numbers  for  all  24,829  eligible
participants.  Unique  invitation  links  were  generated
through  Qualtrics,  where  the  survey  was  hosted,  and
matched to each identifier. These links were provided to
the operator, who disseminated them via an initial email
invitation, followed by an SMS reminder eight days later.
All participants reviewed an information sheet and pro-
vided informed consent before starting the survey. Upon
completion,  participants  were  given  the  option  to  enter
a  prize  draw  for  one  of  20  e-gift  vouchers  worth  $250,
which could not be redeemed for cash or used for gam-
bling.  Ethical  approval  for  the  study  was  granted  by  the
University  of  Sydney  Human  Ethics  Committee  [ID:
2023/029].  Our  overall  project  preregistration  outlines
further  methodological  details,  including  participant
recruitment,  survey  measures,  the  invitation  email,  and
the account data collected (https://osf.io/vdsmw).

Outcome variables
PGSI  harm  index:  The  PGSI  is  a  commonly  used  prob-
lem gambling screening measure with nine items scored
on  a  four-point  Likert  scale  from  “Never”  to  “Always”
(e.g., “How often have you bet more than you could really
afford to lose?”, 37). Items in our survey focused on expe-
riences  over  the  past  12  months.  Consistent  with  previ-
ous  investigations  of  lower-risk  guidelines  [1,  11],  we
focused  on  the  seven  items  relating  to  gambling  harms
within the PGSI (i.e., feeling guilty, losing more than one
can afford, recognition of the problem, health problems,
financial problems, criticism from others, and borrowing
money). The two items that  relate to needing  to gamble
with  more  money  and  loss  chasing  (typically  ordered  as
items 2 and 3) were omitted as these focus on behaviours
that  are  indicative  of  problematic  or  disordered  gam-
bling, but do not necessarily represent experienced harm.
For  each  of  the  seven  harm-focused  items,  participants
were  assigned  a  score  of  0  (representing  a  response  of
“Never”) or 1 (representing a score of “sometimes”, “most
of  the  time”,  or  “almost  always”).  A  score  of  2  or  more
across these seven items was used to classify a participant
as harmed, again consistent with prior research using this
same index [1, 20].

GHM harm index: For our second harm index, we used
the Gambling Harm Measure [38], a 16-item scale mea-
suring  the  impact  of  gambling  across  six  life  domains
(financial,  psychological,  relationship,  physical  health,
work/study, and legal). Gooding et al. [38] share the full
list  of  GHM  items  in  the  recent  evaluation  of  the  mea-
sure, which found strong correlations between the GHM
and other measures of gambling-related harm. Dichoto-
mous  responses  (yes/no)  are  provided  to  all  questions
which  are  asked  in  relation  to  the  last  12  months  (e.g.,
In the last 12 MONTHS, have you experienced any seri-
ous relationship consequences because of your gambling?
For  example,  have  you  lost  friends  or  family;  or  expe-
rienced  separation  or  divorce;  or  engaged  in  physically
violent  arguments?  ).  Questions  are  asked  in  a  graded
manner  with  three  questions  for  each  domain,  the  first
asking  about  prioritising  gambling  over  an  activity/
responsibility,  the  second  asking  about  strains  or  pres-
sures  caused  by  gambling  in  that  domain,  and  the  third
asking  about  serious  consequences  of  gambling  in  that
domain  (the  third  level  of  questioning  is  only  presented
to participants if they say they have experienced harm at
the strain/pressure level in that domain). Any participant
who  responded  “yes”  to  a  question  at  the  second  level
of  any  domain  (i.e.,  strain/pressure  level  of  harm)  was
classed as having experienced harms. Note that the legal
domain only has one question (“In the last 12 MONTHS,
have you done anything illegal due to your gambling? For
example, have you stolen money or valuables, or commit-
ted  fraud  or  embezzlement,  etc.?”).  A  positive  response

to  this  question  also  resulted  in  a  participant  being
classed as harmed.

Participants’  scores  on  the  PGSI  and  GHM  were
strongly  and  positively  correlated  (weighted  Spearman’s
rank test: rs [1,578] = 0.71).

Behavioural indicators used for lower-risk
guidelines
Behaviours used to derive lower-risk guidelines were gen-
erated  using  a  combination  of  behavioural  account  data
and  survey  responses.  We  first  generated  the  following
four behavioural indicators that were analogous to those
studied  in  other  lower-risk  gambling  guideline  studies
(i.e., gambling frequency, spend, spend as a percentage of
income, & breadth of gambling activity):

Gambling  frequency  per  month:  The  average  num-
ber of active betting days (i.e., days in which at least one
bet  was  placed)  per  month  in  the  six  months  preceding
participation.

Monthly  spend  on  bets:  The  average  amount  staked/
spent  on  bets  per  month  in  the  six  months  preceding
participation.  Note,  this  refers  only  to  the  amount  bet,
and not the amount lost.

Percentage  of  monthly  household  income  spent:  In
the  survey,  participants  were  asked  to  indicate  which
household  income  bracket  most  closely  reflected  their
annual income (e.g., $50,000 - $59,999 per year, $60,000
-  $79,999  per  year)  and  as  such,  we  did  not  have  exact
income  values.  Therefore,  to  estimate  annual  income,
we  first  took  the  middle/midpoint  value  within  each
bracket  to  represent  each  person’s  annual  income  (e.g.,
for  the  bracket  “$80,000  -  $99,999/yr”,  the  middle  value
would be $90,000). However, one of the response options
was  an  income  of  more  than  $200,000  a  year.  To  deter-
mine  an  appropriate  value  that  would  split  this  popula-
tion  equally,  we  used  ABS  census  data  which  contains
higher income brackets than we used. Using this data, we
computed the total number of people in income brackets
above $200,000 a year and identified the midpoint value
within these brackets that most closely split those earn-
ing more than $200,000 into two even groups: $257,374.
Estimated annual income values for all participants were
divided  by  12  to  estimate  monthly  income.  Next,  par-
ticipants’ monthly spend on bets value (as created above)
was divided by this monthly income value and multiplied
by  100  to  calculate  the  average  percentage  of  monthly
household  income  spent  each  month.  Only  1,055  par-
ticipants reported their income (responses of “Unknown”
and “I’d rather not say” were permissible).

Number of different activities (i.e., number different of
sports/races bet on): The total number of different sports
and/or races bet on in the six months preceding partici-
pation.  We  used  the  broadest  category  of  activity  type
available  in  the  data,  which  groups  by  the  name  of  the

overall  sport/event  type  (e.g.,  tennis,  boxing,  horserac-
ing). There were at least 33 different activity types present
in the data, with horseracing, greyhound racing, harness
racing,  basketball,  and  soccer  being  the  five  most  com-
mon activities by total bets placed in the month preced-

proposed will be actionable with little explanation.

residuals,  and  the  impact  of  influential  observations—

full statistical model outputs and assumption testing out-

Stage  I:  We  first  determined  whether  each  participant
met the criteria for harm using the PGSI and GHM indi-
ces  and  computed  unweighted  frequency  counts  and
weighted  percentages  for  participants  in  harmed  and
unharmed  categories.  Weighted  percentages  accounted
for  our  oversampling  of  “at-risk”  customers  flagged  by
the  operator’s  risk  detection  system  by  applying  sample
weights (adjusted for response rates) that adjusted for the
relative underrepresentation of “not at-risk” customers.

Stage  II  (H1):  Optimal  cut-off  points  for  each  of  the
eight behavioural indicators of risk were identified using
three  different  approaches.  For  all  approaches,  we  used
`cutpointr`  [42],  an  R  package  designed  specifically  to
assist with identifying optimal limit values in binary clas-
sification  scenarios.  The  three  approaches  to  identify-
ing cut-offs were [1] balancing specificity and sensitivity
using the Youden index, [2] maximising sensitivity whilst
ensuring a minimum specificity of 0.5, and [3] maximis-
ing  specificity  whilst  ensuring  a  minimal  sensitivity  of
0.5. We  used  bootstrapping  with  replacement  and  2,000
samples  to  ensure  the  optimal  cut-off  identified  were
robust  to  variations  in  sampling  procedures  and  pro-
duce  95%  confidence  intervals  (CIs)  for  Area  Under  the
Receiver Operating Characteristic curve values (hereafter
“AUC”  values).  AUC  values  reflect  a  behavioural  indica-
tor’s overall ability to discriminate between harmed and
unharmed  groups  independent  of  any  specific  cut-off
point.  We  used  conventional  guidelines  (e.g.,  43,44)  to
interpret  AUC  values  as  failed  (0.50–0.59),  fair/accept-
able (0.60–0.79), good (0.80–0.89), or excellent (0.9–1.0).
Per  our  preregistration,  we  considered  a  behavioural
indicator to have acceptable classification ability if it had
an AUC value of 0.6 or more. It is important to note that
some studies use 0.70 as a cut-off for acceptable AUC val-
ues, although this varies [44] and we have selected 0.60 as
our minimum acceptable level as the most similar study

Heirene et al. Harm Reduction Journal           (2026) 23:42 to ours that used actual betting account data to develop
lower-risk  guidelines  found  most  AUC  values  in  the
range of 0.497 to 0.664 [12].

Sensitivity,  specificity,  and  accuracy  values  were  com-
puted for all cut-offs identified. Of the three limit values
returned  for  each  behavioural  indicator  (Youden  index,
sensitivity,  &  specificity  maximised),  the  one  with  the
highest  accuracy  rating  (i.e.,  total  proportion  of  cases
correctly classified) was selected for further examination.
Stage III (H2-3): Next, we examined the ability of sur-
passing  the  new  limits  to  predict  both  harm  indices
after  adjusting  for  participants’  demographic  character-
istics  (i.e.,  age,  gender,  employment  status,  and  educa-
tion  level)  and  other  gambling  behaviour  (i.e.,  number
of additional online accounts2 and past-30-day spending
on  other  forms  of  gambling).  Logistic  regression  mod-

of  “at-risk”  customers  were  used  to  separately  test  the
impact of passing each limit in the six months preceding
survey  participation  (defined  as  having  a  monthly  aver-

GHM  harm.  Lastly,  we  performed  two  further  logistic
regression models (one each for the PGSI & GHM harm
indices)  that  used  the  total  number  of  limits  a  person
surpassed (0–8) as a predictor alongside the same demo-
graphic and gambling covariates.

Predictive ability of the optimal lower-risk guidelines

The  optimal  cut-off  points  identified  for  behavioural
indicators  in  the  prior  section  were,  where  appropriate,
rounded to more easily interpreted and actionable values

used  to  classify  PGSI  harm  was  rounded  up  from  3.81
to 4). Note that being above, rather than at or above the
cut-offs was used to denote higher-risk gambling to align
with  the  practical  application  of  lower-risk  guidelines
(i.e.,  to  suggest  upper  limits  for  behaviour  rather  than
points at which behaviour becomes harmful).

Discussion
The  aim  of  this  study  was  to  develop  lower-risk  guide-
lines specific to online wagering in Australia using survey
responses with matched objective account data. We pro-
vide  a  discussion  of  our  findings,  their  limitations,  and
practical implications below.

Technical identification of optimal lower-risk limits for
online wagering

As  per  our  preregistration,  we  selected  the  limit  from
three  approaches  (maximising  Youden,  sensitivity  or
specificity)  that  resulted  in  the  highest  overall  accu-
racy.  Maximising  Youden  and  sensitivity  values  led  to
cut-offs with the highest accuracy levels for PGSI harm,
whereas  maximising  specificity  and  Youden  values  led
to the most accurate cut-offs for GHM harm. This likely
results  from  base  rate  differences  in  harm  between  the
two outcomes in our sample. As GHM-determined harm
was only prevalent in just under 30%  of the unweighted
sample,  increasing  the  limits  and  thereby  not  misclassi-
fying  non-harmed  participants  as  harmed  (i.e.,  increas-
ing the true negative rate) led to the greatest increase in
accuracy in GHM classification. By contrast, PGSI harm
was more common, reported by slightly more than half of
the  unweighted  sample;  in  this  scenario,  overemphasis-
ing either sensitivity or specificity will have a less biased
impact on accuracy rates (hence why Youden-maximised
thresholds were more common for PGSI harm classifica-
tion).  This  demonstrates  the  need  to  carefully  consider
the  metric(s)  by  which  limits  are  selected  and  how  the
proportion of positive and negative cases in a sample may
impact outcomes.

All  threshold  values  for  classifying  PGSI  and  GHM
harm  we  identified  were  higher  than  those  observed  in
previous  attempts  to  derive  Australian  guidelines  [11,
19]. The outcomes suggest our proposed limits will result
in  similar  or  superior  classification  accuracy.  Our  limits
provide a more even balance of sensitivity and specificity,
whereas  Dowling  and  colleagues’  [19]  wagering-specific
limits  have  elevated  sensitivity  rates  and  notably  lower
specificity  rates  resulting  from  lower  threshold  values.
Consequentially, when the two sets of limits are applied
at  a  population  level  there  will  be  a  more  meaningful
impact on overall accuracy rates where the rates of harm
are  lower  than  reported  here.  For  example,  in  Dowling
and  colleagues’  [19]  population  prevalence  survey  sam-
ple,  only  8.34%  and  13.49%  of  horse/dog  racing  bettors
and  sports/other  events  bettors  reported  harm  on  the
PGSI,  compared  to  slightly  over  50%  of  the  unweighted

Comparing the clinical and practical utility of each limit

Our  analyses  indicate  that  the  eight  behavioural  indica-
tors and the limits we identified for them varied substan-
tially  in  their  ability  to  differentiate  lower  from  higher
risk gambling. Starting with the limit with the least value
in this respect, we found that frequency, operationalised

as  the  number  of  betting  days  per  month,  was  not  a
strong  indicator  of  risk.  This  was  the  only  behavioural
indicator for which the AUC value was less than 0.6 (our
preregistered  minimum  level  of  classification  ability).
Further,  when  all  limits  were  included  in  a  regression
model simultaneously, average betting frequencies above
these values was associated with a reduced likelihood of
reporting harm. The risk curve for betting frequency fur-
ther  illustrates  its  limited  value  in  this  context,  showing
the  “flattest”  trendline  of  any  behavioural  indicator—as
betting days per month increased, there was little rise in
the  proportion  of  participants  who  reported  harm  (see
Fig. 3). These findings are consistent with prior research,
although  they  run  contrary  to  the  inclusion  in  betting
frequency as a standard lower-risk guidelines. Dowling et
al. [19] found that exceeding their limit for gambling fre-
quency for sports/other events betting was not predictive
of  harm  once  other  limits  were  included  in  their  logis-
tic  regression  model.  Louderback  and  colleagues  [12]
found  that  while  surpassing  their  gambling  frequency
limit (11 days per month) was associated with a positive
screen  on  the  Brief  Biosocial  Gambling  Screen  [47],  it
was associated with a reduction in the other risk indica-
tors  examined,  including  account  closure  and  voluntary
self-limiting. It is unknown why exceeding the gambling

a reduced likelihood of reporting harm, although it may
be  that  higher-risk  gambling  in  this  sample  was  repre-

to available funds. In a separate analysis of the data pre-
sented here, increased betting and depositing around cer-

a  response  to  common  pay  days  and  sporting  events  in

The  four  “standard”  behavioural  indicators  most  simi-
lar to those used in the Canadian [7] and Australian [11]
guidelines—betting  frequency,  amount  spent,  percent-
age  of  income  spent,  and  breadth  of  gambling  activity
(defined  as  number  of  different  sports  &  races  bet  on
here)—mostly  performed  worse  than  the  wagering-
specific  indicators  examined  (i.e.,  number  of  deposits,
deposit  amount,  percentage  of  monthly  income  depos-
ited  per  month,  and  number  of  wagering  accounts),
highlighting  the  importance  of  considering  activity-
specific  behavioural  indicators  for  lower-risk  guidelines.
For  example,  when  predicting  PGSI  harm,  none  of  the
limits  for  the  four  standard  behavioural  indicators  were
significantly  predictive  of  harm  after  accounting  for  the
impact of all limits simultaneously. Only gambling above
the  limit  for  percentage  of  monthly  income  spent  per
month  remained  significantly  predictive  of  GHM  harm.
We  argue  below,  that  ‘spend’  is  a  more  difficult  variable
for consumers and operators to define and calculate, and
hence this variable, while having some predictive utility,
is not helpful to include as a lower-risk guideline.

Exceeding limits for deposit frequency, the percentage
of  monthly  income  deposited  per  month,  and  the  num-
ber  of  active  wagering  accounts  remained  significantly
and strongly predictive of PGSI and GHM harm in mod-
els  including  all  limits  simultaneously.  The  importance
of  these  behavioural  indicators  is  further  displayed  in
Fig. 3 through the slope of each line. These findings have
important  consequences  for  understanding  risk  in  the
context  of  online  wagering.  First,  increased  expenditure
on gambling relative to one’s household income is a sub-
stantially  stronger  indicator  risk  than  absolute  expen-
diture.  Second,  depositing  behaviours  appear  strongly
linked  to  risk  and  may  reflect  loss  chasing  behaviour,
gambling  beyond  intended  amounts,  or  loss  of  con-
trol [49]. Third, the number of active wagering accounts
someone currently holds may be an important risk factor
in this population. This indicator may be both a cause and
a consequence of gambling harm. For instance, individu-
als experiencing harm might switch between platforms in
search of better outcomes or to avoid detection by opera-
tors monitoring risky behaviour. Additionally, being reg-
istered  on  multiple  sites  may  increase  their  exposure  to
advertisements and promotional offers that contribute to
harmful gambling.

Strengths and limitations of the current study

Our  study  possesses  several  strengths.  It  is  the  first  to
focus  on  developing  limits  specific  to  online  wagering,
including limits for standard and activity-specific behav-
ioural indicators validated against harm according to the
PGSI and the GHM, and one of only two studies to have
used objective behavioural account data to derive lower-
risk  guidelines  [12].  We  used  bootstrapping  to  select
optimal  threshold  values  for  eight  different  behavioural
indicators  using  three  separate  approaches;  employed
weighted  logistic  regression  models  to  validate  the  pre-
dictive  ability  of  these  limits  after  accounting  for  other,
non-wagering  gambling  and  demographic  factors;  and
present  all  outcomes  transparently  here  and/or  in  our
online  Analysis  Document  containing  extensive  supple-
mental  outcomes.  We  also  compare  the  performance  of
our  limits  with  those  most  similar  developed  by  Dowl-
ing  et  al.  [19]  and  demonstrate  the  need  to  potentially
set  lower  lower-risk  threshold  values  for  younger  popu-
lations. Lastly, our study uses some of the most recently
available  data  (2023–2024)  to  derive  these  limits.  For
example, despite all being published in 2021, Louderback
and  colleagues  [12]  note  that  their  samples  were  “more
than nine years old” and the studies by Dowling et al. [11,
19]  used  data  from  surveys  collected  between  2011  and
2015. Further, Currie et al. [1] used data from a longitu-
dinal survey that ran from 2006 to 2011. Whilst all these
studies  have  provided  strong  insights  into  the  selection
and value of lower-risk gambling guidelines, using more
recent data to derive accurate limits may be particularly
important  in  the  context  of  the  ever-changing  online
gambling landscape.

There are several limitations to our study. In a separate
analysis of the dataset used for this study, Heirene et al.
[39]  found  the  sample  of  customers  who  completed  the
survey  were  older  and  more  frequent  bettors  relative  to
all  of  those  invited,  meaning  our  limits  may  be  skewed
towards those more involved in online gambling. Further,
whilst using objective behavioural account data to derive
limits  avoids  biases  in  recall  or  self-preservation  when
reporting  expenditure,  it  only  captures  gambling  with
one  site.  We  attempted  to  account  for  this  by  including
the  self-reported  number  of  wagering  accounts  some-
body held and their other recent gambling as covariates
in  statistical  models,  though  these  are  unlikely  to  fully
capture the variety and extent of gambling outside of the
site  participants  were  recruited  from.  One  solution  to
overcoming the limitations of both self-report and single-
operator  data  for  developing  guidelines  may  be  to  use
open  banking  data. This  can  provide  objective  spending
data across all operators that an individual gambles with,
as well as comparisons to income and other expenses [50,
51].

Practical applications and recommended future research

Like Louderback and colleagues [12], we call for further
evaluations  of  our  newly  established  limits  for  online
wagering  in  other  samples  and  against  other  indica-
tors  of  risk  (e.g.,  self-exclusion,  setting,  &  changing/set-
ting  deposit  limits).  One  notable  caution  is  that  online
wagering  behaviour  is  highly  dynamic  and  influenced
by  government  policies,  available  gambling  formats  and
operator  actions  such  as  promotions.  Research  shows
that  changes  to  how  products  are  provided  are  likely  to
influence  gambling  behaviour;  for  example,  slowing  reel
spins for EGMs was trialled in an attempt to reduce gam-
bling losses but resulted in customers wagering more per
spin [52]. Thus, if policies were introduced such as to cap
deposit  limits,  this  would  likely  change  this  behaviour
and the predictive value of depositing in identifying risk.
Nonetheless,  we  proffer  recommendations  for  how  our
outcomes could be used to inform harm reduction efforts
and how future research can support these efforts.

First, comparing the optimal limits identified for PGSI
and GHM harm revealed that the latter resulted in sub-
stantially  higher  threshold  values,  likely  due  to  a  higher
perceived level of harm being required for participants to
endorse  the  items  on  this  measure.  We  initially  suggest
the limits for PGSI harm are likely to be of greater value
than those for GHM harm if wanting to ensure adequate
sensitivity levels for lower-level harms.

Second,  we  recommend  focusing  on  depositing  as
a  key  behavioural  indicator  as  this  is  an  unambigu-
ous  transaction  with  little  room  for  misinterpretation
and  our  results  show  that  this  is  a  significant  indicator
of  risk.  Conversely,  in  the  context  of  online  wagering,
‘spend’  is  commonly  interpreted  in  different  ways  [53].
For  example,  some  consumers  may  consider  spend  the
amount lost (net outcome), the amount deposited, or the
total  amount  staked  (as  used  here)7.  Deposits  are  argu-
ably  easier  for  consumers  to  mentally  track  than  spend.
Online  wagering  increases  the  ability  to  track  these
spend within accounts, depending on how this is defined,
however,  deposits  have  the  additional  benefit  of  being
tracked independently and across operators by consum-
ers  through  payment  providers  and  contrasted  with
any  withdrawals.  It  has  been  recommended  that  banks
include gambling as a specific category of spend in sum-
maries  for  customers  to  assist  customers  with  tracking
their  spend  across  other  expenses  such  as  utilities,  gro-
ceries, and entertainment [50].

Third, our findings may provide an empirical basis for
income-relative  limit  setting,  whether  voluntary  or  gov-
ernment mandated. Concerns and uncertainty regarding

Our  findings  indicate  that  the  relationship  between
single  behavioural  indicators  and  harm  is  not  one  of  a
steep, linear trendline, consistent with previous research
on behavioural indicators for gambling risk [49]. Yet, the
odds-ratios presented here (Fig. 2) indicate that exceed-
ing  any  of  the  recommended  levels  increases  the  risk  of
experiencing gambling harms; therefore, our findings do
not  suggest  that  breaching  a  single  limit  is  without  risk.
Rather,  exceeding  multiple  limits  compounds  the  risk
exponentially,  and  therefore  warrants  prompt  action
by  both  the  individual  and  operators  if  observed.  Con-
versely,  a  notable  minority  of  individuals  who  gamble
below limits still report harm (hence why we have opted
to use the term “lower-risk guidelines” rather than “low-
risk  guidelines”),  and  thus  stakeholders  should  not  con-
sider sub-limit gambling risk-free or ‘safe’.

The  perceived  value  and  impact  of  our  recommended
limits  remain  unknown.  If  consumers  do  not  find  the
limits  developed  here  to  be  credible  then  their  value  as
harm reduction measures may be low. One recent study
in  Finland  used  surveys  to  assess  the  perceived  feasibil-
ity  and  acceptability  of  the  Canadian  low-risk  gambling
guidelines  [10],  finding  broad  support  for  their  over-
all  value  as  a  harm  reduction  tool  and  for  the  specific
threshold values set for the limits. Replicating this work
in  an  Australian  sample  with  the  limits  developed  here
will be an important step in evaluating their harm-reduc-
tion  potential  as  well  as  the  practical  utility  in  terms  of
how to encourage gambling customers to adhere to these.

Conclusions
This study has established lower-risk guidelines for online
sports  and  race  betting  in  Australia  using  an  exten-
sive  series  of  preregistered  and  exploratory  analyses  of
objective behavioural account data with matched survey

Heirene et al. Harm Reduction Journal           (2026) 23:42
responses. These guidelines  provide  specific  recommen-
dations  for  those  who  choose  to  gamble  that  can  guide
them  to  do  so  in  a  lower-risk  manner.  The  proposed
guidelines  provide  advice  that  is  relative  to  one’s  finan-
cial situation making them more targeted and actionable
than the common instruction to “Gamble responsibly” or
warnings  such  as  “What  are  you  really  gambling  with?”
[6]. Our recommended guidelines are [1] make no more
than four deposits per month, [2] deposit no more than
2% of your pre-tax household income per month, [3] bet
with  no  more  than  two  different  sites  or  apps,  and  [4]
stick to betting on four or fewer different types of sports
or  races.  Exceeding  any  of  these  guidelines  corresponds
to an increase in the risk of experiencing gambling harms
and  exceeding  multiple  results  in  an  exponential  risk
increase. Further research is required to refine and adapt
these to specific cohorts (e.g., younger adults), assess the
perceived  acceptance  of  the  guidelines  among  consum-
ers,  and  develop  implementation  practices  to  encourage
adherence.
