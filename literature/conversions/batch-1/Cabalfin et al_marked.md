Abstract

The Philippines aspires to become a predominantly middle-class society by 2040. Significant 
strides  have  been  achieved  in  the  reduction  of  extreme  poverty.  However,  the  COVID-19 
pandemic exposed the fragility of recent  gains  and the  vulnerability  of many households to 
economic shocks. This study examines the dynamics and characteristics of the middle-income 
class and analyzes household vulnerability to income poverty using the Family Income and 
Expenditure  Survey  and  Labor  Force  Survey  data  from  2018,  2021  and  2023  using  the 
methodology developed by Chaudhuri and Datt (2001).

Traditional poverty measures underestimate the at-risk population. Vulnerability affects 30.0 
percent of Filipino households, 2.75 times higher than the household poverty incidence of 10.9 
percent  in  2023.  Households  face  vulnerability  for  distinctly  different  reasons.  Eighty-six 
percent of vulnerable families experience income volatility, while seventy-three percent of the 
highly vulnerable have persistently low incomes. Stark rural-urban disparities also persist, with 
rural  vulnerability  incidence  at  43.0  percent  in  contrast  with  20.0  percent  in  urban  areas. 
Regional variations of vulnerability range from 9.0 percent in the National Capital Region to 
76.0 percent in rural BARMM.

These findings have significant implications for the motivation and design of social protection 
systems in the country. Social protection must evolve from reactive poverty relief to a broader 
and proactive focus on resilience building. Differentiated interventions must be based on needs 
of  specific  segments:  insurance  and  income  stabilization  mechanisms  for  the  vulnerable 
majority who experience income volatility, and poverty reduction programs targeted for the 
low-income  vulnerable.  Infrastructure  development,  education,  sectoral  transition  from 
agriculture to the highly productive sectors in services, industry and manufacturing, as well as 
climate  risk  management  emerge  as  critical  protective  factors.  Achieving  the  2040  vision 
requires bold policy reforms that expand social protection to universal coverage aligned with 
upper  middle-income  country  standards,  strengthen  household  resilience,  and  address  the 
structural factors that perpetuate vulnerability across sectors.

Keywords:  poverty  reduction,  poverty  alleviation,  social  protection,  middle-income  class, 
middle-class, inclusive growth, vulnerability to poverty, poverty, Philippines

The Middle Class and Vulnerability to Income Poverty: Implications for Social 
Protection in the Philippines

Deanne Lorraine D. Cabalfin, Jose Ramon G. Albert and  
Mohammad A. Mahmoud1

1.  Introduction

The aspiration of the Philippines to become a predominantly middle-class society by 2040 is one 
of the most ambitious socioeconomic goals in its development agenda. This vision embodies not merely 
an economic target but a fundamental transformation toward a more equitable society where prosperity 
is  shared  broadly  across  the  population.  The  country  is  poised  to  become  an  upper  middle-income 
economy in the next few years and aims to reach high-income status by 2045, with improved quality of 
life and better access to services for all citizens. This foremost attention to economic development that 
brings about poverty reduction is reflected in the Ambisyon Natin 2040 vision (NEDA 2016), as well 
as in the Philippine Development Plan. The middle-income class serves as the backbone of sustainable 
economic  growth,  providing  the  consumer  demand  necessary  to  drive  domestic  markets  while 
contributing to social stability and better democratic governance.

While the pace of progress in poverty reduction over the past decade has been steady, the more 
important question concerns its equity aspect: how pro-poor the economic development has been. For 
growth to translate equitably to poverty reduction, the middle-income class must continue to expand 
and  gain  an increasingly important representation.  The  increasing  importance of  the  middle-income 
class does not mean that policies and programs to expand or empower it are incompatible with those 
that  focus  on  poverty  reduction  and  alleviation.  As  the  Philippines  advances  towards  upper-middle 
income status, it is increasingly important to address the factors that make households vulnerable to 
poverty, as well as provide adequate social protection in terms of education, health and in the labor 
market. Equitable policies that seek to expand as well as empower the middle-income class also actually 
help uplift the poor segment of society. For instance, pursuing macroeconomic stability and investing 
in long-term human capital such as  education and health benefits everyone. Promoting policies that 
increase the labor productivity of workers and upgrade their skills, as well as connect them to markets 
and employment opportunities also benefit the majority.

The coronavirus (COVID-19) pandemic has altered the trajectory of middle-class development 
in  the  Philippines,  revealing  both  the  resilience  and  vulnerability  of  households  that  had  recently 
achieved middle-income status (Albert et al. 2024). The economic disruptions brought about by the 
pandemic exposed the fragility of middle-class gains, with many households experiencing downward 
mobility despite previous progress. This study examines the evolving dynamics of the middle-income 
class  in  the  Philippines,  with  particular  attention  given  to  discussion  on  household  vulnerability  to 
income poverty in the context of the COVID-19 pandemic.

Understanding the middle-class is crucial for several reasons. First, a ‘middle-class consensus’2 
determines development success and is associated with better outcomes: higher income, faster growth, 
more  investment  in  human  capital  such  as  education  and  health,  infrastructure  accumulation,  better

economic  policies,  greater  political  stability,  peace  and  more  democracy  (Easterly  2001).  Birdsall 
(2010)  argued  that  middle-class  expansion  signals  underlying  economic  progress  based  on  wealth 
creation rather than redistribution.  Second, the middle-class represents a segment of society that bridges 
the  gap  between  the  poor  and  the  wealthy,  serving  as  a  stabilizing  force  in  economic  and  political 
systems. A large prospering middle-class fosters accountable governance by advocating for political 
stability  that  promotes  fair  competition,  supports  pro-market  reforms,  protects  private  property, 
encourages  private  investments,  and  contributes  to  taxes  that  finance  public  goods  (Birdsall  2016; 
Birdsall 2010; Birdsall, Graham and Pettinato 2000). In Asia, the growth of the middle-class has been 
associated  with  rising  concern  for  welfare  and  environmental  preservation  as  well  as  demands  for 
participatory  democracy  and  personal  freedoms  (Hughes  and  Woldekidan  1994).  More  importantly, 
some  middle-class  households face  vulnerability  of  falling into  poverty  brought  about  by shocks  or 
aggregate  economic  contractions  (Lopez-Calva  and  Ortiz-Juarez  2011;  Ravallion  2009).  In  Latin 
America,  non-poor  vulnerable  households  have  limited  financial  buffers,  with  the  social  protection 
benefits they receive being offset by indirect taxes they face (Birdsall, Lustig and Meyer 2014). In the 
same  vein,  Stampini  et  al  (2016)  find  that  in  Latin  America  the  middle-class  remains  exposed  to  a 
substantial  risk  to  poverty,  underscoring  the  relevance  of  social  safety  nets  for  many  non-poor 
households. This vulnerability has important implications for the social contract: the state must earn 
and  maintain  the  trust  of  the  middle-class  through  effective  public  services  and  adequate  social 
protection.

The Philippines presents a unique case as it pursues upper-middle income status already attained 
by  neighboring  Southeast  Asian  economies  such  as  Malaysia,  Thailand  and  recently  Indonesia.  Its 
archipelagic  geography  creates  diverse  regional  economies  with  uneven  development.  Economic 
concentration in the National Capital Region, adjacent regions and established centers has created stark 
disparities  in  income  and  opportunities.  Moreover,  vulnerability  to  natural  disasters  in  particular 
typhoons and floods worsened by climate change threatens household welfare and economic stability.

In the recent study of the PIDS concerning the middle-class, Albert et al (2024) suggests that 
growing the middle-class is more closely related to new wealth creation rather than typically associated 
themes  such  as  poverty  reduction  or  redistribution.  Albert  et  al.  (2024)  proposed  expanding  the 
Philippine middle-class as a driver of inclusive growth through four pillars: promoting social justice in 
resource  management,  enabling  MSME  opportunities,  ensuring  workforce  readiness  with  social 
protection, and improving digital governance. The middle-income class grew from less than a third of 
the population in 1991 to around forty percent in 2021. This growth has not been linear, with the impact 
of the COVID-19 pandemic reversing some gains made in poverty reduction in the recent years. Albert 
et  al  (2024)  argue  that  transforming  the  Philippines  into  a  middle-class  society  necessitates 
comprehensive  initiatives  on  priority  areas,  and  success  depends  on  effective  leadership  and 
cooperation  involving  government,  private  sector,  civil  society  organizations  and  development 
stakeholders.

Addressing poverty goes beyond identifying the poor, who are the households with inadequate 
income  appropriate  to  an  acceptable  living  standard.  While  the  traditional  measures  of  poverty  are 
important  statistics,  these  are  static  and  does  not  characterize  much  about  a  household’s  economic 
security nor vulnerability of falling back to poverty in the presence of non-trivial risks. As these poverty 
measures  begin  to  improve  or  reduce  further  towards  the  path  to  upper-middle  income  domain, 
responsive  policymaking  would  need  to  adapt  to  characterize  as  well  as  anticipate  measuring  the 
resilience of households against risks that threaten their economic security. Environmental risks such 
as climate impacts of frequent floodings brought about by unpredictable weather due to climate change 
as  well  as  heat  waves  or  hotter  temperatures  are  increasingly  becoming  more  frequent,  and  these

In this paper we provide an updated profile and characterization of the middle-income class in 
the  Philippines  and  examine  implications  for  social  protection  as  the  country  transitions  into  a 
predominantly middle-income society. Using Philippine household income and expenditure survey and 
labor  force  survey  from  2018,  2021  and  2023,  we  analyze  the  size,  characteristics  and  economic 
vulnerability of different income classifications and regional groups, identify factors that contribute to 
downward mobility risks, as  well as  protective factors that build household resilience. We find that 
while the middle-income class has indeed expanded and recovered after the pandemic, many households 
remain  vulnerable  yet  lack  adequate  social  protection  access.  These  findings  have  important 
implications to the motivation and design of inclusive social protection systems that support sustained 
middle-income class development.

2.  Aggregate Poverty Profile and Regional Growth

In the recent two decades poverty incidence in the Philippines declined from 24.9 percent in 2003 
to  15.5  percent  in  2023,  a  9.4  percentage  point  reduction  representing  long-term  poverty  reduction 
(Table 1). All the three indices of poverty, from the head count, poverty gap and distribution-sensitive 
measure show consistent improvement during the two decades. The sharpest decline in poverty occurred 
pre-pandemic from the years 2015 to 2018, where poverty dropped dramatically from 23.5 percent in 
2015 to 16.7 percent in 2018, which is a 6.8 percent point reduction in three years, the largest single-
period  decline  within  the  two  decades  and  represents  the  Philippines’s  strongest  poverty  reduction 
performance. A sudden reversal of the post-pandemic trend and increase of poverty head count from 
16.7 percent in 2018 to 18.1 percent in 2021 erased the recent gains from 2015 to 2018, materializing 
the risk to downward mobility due to aggregate shocks brought about by the COVID-19 pandemic. 
Recovery  after  the  pandemic  where  poverty  head  count  fell to  15.5  percent in  2023  below the  pre-
pandemic level suggests resilience but also raises questions about which households, groups and regions 
genuinely recovered and those who remained vulnerable. Within the two decades, the poverty gap3 (or 
the minimum cost of eliminating poverty4) declined faster in relative terms than the head count, from 
5.6 percent in 2003 to 2.2 percent in 2023, while the distribution-sensitive measure dropped the most 
dramatically, from 2.2 percent to 0.7 percent. This implies that extreme poverty is declining faster than 
overall poverty.

urban share jumping from 28.4 percent in 2018 to 32.5 percent in 2021 and continuing to rise post-
pandemic in 2023. The same pattern holds for the indices for depth and severity, which suggests that 
the urban poor is not only increasing in number but also in depth of poverty. The rise of the share of the 
urban sector to poverty warrants further investigation.

Regional disparities in poverty are stark. In 2023, the National Capital Region accounted for 12.6 
percent of the population but contributed only 1.8 percent of the poor (Table 3). Established centers

in BARMM (32.4 percent), Western Mindanao (31.7 percent), Negros Island Region (28.9 percent) and 
Bicol Region (27.5 percent). Regions that contribute disproportionately to poverty depth and severity

Mindanao contributes 37.7 percent of the total poor despite comprising only 23.9 percent of the 
population, while Visayas contributes 26.0 percent versus 18.8 percent of the population. In contrast, 
Luzon contributes 36.4 percent of total poverty while representing 57.2 percent of the population. These 
spatial patterns reflect persistent disadvantages in regions distant from economic centers, particularly 
island regions, those with limited infrastructure development and connectivity, and conflict-affected 
regions, which have been excluded from the benefits of national growth.

Has growth been pro-poor lately? Growth elasticity of poverty in the Philippines has changed 
considerably over time. Earlier estimates show relatively weak pro-poor growth: Balisacan and Fuwa 
(2004)  estimated  an  elasticity  of  1.6  from  1988  to  1997,  which  appears  weak  in  comparison  to  an 
international standard of 2.5 based on Ravallion’s (2001) sample of developing countries in the 1980s 
and  1990s.  Reyes  and  Tabuga (2011) found  similarly  inelastic responses for  2003  to  2009.  Poverty 
reduction has become more responsive to economic growth after the global financial crisis in 2009. In 
the recent country report, World Bank (2025) attributed the pro-poor growth post-2010 to faster growth 
of real incomes of the bottom 40 percent compared to those from the wealthiest 20 percent, while also 
driven by a shift to wage jobs in services from self-employment in agriculture, creation of formal jobs, 
as  well  as  social  protection.  The  Bank also  found  spatial  convergence  post-2010,  wherein  low-  and 
medium-income regions drove the economic growth.

3.  Profile of the Middle-Income Class

The ‘middle-class’ is widely discussed in the social sciences and has different definitions across 
disciplines. We can either define the middle-class as those households with per capital income within 
an absolute threshold valued at purchasing power parity, or use a relative measure based on the income 
distribution in a country. An earlier work of Thurow (1987) and subsequently Birdsall, Graham and 
Pettinato (2000) defines the middle-class as those earning between 75 percent to 125 percent of the 
median household per capita income, while Easterly (2001) defines the middle-class as those within the 
expenditure quintiles two to four. Milanovic and Yitzhaki (2002) defined the global middle-class as 
those earning a per capita income of $12 to $50 per day. Banerjee and Duflo (2008) used daily per 
capita  expenditures  between  $2  to  $10  valued  at  purchasing  power  parity,  while  Ravallion  (2010) 
ranged between $2 to $13. The Asian Development Bank (2010) adopted a broader range of $2 to $20 
per capita daily expenditure valued in 2005 PPP $.

For the purpose of this paper, we adopt Albert et al.’s (2018a; 2018b) poverty-line based approach 
defining the middle-income households in the Philippines as those with per capita income between two 
and twelve times the official poverty line. In the Philippines, the measure of welfare or living standards 
is  per  capita  current  income  rather  than  consumption.  The  poverty  line  is  defined  as  the  minimum 
income deemed necessary for a household to meet basic needs, food and non-food, and most importantly 
to obtain adequate nutrition. This approach ensures the classification is consistent with official poverty 
statistics and policy-relevant, in particular compatible with existing social protection measures.

Table 8 presents the distribution of Filipino households by income and reveals the materialization 
of  downward mobility  risk  during  the  COVID-19  pandemic.  Subsistence  poverty  dropped  from  3.4 
percent in 2018 to 2.7 percent in 2023 nationally. Even during the COVID-19 spike in 2021, subsistence 
poverty was only at 3.9 percent.

The middle-income class is growing but remains vulnerable. The pandemic induced downward 
mobility, with middle- to high-income households dropping from 52.8 percent in 2018 to 49.1 percent 
in  2021  but  recovered  to  51.3  percent  by  2023.  While  poverty  declined  to  10.9  percent  among

4.  Literature Review

A foundational theoretical paper by Ravallion (1988) distinguished between permanent income 
from its variable component arising from risk and developed a framework for decomposing poverty 
into persistent and transient components to understand the poverty cost of income variability in rural 
India. Morduch (1994) formalized the distinction between chronic or structural poverty and vulnerable 
or stochastic poverty. Stochastic poverty in low-income countries is when a household’s consumption 
varies a good deal over time and may fall below the poverty line even though its permanent income is 
above  the  poverty  threshold.  This  is  due  to  income  shocks,  insufficient  insurance  mechanisms  and 
limited  access  to  credit  and  savings  to  smooth  consumption  (Morduch  1994).  Many  researchers 
contributed to the literature differentiating chronic from transient poverty for the purposes of clarifying 
what poverty alleviation programs and social protection are needed by different socioeconomic groups. 
A study by Jalan and Ravallion (2000) tested the determinants of chronic and transient poverty in rural 
China,  and  found  that  wealth  holdings,  physical  capital  and  life  cycle  matters  both  for  chronic  and 
transient  poverty,  while  household  size,  education  level,  geographical  location  and  agricultural 
productivity  matters  for  chronic  poverty.  The  implication  of  this  is  that  programs  appropriate  for

15

---

<!-- PAGE 23 -->

reducing  poverty  does  not  address  transient  poverty,  which  may  better  be  addressed  by  policy 
instruments such as those that provide employment and livelihood opportunities like seasonal public 
work programs, credit schemes, savings, insurance and income stabilization mechanisms.

Understanding vulnerability and risks, in connection to poverty is crucial for several reasons. 
Dercon  (2001)  stated  that  vulnerability  and  risk  is  also  a  cause  of  destitution  and  not  just  another 
dimension of poverty. Material vulnerability caused by shocks may have long-lasting consequences to 
the welfare and human capital formation among households, especially the poor. When a household 
suddenly faces a shock without adequate savings, they may be forced to forego needed health spending 
or withdraw children from school, or even sell productive assets, which can lead to high welfare costs 
into  their  future.  In  agrarian  economies  in  India,  seasonal  fluctuations  that  result  to  unanticipated 
income shocks affects uninsured households who tend to sacrifice their children’s school attendance as 
a  form  of  self-insurance,  which  has  costly,  long-run  implications  for  the  household  (Jacoby  and 
Skoufias 1997). Dercon (2005) asserted that risk and vulnerability is an ignored aspect in the design of 
anti-poverty policies.

Ligon and Schechter (2003) viewed vulnerability as a measure of welfare loss associated with 
uncertainty  to  consumption,  associated  with  poverty  and  exposure  to  aggregate  shocks.  In  their 
Bulgarian  study,  poverty  and  aggregate  shocks  reduced  welfare  equally,  with  aggregate  shocks 
mattering  more  than  idiosyncratic  risks.  Calvo  and  Dercon  (2005;  2013)  formalized  vulnerability 
measurement through an axiomatic approach that distinguishes between vulnerability (forward-looking 
threat) from poverty (outcome).

The influential literature earlier cited that elaborated on the links between vulnerability, risks and 
poverty  concentrated  empirical  testing  in  agricultural  and  rural  economies  in  developing  country 
contexts,  which  are  characterized  with  shocks  such  as  the  seasonality  of  agricultural  production, 
climate, droughts and famine. In the early 2000s, the vulnerability to poverty context was also viewed 
in  the  lens  of  globalization  and  shocks  to  domestic  economies  brought  about  by  increasing 
internationalization, such as financial crises and structural reforms (Dercon 2001). The current decade 
saw the advent of the fourth industrial revolution, increasing automation and changes in the world of 
work,  such  as  the  introduction  of  gig  economy  and  increasing  importance  of  self-employment  and 
informal employment. Also, extreme poverty has fallen dramatically compared to a decade ago, with 
lower  poverty rates  now  observed  in  developing economies.  Given these  recent  developments,  new 
areas of research on vulnerability, poverty and emerging sources of risks can be explored to understand 
better  the  current  dynamics  of  vulnerability  and  poverty  and  to  inform  design  of  social  protection 
systems that are appropriate in this new context.

Vulnerability analysis extends beyond monitoring those currently in poverty to encompassing 
future poverty risks. Dercon (2001) defined vulnerability as the probability of significant welfare losses 
due to uncertain events. We also differentiate risks according to its effect: idiosyncratic if it only affects 
few individuals or households, and covariate if it affects an entire community or region. Dercon (2001) 
provided a framework for analyzing vulnerability to poverty, emphasizing the importance of household 
assets, incomes and capabilities as protective factors against vulnerability and poverty. The framework 
also acknowledges the potential risks associated with assets accumulation, incomes and capabilities. 
The framework highlights the importance of asset building and accumulation, not just of physical and 
financial assets, but also human capital in the form of adequate nutrition and health, as well as quality 
education that empowers households with skills and capabilities to be productive in the labor market.

Chaudhuri and Datt (2001) developed a methodology measuring vulnerability as the probability 
of a household finding itself income-poor in the next period and applied this in the Philippine context 
in the years 1997 to 1998. Chaudhuri, Jalan and Suryahadi (2002) used this approach in Indonesia using 
cross-sectional data, while Chaudhuri (2003) expanded the framework with illustrative examples from 
the  Philippines,  Indonesia  and  rural  south  and  southwestern  China  (1985-1990).  Albert  and  Ramos 
(2008) and later Albert and Vizmanos (2018) updated these estimates for the Philippines (2003, 2009 
and 2015), finding significant vulnerability across various groups and recommending forward-looking 
interventions that build the resilience of households to reduce future poverty.

Social protection systems play crucial roles in preventing downward mobility. Social protection 
programs  have  been  key  instruments  to  reduce  the  incidence  and  depth  of  poverty  in  developing 
economies. Barrientos and Hulme (2008) emphasized the importance of social protection not only in

managing globalization risks but also viewing it in a broader focus on basic needs and building lasting 
capabilities,  reflected  in  the  increasing  importance  of  programs  and  policies  that  complement  cash 
transfers with basic services, such as education and health, employment guarantees or asset building. In 
Southeast Asia, Cook and Pincus (2014) stated that common challenges in social protection include 
economic  risks  associated  with  financial  globalization,  rapid  urbanization,  high  levels  of  informal 
employment, rising dependency ratios and highly unequal gender division of labor. Social insurance 
programs  are  still  concentrated  on  the  formal  and  public  sectors,  supplemented  in  middle-income 
countries with voluntary, contributory programs for small businesses, farmers and the informal sector 
(Cook and Pincus 2014).

Despite its importance, some studies also find that social protection policies may crowd out or 
have negative welfare effects in communities, for instance, beneficiaries withdrawing from informal 
networks which may harm non-beneficiaries (Dercon 2001). In this way, actions to reduce risk impact 
or  exposure  to  it,  such  as  savings,  access  to  credit  and  insurance,  temporary  safety  nets  and  social 
protection  are  needed.  Community-based  risk  sharing  among  informal  networks,  friends  or  social 
capital are also considered emergency safety nets of people in times of unexpected crisis.

Safety  net  interventions  for  individuals  facing  temporary  economic  vulnerability  can  prevent 
many from becoming persistently impoverished, which would reduce the strain on programs targeting 
chronic poverty (Balisacan and Fuwa 2004; Barrientos and Hulme 2008). The Philippines' cornerstone 
social protection program, the Pantawid Pamilyang Pilipino Program (4Ps) primarily target the poorest 
segments,  leaving  low  income  but  non-poor,  as  well  as  the  middle-class  households  with  limited 
protection against economic shocks, as highlighted during the COVID-19 pandemic.

5.  Methodology

to income poverty by measuring its expected income and the volatility (variance) of its income streams. 
While this approach ideally uses longitudinal data wherein the same households are observed over a 
certain  period,  data  limitations  restrict  us  to  use  the  available  cross-sectional  data  from  the  Family

(2003),  we  assume  the  variation  of  income  is  due  to  observable  household  and  community 
characteristics as well as stochastic (random) factors6, for example, events unique to a household such 
as disasters or weather shocks, illness, job loss, or significant price fluctuations.

6.  Data

We utilize data from the merged Family Income and Expenditure Survey and Labor Force Survey 
(FIES-LFS) conducted by the Philippine Statistics Authority (PSA) for the years 2018, 2021, and 2023. 
The  merged  FIES-LFS  datasets  provide  comprehensive  information  on  household  income, 
expenditures, employment and household attributes and living conditions, making it the primary source 
for analyzing middle-class dynamics in the Philippines. The FIES is also the survey used for generating 
poverty and income distribution statistics in the Philippines. The FIES is now conducted every two 
years. The 2018 FIES sampled 147,717 households from urban and rural areas of each province, while 
the 2021 round covered 165,029 households, and the 2023 round covered 163,268 households. The 
basic household income models are estimated using these data.

We supplement the household income models with data on infrastructure conditions of provinces 
and  highly  urbanized  cities,  obtained  from  the  2020 Census of  Population  and Housing  (CPH) also 
released by the PSA. The CPH provides the characteristics of the 42,046 barangays in the Philippines. 
We  construct  two  simple  infrastructure  indices  for  each  province  and  highly  urbanized  city:  a 
communications  infrastructure  index  derived  from  the  percentage  of  barangays  with  cellular  phone 
signal,  and  a  water  infrastructure  index  based  on  the  percentage  of  barangays  with  community 
waterworks  system.  These  location-based  covariates  capture  how  infrastructure  affects  household 
income levels and variance.

In  addition  to  these  we  also  obtained  permission  to  use  rainfall  data  from  the  Philippine

major socioeconomic groups.

7.  Interpretation of the Vulnerability Estimate

The  mean  vulnerability  level  within  the  group  should  in  the  absence  of  aggregate  shocks 
approximately equal the observed household poverty rate. While the choice of a vulnerability threshold 
is arbitrary, we follow the method of Chaudhuri and Datt (2001) in categorizing households according 
to their vulnerability risk. The high vulnerability threshold is 0.50, while the cutoff for vulnerable is the 
official poverty incidence during the year.

Amongst  the  vulnerable,  we  distinguish  between  the  relatively  vulnerable,  those  with  an 
estimated vulnerability level greater than the observed incidence of poverty but less than 0.50, and the 
highly vulnerable, those having an estimated vulnerability level greater than 0.50 (Table 16). In this 
manner we can compare group vulnerability rates side by side with poverty rates.

8.  Results and Discussion

8.1 Discussion of Vulnerability: Overall Picture

This  exercise  of  estimating  vulnerability  reveals  to  us  a  much  larger  at-risk  population  than 
observed poverty head count rates alone show. Table 20 illustrates that across the years 2018 to 2023, 
the national poverty rate among families is around 10.9 percent to 13.2 percent, while the vulnerability 
rate is 29.0 percent to 32.0 percent, suggesting on average that vulnerability is 2.5 times higher than 
current poverty. In simple terms many non-poor households still face significant risk of falling into 
income poverty. The vulnerability estimates support the often-stated claim that the poverty statistics 
underestimates the proportion of the population that is vulnerable to poverty. Indeed here we find that 
there are households whose probability of poverty may be high who are nevertheless observed to be 
non-poor, and conversely, there are those observed poor but assessed as non-vulnerable.

While poverty and vulnerability are closely related, we estimate that around 15.0 percent to 18.0 
percent  of  the  poor  are  not  vulnerable,  suggesting  transitory  poverty  for  this  group.  However,  a 
significant proportion of non-poor households faces risk, with 21.0 percent to 24.0 percent of the non-
poor assessed as vulnerable to falling into poverty. This represents a significant share often missed and 
overlooked by traditional poverty measures.

As with the previous study by Chaudhuri and Datt (2001) we also find the same observation that 
the vulnerable group is quite different to the currently observed poor population. We estimate that 2.0 
percent to 4.0 percent of the non-vulnerable are observed to be poor, and the bulk (65.0 percent to 69.0 
percent) of the vulnerable are non-poor. Of the 30.0 percent vulnerable population nationally in 2023, 
the relatively vulnerable comprise 24.0 percent while the highly vulnerable make up the remaining 6.0 
percent of the population. While both poverty rates and vulnerability rates increased during the COVID-
19 pandemic in 2021, partial recovery has been observed by 2023 (household poverty rate 12.1 percent, 
2018; 13.2 percent, 2021; 10.9 percent, 2023; vulnerability rate 29.0 percent, 2018; 32.0 percent, 2021; 
30.0 percent, 2023).

This has important policy implications: social protection should address and target not just the 
currently  poor  (10.9  percent  of  households)  but  also  aim  to  increase  the  financial  resilience  and 
capabilities  of  the  vulnerable  population  in  responding  to  economic  shocks.  In  this  sense  social 
protection  must  increase  overall  resilience  of  households  instead  of  just  providing  temporary  relief 
which is often for transitory poverty.

It is important to emphasize that vulnerability is conceptually distinct from observed poverty. 
While poverty is an ex post outcome measured at a point in time, vulnerability is an ex ante, forward-
looking measure of the risk of being poor in the future. As such, vulnerability is not expected to coincide 
perfectly with observed poverty. The existence of households that are non-poor but vulnerable reflects 
income  fragility  and  exposure  to  shocks,  while  the  presence  of  some  poor  but  non-vulnerable 
households reflects transitory poverty. This distinction is central to the policy relevance of vulnerability 
estimates, particularly in contexts where poverty is increasingly dynamic and driven by shocks rather 
than persistent deprivation.

8.2 Vulnerability and Poverty Profiles

The overall ratio of vulnerability incidence to poverty incidence is 2.75 in 2023 (Table 21). While 
poverty rates showed gradual decrease, the vulnerability-to-poverty ratio increased from 2.40 in 2018 
to 2.75 in 2023, suggesting that there is an increase to households at risk of falling into poverty even as

22

---

<!-- PAGE 30 -->

current  poverty  rates  are  decreasing.  The  vulnerability  to  poverty  ratio  differs  across  categories  of 
households according to urban location, regions, highest education and sector of employment of the 
household head.

Importantly, the ordering of vulnerability across regions, urban-rural areas, and socioeconomic 
groups closely mirrors the ordering of observed poverty, which provides an internal validity check on 
the  vulnerability  estimates.  At  the  same  time,  the  magnitudes  differ  systematically,  reflecting  the 
forward-looking and risk-based nature of vulnerability as distinct from realized poverty.

Rural  areas  face  more  than  double  the  poverty  rate  of  urban  areas  (16.0  percent  of  rural 
households versus 7.0 percent of urban in 2023). Rural vulnerability also remains alarmingly high at 
43.0 percent versus 20.0 percent urban areas in 2023. This gap remains relatively consistent across all 
three years. Among the regions, the National Capital Region has the highest vulnerability to poverty 
ratio (7.90) when compared to the national average. While, it has the lowest poverty rate (1.1 percent), 
the vulnerability rate is 9.0 percent. This pattern is not anomalous. Rather, it reflects that while average 
incomes in the NCR are high and poverty incidence is low, many households remain exposed to labor 
market volatility, informal and contractual employment, high living costs, and macroeconomic shocks. 
In such contexts, vulnerability captures economic insecurity rather than deprivation per se, and thus 
remains policy-relevant even where poverty is low. In other words, a lot of Metro Manila non-poor are 
at  risk  of  downward  mobility,  and  that  poverty  may  be  underestimated  even  among  the  relatively 
middle-income regions.

Across  all  regions,  rural  areas  consistently  face  higher  vulnerability  rates  compared  to  urban 
areas: Northern and Central Luzon: 29.0 percent vulnerable among rural versus 15.0 percent urban; 
Southern Luzon 40.0 percent rural versus 22.0 percent urban; Western and Central Visayas 37.0 percent 
rural versus 25.0 percent urban; Eastern Visayas 53.0 percent rural versus 32.0 percent urban; Mindanao 
(excluding BARMM) 55.0 percent rural versus 27.0 percent urban; and BARMM 76.0 percent rural 
versus 55.0 percent urban. It is also worth noting that aside from the high gaps of poverty incidence 
between rural and urban areas, the same can be said of vulnerability rates.

We find more pronounced differences for the profile of the highly vulnerable (households with 
greater  than  50.0  percent  probability  of  being  poor).  In  2023,  rural  Mindanao  including  BARMM 
accounts for a quarter of the poor households (25.0 percent), but it accounts for more than a third (35.0 
percent)  of the  highly  vulnerable.  BARMM also  shows  extreme  vulnerability:  76.0  percent  of  rural 
BARMM population is vulnerable in 2023, with 23.0 percent poor.

Similarly, households whose heads have less than primary or with primary education account for 
57.0 percent of the poor, but account for 77.0 percent of the highly vulnerable. These differences have 
implications for targeting of anti-poverty policies. In line with this, we also find that education is a key 
protective  factor  against  poverty  and  vulnerability.  Households  headed  by  individuals  with  tertiary 
education are less vulnerable and less likely to be observed poor, with vulnerability rate at 5.0 percent 
and poverty rate at 2.0 percent.

Agriculture  remains  the  highest-risk  sector,  with  25.0  percent  poverty  rate  and  60.0  percent

percent  of  the  vulnerable,  and  61.0  percent  of  the  highly  vulnerable.  Industry,  manufacturing  and

8.3 Sources of Vulnerability

Households with the same level of vulnerability may be vulnerable for different reasons: some 
households  have  relatively  lower  income  to  begin  with,  while  others  have  higher  volatility  or 
uncertainty of income. Recognizing the sources of vulnerability among key groups of interest may aid 
policymakers and program planners towards an efficient and equitable manner of allocating resources 
for social protection. In terms of policy intervention, households with low endowments and thus lower 
income,  which  are  likely  the  poor  households,  will  benefit  more  from  a  conditional  cash  transfer 
program like the Pantawid Pamilyang Pilipino Program (4Ps Program), where households are provided 
with cash conditional to their children’s school attendance and consistent health check-ups to ensure 
that  they  complete  basic  education  and  develop  good  health  and  nutrition  habits,  with  the  aim  of 
breaking the intergenerational cycle of poverty among families. Households who are vulnerable due to 
high uncertainty of income streams may be better helped with policies that reduce the risks that they 
face or insure them against such risks.

Following  Chaudhuri  and  Datt  (2001)  we  adopt  a  classification  of  households  by  sources 
vulnerability. The first group are the non-vulnerable, defined as those with estimated vulnerability level 
below the poverty rate (in 2023, 10.9 percent), who will have estimated levels of mean income well 
above the poverty line. The second group are those with relatively volatile income streams: labeled 
high-volatility-income vulnerable, whose estimated vulnerability level is above the poverty rate, but the 
estimated mean income is above the poverty line. The third group are the households with relatively 
low endowments to begin with: labeled as low-mean-income vulnerable, whose mean levels of income 
fall below the poverty line and whose vulnerability level or probability of finding themselves poor in 
the period is above 50.0 percent.

Figure 3 plots the estimated mean income against the estimated standard deviation of income for 
all households observed to be vulnerable in 2023. The scatterplot shows that households at the same 
level of vulnerability can be vulnerable for different reasons. For example, well-off households with 
relatively higher incomes may be vulnerable if there is their income streams fluctuate more compared 
to households with stable employment and income sources. Households with relatively low income to 
begin  with  are  vulnerable  due  to  this  reason  regardless  of  the  variability  of  their  income  sources, 
however an increase in the uncertainty of their income sources may leave them highly vulnerable.

percent).  The  same  is  also  observed  among  households  with  heads  having  tertiary  or  secondary

of the vulnerable are due to high volatility of income.

The  contrast  among  the  importance  of  sources  of  vulnerability  emerges  among  the  high-
vulnerable population. At least 70.0 percent of the highly vulnerable in rural areas are due to low-mean 
incomes, while the urban highly vulnerable are relatively more diverse: around 28.0 percent to 45.0 
percent  are  due  to  high-volatility  of  income.    A  sizeable  share  (75.0  to  79.0  percent)  of  the  highly 
vulnerable  amongst  households  with  heads  that  are  either  primary-educated  or  lesser  are  low-mean 
income vulnerable, while 63% of highly vulnerable households with tertiary-educated heads are high-
volatility of income vulnerable.

Provinces  with  low  levels  of  infrastructure  have  higher  vulnerable  rates.  Provinces  with  low 
index of communications infrastructure have dramatically high vulnerability rate at 68.0 percent, versus 
29.0 percent vulnerable in high-infrastructure provinces. For provinces with low index of waterworks 
systems, the incidence of vulnerability is 39.0 percent versus 29.0 percent in high-infrastructure areas.

Regardless of the infrastructure conditions, the sources of vulnerability remain remarkably constant for 
both vulnerable and highly vulnerable households. For vulnerable households, high-volatility of income 
is the dominant source of vulnerability, around 74.0 to 87.0 percent, in contrast amongst the highly 
vulnerable group, where low-mean incomes are the major source of vulnerability at 72.0 to 74.0 percent.

Vulnerability  extends  well  beyond current  poverty:  44.0  percent  of the  low  income  (but  non-
poor) households, and even 11.0 percent of the lower middle-income households face significant risks 
of vulnerability to poverty, primarily from income volatility. Social protection must expand beyond the 
poorest to include these vulnerable populations. While conditional cash transfers like the 4Ps address 
chronic poverty, vulnerable non-poor households need income stabilization support such as insurance, 
disaster protection, health coverage and asset-building programs. This calls for differentiated policy 
responses that proactively builds and strengthens the resilience of households, alongside current poverty 
alleviation programs.

9.  Policy Implications

Social protection must expand beyond reactive poverty relief to a broader focus on proactive resilience 
building, as vulnerability incidence exceeds current poverty, with nearly a quarter of non-poor households at 
significant risk to downward mobility. Social protection framework and programs must be designed to prevent 
downward mobility and not just address current destitution. Differentiated policy interventions must address 
the needs of groups, primarily based on their sources of vulnerability. For the households experiencing unstable 
incomes  or  high  volatility  of  income,  which comprise  86.0  percent  of  the  vulnerable, income  stabilization 
mechanisms such as unemployment insurance, health insurance coverage, as well as enhanced access to credit 
and  savings  instruments  promotes  resilience  and  asset  building.  Complementary  programs  such  as  public 
works  or  cash-for-work  programs,  business  continuity  support  for  micro,  small  and  medium  enterprises 
(MSMEs)  and  emergency  cash  assistance  during  shocks  or  disasters  may  also  help  these  households  and 
prevent them from downward mobility to poverty.

Amongst the low-mean income vulnerable, long-term human capital investments must be prioritized 
through continuation of their participation in the 4Ps conditional cash transfers which must include proper 
targeting  of  the  poorest  and  those  with  the  lowest  endowments.  These  can  be  complemented  with  skills 
upgrading, livelihood programs and asset building initiatives to prepare them to be financially independent 
after graduation of support. The growing importance of the middle-income class, as well as the equity focus 
on lifting the poorest out of destitution must mean that social protection encompasses and address the needs of 
these various socioeconomic segments of society. Fiscal prudence as well as equity in targeting must also be 
emphasized to keep the administrative cost of social protection low and efficient, reaching the actual intended 
groups who need these interventions the most.

9.1 Social Protection

As  the  Philippines transitions  toward upper-middle  income  status, the nature  of  poverty  is  changing 
from  predominantly  chronic  to  increasingly  dynamic  and  shock-driven.  This  implies  that  social  protection 
systems must evolve accordingly — from a primary focus on alleviating existing poverty toward a broader role 
in preventing future poverty by protecting households against risks and downward mobility.  Poverty itself is 
not static, thus interventions cannot be based solely on static lists of poor households.  Further, the middle 
class, especially the lower middle class, are also in need of social protection.

Middle-income households represent 51.3 percent of the population but have minimal social protection 
despite contributing significantly to the economy. In the Philippines, only about a third (34.9 percent) of the 
population is covered by at least one social protection benefit excluding health, in comparison to higher social 
protection  effective  coverage  rate  in  some  Southeast  Asian  countries  such  as  Singapore  (100.0  percent), 
Thailand (70.1 percent), and even Indonesia (54.3 percent) and Viet Nam (38.3 percent) as illustrated in Table 
30.  Among  upper-middle  income  countries,  the  effective  social  protection  effective  coverage  rate  is  71.2 
percent (ILO 2024).

10. Policy Recommendations

Strengthening Social Protection Systems

The vulnerability analysis shows that economic insecurity in the Philippines extends well beyond the 
currently poor. A substantial share of households above the poverty line face a high probability of falling into 
poverty due to income volatility, health shocks, labor market instability, and climate-related risks. This implies 
that  a  social  protection  system  focused  narrowly  on  the  poorest  is  no  longer  sufficient  in  a  context  where 
poverty is increasingly dynamic and shock-driven. As the Philippines approaches upper-middle income status, 
social protection must therefore evolve from a primarily poverty-alleviation instrument into a broader system 
for preventing downward mobility and strengthening household resilience.

This shift supports a gradual transition toward universal social protection — understood not as universal 
transfers,  but  as  universal  access  to  risk  protection  mechanisms,  including  health  insurance,  income  and 
employment protection, disaster and climate risk protection, and old-age security. Several priorities follow 
directly from the vulnerability findings:

1.  Expand protection to vulnerable non-poor households, especially the lower-income, and lower 
middle-income class: Extend social protection coverage beyond the poor to include low income but 
not  poor  and  lower  middle  income  housholds  identified  as  vulnerable  due  to  income  volatity  and 
exposure  to  economic  shocks,  through  instruments  sic  as  unemployment  insurance  or  temporary 
income support, and contributory social insurance programs.

2.  Reorient programs toward building resilience: Complement consumption-support programs with 
interventions that build long-term resilience, including support for skills, stable employment, savings, 
insurance, and productive asset accumulation rather than just providing temporary relief.

3.  Improve identification of vulnerable households: Strengthen targeting and information systems to 
better identify households at risk of falling into poverty, reduce exclusion and inclusion errors, and 
enable timely, preventive interventions rather than purely reactive responses.

4.  Integrate disaster and climate risk into social protection: Mainstream disaster risk management 
and  climate  adaptation  into  social  protection  systems  to  protect  household  assets  and  enable  rapid 
recovery following shocks such as natural disasters.

Promoting Regional Development

The vulnerability profiles reveal strong spatial concentration of risk, particularly in rural and lagging 
regions  where  households  face  systematically  higher  vulnerability  even  when  they  are  not  currently  poor. 
These patterns reflect unequal access to infrastructure, markets, services, and productive opportunities, which

of doing business and improve access to markets and services.

2.  Promote regional economic diversification: Support the development of productive sectors outside 
the  traditional  economic  centers  through  targeted  investments,  industrial  policy  complemented  by 
servicification, and support for regional value chains that create stable employment and new sources 
of wealth.

3.  Strengthen human capital in lagging regions: Improve the quality and accessibility of education and 
health services in lagging regions to raise productivity and reduce intergenerational transmission of 
vulnerability.

4.  Enhance local institutional capacity: Build the capacity of local governments to plan, implement and 
monitor development programs effectively and to manage public resources in ways that respond to 
local vulnerability profiles.

Improving Labor Market Outcomes

The  analysis indicates  that  vulnerability  is  closely linked  to  the  quality and  stability of employment 
rather than income levels alone. Households dependent on informal, seasonal, or low-productivity employment 
face significantly higher vulnerability, even when their current incomes place them above the poverty line. 
Strengthening  labor  market  outcomes  is  therefore  central  to  reducing  vulnerability  and  enabling  sustained 
upward mobility. Suggested policy priorities include:

1.  Expand formal and stable employment: Encourage formal job creation through regulatory reforms, 
simplified  business  registration  and  incentives  for  firms  to  formalize  and  invest  in  labor-intensive 
productive activities.

2.  Improve income security in the labor market: Strengthen labor institutions and protections to reduce

income volatility and employment insecurity while maintaining flexibility for firms.

3.  Invest  in  skills  aligned  with  structural  transformation:  Expand  workforce  development  and 
lifelong learning systems to equip workers with skills needed for higher-productivity sectors, including 
digital and green industries.

4.  Support  entrepreneurship  and  MSMEs:  Improve  access  to  finance,  training,  technology  and 
markets for micro, small and medium enterprises to foster job creation and income diversification, 
particularly in rural areas.

Addressing Vulnerability Factors

Beyond income and employment, vulnerability is driven by exposure to shocks, limited access to risk 
management  tools,  and  constraints  on  asset  accumulation.  Health  risks,  disasters,  financial  exclusion,  and 
insecure property and livelihood assets play a central role in pushing households into poverty and preventing

recovery. Reducing vulnerability therefore requires targeted interventions that address these underlying risk 
channels.

To reduce household vulnerability to poverty, the study recommends:

1.  Expand access to financial risk management tools: Increase access to savings, credit, and insurance 
to enable households to smooth consumption, manage shocks, and invest in productive activities.

2.  Strengthen health systems and financial protection: Improve the quality, accessibility and financial 
coverage  of  health  services  to  reduce  catastrophic  health  expenditures  and  health-related  income 
losses.

3.  Enhance disaster preparedness and response systems: Invest in early warning, preparedness, and

post-disaster recovery mechanisms that protect lives, assets and livelihoods.

4.  Support asset building and security: Promote policies that strengthen households asset ownership 
and security, including  land tenure, housing, and business capital, particularly for vulnerable and near-
poor households.

11. Conclusion

The analysis of middle-class development and vulnerability to income poverty in the Philippines reveals 
a  complex  picture  of  progress and  persistent  challenges.  While the  country  has made  significant  strides in 
reducing extreme poverty and in expanding the middle-income class over the past three decades, the COVID-
19 pandemic highlighted the fragility of these gains and the vulnerability of many households to shocks. The 
vision  of  a  predominantly  middle-class  Philippines  by  2040  remains  achievable  but  requires  sustained 
commitment to policies that promote inclusive growth, reduce vulnerability and build resilience. The insights 
presented in this study provides a foundation for evidence-based policy making that can help realize this vision 
while ensuring that the benefits of development are shared broadly across the population.

The recovery of the share of the middle-income class to 41.3 percent by 2023 demonstrates resilience 
and  the  effectiveness  of  policy  responses.  However,  the  recovery  has  not  yet  returned  to  pre-pandemic 
baselines which indicates persistent vulnerabilities that require policy attention to build sustainable middle-
class growth. The persistent urban-rural divide in middle-class development reflects deeper structural issues 
in the Philippine economy. Rural areas continue to lag in economic opportunities, infrastructure, and access to 
services, limiting the potential for middle-class growth outside urban centers. The concentration of middle-
income  households  in  specific  regions  particularly  in  NCR,  CAR,  and  CALABARZON  and  the  persistent 
urban-rural divide point to the importance of geographic factors in determining economic opportunities, and 
the need for more inclusive development strategies. This concentration, while beneficial for regional economic 
growth, may exacerbate inequality and limit long-term growth potential by underutilizing human and natural 
resources in other regions. The policy challenge lies in expanding middle-class opportunities while maintaining 
the economic dynamism of leading regions.

The vulnerability analysis reveals that many Filipino households, including those currently classified as 
middle-income, face significant risks of falling into poverty. The vulnerability rates among low-income but 
non-poor and lower middle-income households suggest that recent gains may seem fragile and be susceptible 
to  reversal  during  economic  downturns.  The  factors  associated  with  vulnerability  point to  potential  policy 
interventions. Education emerges as a crucial factor, suggesting that investments in human capital can reduce

household  vulnerability.  Employment  stability  is  equally  important,  indicating  the  need  for  policies  that 
promote formal employment and job security.

The social protection system has made important contributions to poverty reduction, particularly through 
the  4Ps  program,  which  have  allowed  households  to  build  resilience  and  invest  in  human  capital  through 
education and health and thereby break the intergenerational cycle of poverty among families. The analysis 
reveals significant gaps in social protection coverage. Existing social protection programs, especially the 4Ps 
primarily targets the poorest segments of the population, leaving the non-poor low-income and middle-class 
households exposed to economic shocks. The COVID-19 pandemic highlighted these gaps, as many middle-
class families experienced significant hardship but were ineligible for 4Ps assistance, while they were provided 
support through different means such as “ayuda”.

The vulnerability assessment defines the probability of a household finding itself income-poor but does 
not capture its long-term economic prospects. The 4Ps program is intended to lift households out of future 
poverty  by  investing  in  children’s  present  education  and  health  through  conditional  cash  transfers.  It  is 
important for low-income households to build the assets and capabilities necessary for resilience and sustained 
household upward mobility, and the 4Ps program through programmatic improvements can be instrumental in 
achieving this.

The Philippines is soon to breach upper middle-income status. With this, the poverty thresholds will 
have to be significantly adjusted upward. The Philippines still has a considerable share of its population who 
remain vulnerable, deemed ‘poor’, at 58.7 percent, if assessed at upper-middle income country standards of 
the World Bank, in which the poverty threshold is $8.30 a day. This means that we should be rethinking our 
views of social protection to cover not just the poor, but the near poor, and even up to the lower middle class, 
and progressively work toward universal social protection.

The findings in this study underscore that poverty in the Philippines is increasingly dynamic rather than 
static. Preventing future poverty by strengthening household resilience is therefore as important as reducing 
current poverty. Vulnerability analysis provides a necessary complement to traditional poverty measurement 
in guiding this transition.

Abstract
The Philippines aspires to become a predominantly middle-class society by 2040. Significant
strides have been achieved in the reduction of extreme poverty. However, the COVID-19
pandemic exposed the fragility of recent gains and the vulnerability of many households to
economic shocks. This study examines the dynamics and characteristics of the middle-income
class and analyzes household vulnerability to income poverty using the Family Income and
Expenditure Survey and Labor Force Survey data from 2018, 2021 and 2023 using the
methodology developed by Chaudhuri and Datt (2001).
Traditional poverty measures underestimate the at-risk population. Vulnerability affects 30.0
percent of Filipino households, 2.75 times higher than the household poverty incidence of 10.9
percent in 2023. Households face vulnerability for distinctly different reasons. Eighty-six
percent of vulnerable families experience income volatility, while seventy-three percent of the
highly vulnerable have persistently low incomes. Stark rural-urban disparities also persist, with
rural vulnerability incidence at 43.0 percent in contrast with 20.0 percent in urban areas.
Regional variations of vulnerability range from 9.0 percent in the National Capital Region to
76.0 percent in rural BARMM.
These findings have significant implications for the motivation and design of social protection
systems in the country. Social protection must evolve from reactive poverty relief to a broader
and proactive focus on resilience building. Differentiated interventions must be based on needs
of specific segments: insurance and income stabilization mechanisms for the vulnerable
majority who experience income volatility, and poverty reduction programs targeted for the
low-income vulnerable. Infrastructure development, education, sectoral transition from
agriculture to the highly productive sectors in services, industry and manufacturing, as well as
climate risk management emerge as critical protective factors. Achieving the 2040 vision
requires bold policy reforms that expand social protection to universal coverage aligned with
upper middle-income country standards, strengthen household resilience, and address the
structural factors that perpetuate vulnerability across sectors.

1. Introduction
The aspiration of the Philippines to become a predominantly middle-class society by 2040 is one
of the most ambitious socioeconomic goals in its development agenda. This vision embodies not merely
an economic target but a fundamental transformation toward a more equitable society where prosperity
is shared broadly across the population. The country is poised to become an upper middle-income
economy in the next few years and aims to reach high-income status by 2045, with improved quality of
life and better access to services for all citizens. This foremost attention to economic development that
brings about poverty reduction is reflected in the Ambisyon Natin 2040 vision (NEDA 2016), as well
as in the Philippine Development Plan. The middle-income class serves as the backbone of sustainable
economic growth, providing the consumer demand necessary to drive domestic markets while
contributing to social stability and better democratic governance.
While the pace of progress in poverty reduction over the past decade has been steady, the more
important question concerns its equity aspect: how pro-poor the economic development has been. For
growth to translate equitably to poverty reduction, the middle-income class must continue to expand
and gain an increasingly important representation. The increasing importance of the middle-income
class does not mean that policies and programs to expand or empower it are incompatible with those
that focus on poverty reduction and alleviation. As the Philippines advances towards upper-middle
income status, it is increasingly important to address the factors that make households vulnerable to
poverty, as well as provide adequate social protection in terms of education, health and in the labor
market. Equitable policies that seek to expand as well as empower the middle-income class also actually
help uplift the poor segment of society. For instance, pursuing macroeconomic stability and investing
in long-term human capital such as education and health benefits everyone. Promoting policies that
increase the labor productivity of workers and upgrade their skills, as well as connect them to markets
and employment opportunities also benefit the majority.
The coronavirus (COVID-19) pandemic has altered the trajectory of middle-class development
in the Philippines, revealing both the resilience and vulnerability of households that had recently
achieved middle-income status (Albert et al. 2024). The economic disruptions brought about by the
pandemic exposed the fragility of middle-class gains, with many households experiencing downward
mobility despite previous progress. This study examines the evolving dynamics of the middle-income
class in the Philippines, with particular attention given to discussion on household vulnerability to
income poverty in the context of the COVID-19 pandemic.
Understanding the middle-class is crucial for several reasons. First, a ‘middle-class consensus’2
determines development success and is associated with better outcomes: higher income, faster growth,
more investment in human capital such as education and health, infrastructure accumulation, better

economic policies, greater political stability, peace and more democracy (Easterly 2001). Birdsall
(2010) argued that middle-class expansion signals underlying economic progress based on wealth
creation rather than redistribution. Second, the middle-class represents a segment of society that bridges
the gap between the poor and the wealthy, serving as a stabilizing force in economic and political
systems. A large prospering middle-class fosters accountable governance by advocating for political
stability that promotes fair competition, supports pro-market reforms, protects private property,
encourages private investments, and contributes to taxes that finance public goods (Birdsall 2016;
Birdsall 2010; Birdsall, Graham and Pettinato 2000). In Asia, the growth of the middle-class has been
associated with rising concern for welfare and environmental preservation as well as demands for
participatory democracy and personal freedoms (Hughes and Woldekidan 1994). More importantly,
some middle-class households face vulnerability of falling into poverty brought about by shocks or
aggregate economic contractions (Lopez-Calva and Ortiz-Juarez 2011; Ravallion 2009). In Latin
America, non-poor vulnerable households have limited financial buffers, with the social protection
benefits they receive being offset by indirect taxes they face (Birdsall, Lustig and Meyer 2014). In the
same vein, Stampini et al (2016) find that in Latin America the middle-class remains exposed to a
substantial risk to poverty, underscoring the relevance of social safety nets for many non-poor
households. This vulnerability has important implications for the social contract: the state must earn
and maintain the trust of the middle-class through effective public services and adequate social
protection.
The Philippines presents a unique case as it pursues upper-middle income status already attained
by neighboring Southeast Asian economies such as Malaysia, Thailand and recently Indonesia. Its
archipelagic geography creates diverse regional economies with uneven development. Economic
concentration in the National Capital Region, adjacent regions and established centers has created stark
disparities in income and opportunities. Moreover, vulnerability to natural disasters in particular
typhoons and floods worsened by climate change threatens household welfare and economic stability.
In the recent study of the PIDS concerning the middle-class, Albert et al (2024) suggests that
growing the middle-class is more closely related to new wealth creation rather than typically associated
themes such as poverty reduction or redistribution. Albert et al. (2024) proposed expanding the
Philippine middle-class as a driver of inclusive growth through four pillars: promoting social justice in
resource management, enabling MSME opportunities, ensuring workforce readiness with social
protection, and improving digital governance. The middle-income class grew from less than a third of
the population in 1991 to around forty percent in 2021. This growth has not been linear, with the impact
of the COVID-19 pandemic reversing some gains made in poverty reduction in the recent years. Albert
et al (2024) argue that transforming the Philippines into a middle-class society necessitates
comprehensive initiatives on priority areas, and success depends on effective leadership and
cooperation involving government, private sector, civil society organizations and development
stakeholders.
Addressing poverty goes beyond identifying the poor, who are the households with inadequate
income appropriate to an acceptable living standard. While the traditional measures of poverty are
important statistics, these are static and does not characterize much about a household’s economic
security nor vulnerability of falling back to poverty in the presence of non-trivial risks. As these poverty
measures begin to improve or reduce further towards the path to upper-middle income domain,
responsive policymaking would need to adapt to characterize as well as anticipate measuring the
resilience of households against risks that threaten their economic security. Environmental risks such
as climate impacts of frequent floodings brought about by unpredictable weather due to climate change
as well as heat waves or hotter temperatures are increasingly becoming more frequent, and these
2

episodes affects most Filipino families regardless of income levels. External risks such as new
developments in the international trading systems such as the rise of protectionist stance among major
economies which affect trade and investments has impact on the businesses and employment
domestically. In the presence of these risks, government policy must adapt to protect and strengthen the
security and resilience of households. An important avenue by which to protect the resilience of
households is by an effective social protection system.
In this paper we provide an updated profile and characterization of the middle-income class in
the Philippines and examine implications for social protection as the country transitions into a
predominantly middle-income society. Using Philippine household income and expenditure survey and
labor force survey from 2018, 2021 and 2023, we analyze the size, characteristics and economic
vulnerability of different income classifications and regional groups, identify factors that contribute to
downward mobility risks, as well as protective factors that build household resilience. We find that
while the middle-income class has indeed expanded and recovered after the pandemic, many households
remain vulnerable yet lack adequate social protection access. These findings have important
implications to the motivation and design of inclusive social protection systems that support sustained
middle-income class development.
2. Aggregate Poverty Profile and Regional Growth
In the recent two decades poverty incidence in the Philippines declined from 24.9 percent in 2003
to 15.5 percent in 2023, a 9.4 percentage point reduction representing long-term poverty reduction
(Table 1). All the three indices of poverty, from the head count, poverty gap and distribution-sensitive
measure show consistent improvement during the two decades. The sharpest decline in poverty occurred
pre-pandemic from the years 2015 to 2018, where poverty dropped dramatically from 23.5 percent in
2015 to 16.7 percent in 2018, which is a 6.8 percent point reduction in three years, the largest single-
period decline within the two decades and represents the Philippines’s strongest poverty reduction
performance. A sudden reversal of the post-pandemic trend and increase of poverty head count from
16.7 percent in 2018 to 18.1 percent in 2021 erased the recent gains from 2015 to 2018, materializing
the risk to downward mobility due to aggregate shocks brought about by the COVID-19 pandemic.
Recovery after the pandemic where poverty head count fell to 15.5 percent in 2023 below the pre-
pandemic level suggests resilience but also raises questions about which households, groups and regions
genuinely recovered and those who remained vulnerable. Within the two decades, the poverty gap3 (or
the minimum cost of eliminating poverty4) declined faster in relative terms than the head count, from
5.6 percent in 2003 to 2.2 percent in 2023, while the distribution-sensitive measure dropped the most
dramatically, from 2.2 percent to 0.7 percent. This implies that extreme poverty is declining faster than
overall poverty.

  Has growth been pro-poor lately? Growth elasticity of poverty in the Philippines has changed
considerably over time. Earlier estimates show relatively weak pro-poor growth: Balisacan and Fuwa

medium-income regions drove the economic growth.

3. Profile of the Middle-Income Class
The ‘middle-class’ is widely discussed in the social sciences and has different definitions across
disciplines. We can either define the middle-class as those households with per capital income within
an absolute threshold valued at purchasing power parity, or use a relative measure based on the income
distribution in a country. An earlier work of Thurow (1987) and subsequently Birdsall, Graham and
Pettinato (2000) defines the middle-class as those earning between 75 percent to 125 percent of the
median household per capita income, while Easterly (2001) defines the middle-class as those within the
expenditure quintiles two to four. Milanovic and Yitzhaki (2002) defined the global middle-class as
those earning a per capita income of $12 to $50 per day. Banerjee and Duflo (2008) used daily per
capita expenditures between $2 to $10 valued at purchasing power parity, while Ravallion (2010)
ranged between $2 to $13. The Asian Development Bank (2010) adopted a broader range of $2 to $20
per capita daily expenditure valued in 2005 PPP $.
For the purpose of this paper, we adopt Albert et al.’s (2018a; 2018b) poverty-line based approach
defining the middle-income households in the Philippines as those with per capita income between two
and twelve times the official poverty line. In the Philippines, the measure of welfare or living standards
is per capita current income rather than consumption. The poverty line is defined as the minimum
income deemed necessary for a household to meet basic needs, food and non-food, and most importantly
to obtain adequate nutrition. This approach ensures the classification is consistent with official poverty
statistics and policy-relevant, in particular compatible with existing social protection measures.

of downward mobility risk during the COVID-19 pandemic. Subsistence poverty dropped from 3.4
percent in 2018 to 2.7 percent in 2023 nationally. Even during the COVID-19 spike in 2021, subsistence
poverty was only at 3.9 percent.
The middle-income class is growing but remains vulnerable. The pandemic induced downward
mobility, with middle- to high-income households dropping from 52.8 percent in 2018 to 49.1 percent
in 2021 but recovered to 51.3 percent by 2023. While poverty declined to 10.9 percent among
9

households in 2023 (subsistence poor at 2.7 percent, poor but not in subsistence at 8.2 percent), the low-
income but not poor representing vulnerable households living just above the poverty line, grew from
35.1 percent in 2018 to 37.8 percent in 2023, encompassing 10.4 million households. Despite the
middle- to high-income household recovery from 49.1 percent in 2021 to 51.3 percent in 2023, the low-
income but not poor remain sizeable at 37.8 percent of total households. This suggests that many
households that fell during the COVID-19 pandemic have not fully recovered and remain vulnerable
despite being out of poverty.
While urban areas remain dominated by middle-income households (60.1 percent middle-to-high
income), urban experienced a 4.5 percentage point shift toward lower-income categories from 2018 to

not poor category, and 40.5 percent in middle- to high-income. While the national poverty rate is falling,
household vulnerability seems to be rising with nearly half of Filipino households (48.7 percent) either
poor or low-income.

4.  Literature Review
A foundational theoretical paper by Ravallion (1988) distinguished between permanent income
from its variable component arising from risk and developed a framework for decomposing poverty
into persistent and transient components to understand the poverty cost of income variability in rural
India. Morduch (1994) formalized the distinction between chronic or structural poverty and vulnerable
or stochastic poverty. Stochastic poverty in low-income countries is when a household’s consumption
varies a good deal over time and may fall below the poverty line even though its permanent income is
above the poverty threshold. This is due to income shocks, insufficient insurance mechanisms and
limited access to credit and savings to smooth consumption (Morduch 1994). Many researchers
contributed to the literature differentiating chronic from transient poverty for the purposes of clarifying
what poverty alleviation programs and social protection are needed by different socioeconomic groups.
A study by Jalan and Ravallion (2000) tested the determinants of chronic and transient poverty in rural
China, and found that wealth holdings, physical capital and life cycle matters both for chronic and
transient  poverty,  while  household  size,  education  level,  geographical  location  and  agricultural
productivity matters for chronic poverty. The implication of this is that programs appropriate for
15

reducing poverty does not address transient poverty, which may better be addressed by policy
instruments such as those that provide employment and livelihood opportunities like seasonal public
work programs, credit schemes, savings, insurance and income stabilization mechanisms.
Understanding vulnerability and risks, in connection to poverty is crucial for several reasons.
Dercon (2001) stated that vulnerability and risk is also a cause of destitution and not just another
dimension of poverty. Material vulnerability caused by shocks may have long-lasting consequences to
the welfare and human capital formation among households, especially the poor. When a household
suddenly faces a shock without adequate savings, they may be forced to forego needed health spending
or withdraw children from school, or even sell productive assets, which can lead to high welfare costs
into their future. In agrarian economies in India, seasonal fluctuations that result to unanticipated
income shocks affects uninsured households who tend to sacrifice their children’s school attendance as
a form of self-insurance, which has costly, long-run implications for the household (Jacoby and
Skoufias 1997). Dercon (2005) asserted that risk and vulnerability is an ignored aspect in the design of
anti-poverty policies.
Ligon and Schechter (2003) viewed vulnerability as a measure of welfare loss associated with
uncertainty to consumption, associated with poverty and exposure to aggregate shocks. In their
Bulgarian study, poverty and aggregate shocks reduced welfare equally, with aggregate shocks
mattering more than idiosyncratic risks. Calvo and Dercon (2005; 2013) formalized vulnerability
measurement through an axiomatic approach that distinguishes between vulnerability (forward-looking
threat) from poverty (outcome).
The influential literature earlier cited that elaborated on the links between vulnerability, risks and
poverty concentrated empirical testing in agricultural and rural economies in developing country
contexts, which are characterized with shocks such as the seasonality of agricultural production,
climate, droughts and famine. In the early 2000s, the vulnerability to poverty context was also viewed
in the lens of globalization and shocks to domestic economies brought about by increasing
internationalization, such as financial crises and structural reforms (Dercon 2001). The current decade
saw the advent of the fourth industrial revolution, increasing automation and changes in the world of
work, such as the introduction of gig economy and increasing importance of self-employment and
informal employment. Also, extreme poverty has fallen dramatically compared to a decade ago, with
lower poverty rates now observed in developing economies. Given these recent developments, new
areas of research on vulnerability, poverty and emerging sources of risks can be explored to understand
better the current dynamics of vulnerability and poverty and to inform design of social protection
systems that are appropriate in this new context.
Vulnerability analysis extends beyond monitoring those currently in poverty to encompassing
future poverty risks. Dercon (2001) defined vulnerability as the probability of significant welfare losses
due to uncertain events. We also differentiate risks according to its effect: idiosyncratic if it only affects
few individuals or households, and covariate if it affects an entire community or region. Dercon (2001)
provided a framework for analyzing vulnerability to poverty, emphasizing the importance of household
assets, incomes and capabilities as protective factors against vulnerability and poverty. The framework
also acknowledges the potential risks associated with assets accumulation, incomes and capabilities.
The framework highlights the importance of asset building and accumulation, not just of physical and
financial assets, but also human capital in the form of adequate nutrition and health, as well as quality
education that empowers households with skills and capabilities to be productive in the labor market.

Chaudhuri and Datt (2001) developed a methodology measuring vulnerability as the probability
of a household finding itself income-poor in the next period and applied this in the Philippine context
in the years 1997 to 1998. Chaudhuri, Jalan and Suryahadi (2002) used this approach in Indonesia using
cross-sectional data, while Chaudhuri (2003) expanded the framework with illustrative examples from
the Philippines, Indonesia and rural south and southwestern China (1985-1990). Albert and Ramos
(2008) and later Albert and Vizmanos (2018) updated these estimates for the Philippines (2003, 2009
and 2015), finding significant vulnerability across various groups and recommending forward-looking
interventions that build the resilience of households to reduce future poverty.
Social protection systems play crucial roles in preventing downward mobility. Social protection
programs have been key instruments to reduce the incidence and depth of poverty in developing
economies. Barrientos and Hulme (2008) emphasized the importance of social protection not only in
17

managing globalization risks but also viewing it in a broader focus on basic needs and building lasting
capabilities, reflected in the increasing importance of programs and policies that complement cash
transfers with basic services, such as education and health, employment guarantees or asset building. In
Southeast Asia, Cook and Pincus (2014) stated that common challenges in social protection include
economic risks associated with financial globalization, rapid urbanization, high levels of informal
employment, rising dependency ratios and highly unequal gender division of labor. Social insurance
programs are still concentrated on the formal and public sectors, supplemented in middle-income
countries with voluntary, contributory programs for small businesses, farmers and the informal sector
(Cook and Pincus 2014).
Despite its importance, some studies also find that social protection policies may crowd out or
have negative welfare effects in communities, for instance, beneficiaries withdrawing from informal
networks which may harm non-beneficiaries (Dercon 2001). In this way, actions to reduce risk impact
or exposure to it, such as savings, access to credit and insurance, temporary safety nets and social
protection are needed. Community-based risk sharing among informal networks, friends or social
capital are also considered emergency safety nets of people in times of unexpected crisis.
Safety net interventions for individuals facing temporary economic vulnerability can prevent
many from becoming persistently impoverished, which would reduce the strain on programs targeting
chronic poverty (Balisacan and Fuwa 2004; Barrientos and Hulme 2008). The Philippines' cornerstone
social protection program, the Pantawid Pamilyang Pilipino Program (4Ps) primarily target the poorest
segments, leaving low income but non-poor, as well as the middle-class households with limited
protection against economic shocks, as highlighted during the COVID-19 pandemic.
5. Methodology
We employ the Chaudhuri and Datt (2001) methodology to estimate a household’s vulnerability
to income poverty by measuring its expected income and the volatility (variance) of its income streams.
While this approach ideally uses longitudinal data wherein the same households are observed over a
certain period, data limitations restrict us to use the available cross-sectional data from the Family
Income and Expenditure Survey (FIES) for the years 2018, 2021 and 2023. Following Chaudhuri
(2003), we assume the variation of income is due to observable household and community
characteristics as well as stochastic (random) factors6, for example, events unique to a household such
as disasters or weather shocks, illness, job loss, or significant price fluctuations.

6. Data
We utilize data from the merged Family Income and Expenditure Survey and Labor Force Survey
(FIES-LFS) conducted by the Philippine Statistics Authority (PSA) for the years 2018, 2021, and 2023.
The merged FIES-LFS datasets provide comprehensive information on household income,
expenditures, employment and household attributes and living conditions, making it the primary source
for analyzing middle-class dynamics in the Philippines. The FIES is also the survey used for generating
poverty and income distribution statistics in the Philippines. The FIES is now conducted every two
years. The 2018 FIES sampled 147,717 households from urban and rural areas of each province, while
the 2021 round covered 165,029 households, and the 2023 round covered 163,268 households. The
basic household income models are estimated using these data.
We supplement the household income models with data on infrastructure conditions of provinces
and highly urbanized cities, obtained from the 2020 Census of Population and Housing (CPH) also
released by the PSA. The CPH provides the characteristics of the 42,046 barangays in the Philippines.
We construct two simple infrastructure indices for each province and highly urbanized city: a
communications infrastructure index derived from the percentage of barangays with cellular phone
signal, and a water infrastructure index based on the percentage of barangays with community
waterworks system. These location-based covariates capture how infrastructure affects household
income levels and variance.
19

In addition to these we also obtained permission to use rainfall data from the Philippine
Atmospheric, Geophysical and Astronomical Services Administration (PAGASA) to enrich our
household income models by way of including rainfall shocks. The PAGASA collects rainfall data from
weather stations spread across the Philippines. We made use of monthly average rainfall data obtained
by PAGASA across 48 weather stations from the years 2000 to 2024. We define rainfall shocks, as well
as identify each province’s nearest weather station using the methodology of Bayudan-Dacuycuy and
Baje (2018). A rainfall shock in a province occurs when at least one month in a period shows rainfall
exceeding two standard deviations above the 25-year monthly average. The PAGASA rainfall shock
data from 2018 to 2023 per weather stations are merged with the 117 provinces including the highly
urbanized cities in the FIES dataset. We interact the rainfall shocks with rural location indicator to
capture differential effects on rural households.
The use of the merged FIES-LFS from 2018, 2021 and 2023 allows us to examine the
vulnerability rates and changes across households before and after the COVID-19 pandemic. This also
offers us an opportunity for cross-validation of the results through comparison across the years. We use
the FIES-LFS data from 2018, 2021 and 2023 to examine how well do the estimates of vulnerability
and hence of predicted poverty track actual poverty rates as in Chaudhuri and Datt (2001). More
broadly, we also compare the distribution of vulnerability rates against income poverty rates across
major socioeconomic groups.
7. Interpretation of the Vulnerability Estimate
The mean vulnerability level within the group should in the absence of aggregate shocks
approximately equal the observed household poverty rate. While the choice of a vulnerability threshold
is arbitrary, we follow the method of Chaudhuri and Datt (2001) in categorizing households according
to their vulnerability risk. The high vulnerability threshold is 0.50, while the cutoff for vulnerable is the
official poverty incidence during the year.
Amongst the vulnerable, we distinguish between the relatively vulnerable, those with an
estimated vulnerability level greater than the observed incidence of poverty but less than 0.50, and the
highly vulnerable, those having an estimated vulnerability level greater than 0.50 (Table 16). In this
manner we can compare group vulnerability rates side by side with poverty rates.

8. Results and Discussion
8.1 Discussion of Vulnerability: Overall Picture
This exercise of estimating vulnerability reveals to us a much larger at-risk population than
observed poverty head count rates alone show. Table 20 illustrates that across the years 2018 to 2023,
the national poverty rate among families is around 10.9 percent to 13.2 percent, while the vulnerability
rate is 29.0 percent to 32.0 percent, suggesting on average that vulnerability is 2.5 times higher than
current poverty. In simple terms many non-poor households still face significant risk of falling into
income poverty. The vulnerability estimates support the often-stated claim that the poverty statistics
underestimates the proportion of the population that is vulnerable to poverty. Indeed here we find that
there are households whose probability of poverty may be high who are nevertheless observed to be
non-poor, and conversely, there are those observed poor but assessed as non-vulnerable.
While poverty and vulnerability are closely related, we estimate that around 15.0 percent to 18.0
percent of the poor are not vulnerable, suggesting transitory poverty for this group. However, a
significant proportion of non-poor households faces risk, with 21.0 percent to 24.0 percent of the non-
poor assessed as vulnerable to falling into poverty. This represents a significant share often missed and
overlooked by traditional poverty measures.
As with the previous study by Chaudhuri and Datt (2001) we also find the same observation that
the vulnerable group is quite different to the currently observed poor population. We estimate that 2.0
percent to 4.0 percent of the non-vulnerable are observed to be poor, and the bulk (65.0 percent to 69.0
percent) of the vulnerable are non-poor. Of the 30.0 percent vulnerable population nationally in 2023,
the relatively vulnerable comprise 24.0 percent while the highly vulnerable make up the remaining 6.0
percent of the population. While both poverty rates and vulnerability rates increased during the COVID-
19 pandemic in 2021, partial recovery has been observed by 2023 (household poverty rate 12.1 percent,
2018; 13.2 percent, 2021; 10.9 percent, 2023; vulnerability rate 29.0 percent, 2018; 32.0 percent, 2021;
30.0 percent, 2023).
This has important policy implications: social protection should address and target not just the
currently poor (10.9 percent of households) but also aim to increase the financial resilience and
capabilities of the vulnerable population in responding to economic shocks. In this sense social
protection must increase overall resilience of households instead of just providing temporary relief

It is important to emphasize that vulnerability is conceptually distinct from observed poverty.
While poverty is an ex post outcome measured at a point in time, vulnerability is an ex ante, forward-
looking measure of the risk of being poor in the future. As such, vulnerability is not expected to coincide
perfectly with observed poverty. The existence of households that are non-poor but vulnerable reflects
income  fragility  and  exposure  to  shocks,  while  the  presence  of  some  poor  but  non-vulnerable
households reflects transitory poverty. This distinction is central to the policy relevance of vulnerability
estimates, particularly in contexts where poverty is increasingly dynamic and driven by shocks rather
than persistent deprivation.

8.2 Vulnerability and Poverty Profiles
The overall ratio of vulnerability incidence to poverty incidence is 2.75 in 2023 (Table 21). While
poverty rates showed gradual decrease, the vulnerability-to-poverty ratio increased from 2.40 in 2018
to 2.75 in 2023, suggesting that there is an increase to households at risk of falling into poverty even as
22

current poverty rates are decreasing. The vulnerability to poverty ratio differs across categories of
households according to urban location, regions, highest education and sector of employment of the
household head.
Importantly, the ordering of vulnerability across regions, urban-rural areas, and socioeconomic
groups closely mirrors the ordering of observed poverty, which provides an internal validity check on
the vulnerability estimates. At the same time, the magnitudes differ systematically, reflecting the
forward-looking and risk-based nature of vulnerability as distinct from realized poverty.
Rural areas face more than double the poverty rate of urban areas (16.0 percent of rural
households versus 7.0 percent of urban in 2023). Rural vulnerability also remains alarmingly high at
43.0 percent versus 20.0 percent urban areas in 2023. This gap remains relatively consistent across all
three years. Among the regions, the National Capital Region has the highest vulnerability to poverty
ratio (7.90) when compared to the national average. While, it has the lowest poverty rate (1.1 percent),
the vulnerability rate is 9.0 percent. This pattern is not anomalous. Rather, it reflects that while average
incomes in the NCR are high and poverty incidence is low, many households remain exposed to labor
market volatility, informal and contractual employment, high living costs, and macroeconomic shocks.
In such contexts, vulnerability captures economic insecurity rather than deprivation per se, and thus
remains policy-relevant even where poverty is low. In other words, a lot of Metro Manila non-poor are
at risk of downward mobility, and that poverty may be underestimated even among the relatively
middle-income regions.
Across all regions, rural areas consistently face higher vulnerability rates compared to urban
areas: Northern and Central Luzon: 29.0 percent vulnerable among rural versus 15.0 percent urban;
Southern Luzon 40.0 percent rural versus 22.0 percent urban; Western and Central Visayas 37.0 percent
rural versus 25.0 percent urban; Eastern Visayas 53.0 percent rural versus 32.0 percent urban; Mindanao
(excluding BARMM) 55.0 percent rural versus 27.0 percent urban; and BARMM 76.0 percent rural
versus 55.0 percent urban. It is also worth noting that aside from the high gaps of poverty incidence
between rural and urban areas, the same can be said of vulnerability rates.
We find more pronounced differences for the profile of the highly vulnerable (households with
greater than 50.0 percent probability of being poor). In 2023, rural Mindanao including BARMM
accounts for a quarter of the poor households (25.0 percent), but it accounts for more than a third (35.0
percent) of the highly vulnerable. BARMM also shows extreme vulnerability: 76.0 percent of rural
BARMM population is vulnerable in 2023, with 23.0 percent poor.
Similarly, households whose heads have less than primary or with primary education account for
57.0 percent of the poor, but account for 77.0 percent of the highly vulnerable. These differences have
implications for targeting of anti-poverty policies. In line with this, we also find that education is a key
protective factor against poverty and vulnerability. Households headed by individuals with tertiary
education are less vulnerable and less likely to be observed poor, with vulnerability rate at 5.0 percent
and poverty rate at 2.0 percent.
Agriculture remains the highest-risk sector, with 25.0 percent poverty rate and 60.0 percent
vulnerability rate in 2023. Households reliant on agriculture (with heads employed in this sector)
comprise 19.0 percent of the population, but accounts for 44.0 percent of the poor households, 39.0
percent of the vulnerable, and 61.0 percent of the highly vulnerable. Industry, manufacturing and
services have lower poverty incidence, at 12.0 percent, 8.0 percent and 6.0 percent respectively, and
23

with lower rates of high vulnerability at 6.0 percent, 4.0 percent and 2.0 percent, respectively. For this
reason, transitioning more of the labor force and households to services, industry and manufacturing,
which have higher productivity and value-added is an important factor in addressing poverty and
vulnerability especially among rural areas and regions distant from the economic centers.
8.3 Sources of Vulnerability
Households with the same level of vulnerability may be vulnerable for different reasons: some
households have relatively lower income to begin with, while others have higher volatility or
uncertainty of income. Recognizing the sources of vulnerability among key groups of interest may aid
policymakers and program planners towards an efficient and equitable manner of allocating resources
for social protection. In terms of policy intervention, households with low endowments and thus lower
income, which are likely the poor households, will benefit more from a conditional cash transfer
program like the Pantawid Pamilyang Pilipino Program (4Ps Program), where households are provided
with cash conditional to their children’s school attendance and consistent health check-ups to ensure
that they complete basic education and develop good health and nutrition habits, with the aim of
breaking the intergenerational cycle of poverty among families. Households who are vulnerable due to
high uncertainty of income streams may be better helped with policies that reduce the risks that they
face or insure them against such risks.
Following Chaudhuri and Datt (2001) we adopt a classification of households by sources
vulnerability. The first group are the non-vulnerable, defined as those with estimated vulnerability level
below the poverty rate (in 2023, 10.9 percent), who will have estimated levels of mean income well
above the poverty line. The second group are those with relatively volatile income streams: labeled
high-volatility-income vulnerable, whose estimated vulnerability level is above the poverty rate, but the
estimated mean income is above the poverty line. The third group are the households with relatively
low endowments to begin with: labeled as low-mean-income vulnerable, whose mean levels of income
fall below the poverty line and whose vulnerability level or probability of finding themselves poor in
the period is above 50.0 percent.
Figure 3 plots the estimated mean income against the estimated standard deviation of income for
all households observed to be vulnerable in 2023. The scatterplot shows that households at the same
level of vulnerability can be vulnerable for different reasons. For example, well-off households with
relatively higher incomes may be vulnerable if there is their income streams fluctuate more compared
to households with stable employment and income sources. Households with relatively low income to
begin with are vulnerable due to this reason regardless of the variability of their income sources,
however an increase in the uncertainty of their income sources may leave them highly vulnerable.

Table 24 presents the overall picture on the sources of vulnerability in 2023. We estimate that

Table 25 presents the shares of high-volatility of income and low-mean income groups amongst
the vulnerable populations as well as amongst the highly vulnerable. Amongst the regions, while the
vulnerability rate is low in NCR (9.0 percent), it is entirely on account of high volatility of income (97.0
percent). The same is also observed among households with heads having tertiary or secondary
education. Across all regions, even after subdividing among rural and urban areas, at least 70.0 percent
of the vulnerable are due to high volatility of income.
The contrast among the importance of sources of vulnerability emerges among the high-
vulnerable population. At least 70.0 percent of the highly vulnerable in rural areas are due to low-mean
incomes, while the urban highly vulnerable are relatively more diverse: around 28.0 percent to 45.0
percent are due to high-volatility of income. A sizeable share (75.0 to 79.0 percent) of the highly
vulnerable amongst households with heads that are either primary-educated or lesser are low-mean
income vulnerable, while 63% of highly vulnerable households with tertiary-educated heads are high-
volatility of income vulnerable.
Provinces with low levels of infrastructure have higher vulnerable rates. Provinces with low
index of communications infrastructure have dramatically high vulnerability rate at 68.0 percent, versus
29.0 percent vulnerable in high-infrastructure provinces. For provinces with low index of waterworks
systems, the incidence of vulnerability is 39.0 percent versus 29.0 percent in high-infrastructure areas.

Regardless of the infrastructure conditions, the sources of vulnerability remain remarkably constant for
both vulnerable and highly vulnerable households. For vulnerable households, high-volatility of income
is the dominant source of vulnerability, around 74.0 to 87.0 percent, in contrast amongst the highly
vulnerable group, where low-mean incomes are the major source of vulnerability at 72.0 to 74.0 percent.
Vulnerability extends well beyond current poverty: 44.0 percent of the low income (but non-
poor) households, and even 11.0 percent of the lower middle-income households face significant risks
of vulnerability to poverty, primarily from income volatility. Social protection must expand beyond the
poorest to include these vulnerable populations. While conditional cash transfers like the 4Ps address
chronic poverty, vulnerable non-poor households need income stabilization support such as insurance,
disaster protection, health coverage and asset-building programs. This calls for differentiated policy
responses that proactively builds and strengthens the resilience of households, alongside current poverty
alleviation programs.

9. Policy Implications
Social protection must expand beyond reactive poverty relief to a broader focus on proactive resilience
building, as vulnerability incidence exceeds current poverty, with nearly a quarter of non-poor households at
significant risk to downward mobility. Social protection framework and programs must be designed to prevent
downward mobility and not just address current destitution. Differentiated policy interventions must address
the needs of groups, primarily based on their sources of vulnerability. For the households experiencing unstable
incomes or high volatility of income, which comprise 86.0 percent of the vulnerable, income stabilization
mechanisms such as unemployment insurance, health insurance coverage, as well as enhanced access to credit
and savings instruments promotes resilience and asset building. Complementary programs such as public
works or cash-for-work programs, business continuity support for micro, small and medium enterprises
(MSMEs) and emergency cash assistance during shocks or disasters may also help these households and
prevent them from downward mobility to poverty.
Amongst the low-mean income vulnerable, long-term human capital investments must be prioritized
through continuation of their participation in the 4Ps conditional cash transfers which must include proper
targeting of the poorest and those with the lowest endowments. These can be complemented with skills
upgrading, livelihood programs and asset building initiatives to prepare them to be financially independent
after graduation of support. The growing importance of the middle-income class, as well as the equity focus
on lifting the poorest out of destitution must mean that social protection encompasses and address the needs of
these various socioeconomic segments of society. Fiscal prudence as well as equity in targeting must also be
emphasized to keep the administrative cost of social protection low and efficient, reaching the actual intended
groups who need these interventions the most.
9.1 Social Protection
As the Philippines transitions toward upper-middle income status, the nature of poverty is changing
from predominantly chronic to increasingly dynamic and shock-driven. This implies that social protection
systems must evolve accordingly — from a primary focus on alleviating existing poverty toward a broader role
in preventing future poverty by protecting households against risks and downward mobility. Poverty itself is
not static, thus interventions cannot be based solely on static lists of poor households. Further, the middle
class, especially the lower middle class, are also in need of social protection.

The Social Protection Plan (2023 to 2028) indicates a paradigm shift in high-level social security policy
within the Philippines. It seeks to improve the welfare of Filipinos by enhancing their capacity to handle risks
and decrease vulnerability. Universal coverage means programs are accessible to everyone when needed. By
2028, the objectives of the Plan include reinforcing and streamlining social protection systems by managing
life cycle risks, addressing natural disasters and health emergencies, and building a universal, modern and
integrated system. Implementation of the Social Protection Floor requires strategies that secure income
38

protection and health services for every age, guaranteeing program availability during critical life transitions
and unforeseen circumstances.
The Social Protection Plan suggests a desire to evolve the social security system from just treating the
symptom of poverty by providing transfers to those already living in extreme poverty, towards a more modern,
innovative system that focuses on tackling the causes of poverty by addressing the lifecycle risks that all
members of society face.
10. Policy Recommendations
Strengthening Social Protection Systems
The vulnerability analysis shows that economic insecurity in the Philippines extends well beyond the
currently poor. A substantial share of households above the poverty line face a high probability of falling into
poverty due to income volatility, health shocks, labor market instability, and climate-related risks. This implies
that a social protection system focused narrowly on the poorest is no longer sufficient in a context where
poverty is increasingly dynamic and shock-driven. As the Philippines approaches upper-middle income status,
social protection must therefore evolve from a primarily poverty-alleviation instrument into a broader system
for preventing downward mobility and strengthening household resilience.
This shift supports a gradual transition toward universal social protection — understood not as universal
transfers, but as universal access to risk protection mechanisms, including health insurance, income and
employment protection, disaster and climate risk protection, and old-age security. Several priorities follow
directly from the vulnerability findings:
1. Expand protection to vulnerable non-poor households, especially the lower-income, and lower
middle-income class: Extend social protection coverage beyond the poor to include low income but
not poor and lower middle income housholds identified as vulnerable due to income volatity and
exposure to economic shocks, through instruments sic as unemployment insurance or temporary
income support, and contributory social insurance programs.
2. Reorient programs toward building resilience: Complement consumption-support programs with
interventions that build long-term resilience, including support for skills, stable employment, savings,
insurance, and productive asset accumulation rather than just providing temporary relief.
3. Improve identification of vulnerable households: Strengthen targeting and information systems to
better identify households at risk of falling into poverty, reduce exclusion and inclusion errors, and
enable timely, preventive interventions rather than purely reactive responses.
4. Integrate disaster and climate risk into social protection: Mainstream disaster risk management
and climate adaptation into social protection systems to protect household assets and enable rapid
recovery following shocks such as natural disasters.
Promoting Regional Development
The vulnerability profiles reveal strong spatial concentration of risk, particularly in rural and lagging
regions where households face systematically higher vulnerability even when they are not currently poor.
These patterns reflect unequal access to infrastructure, markets, services, and productive opportunities, which
39

constrain income stability and upward mobility. Reducing vulnerability and expanding the middle class
therefore requires a more spatially balanced development strategy.
To address regional disparities in middle-class development, the study recommends:
1. Prioritize infrastructure in high-vulnerability regions: Invest in transport, communications, energy
and digital infrastructure in lagging and predominantly rural areas to reduce isolation, transaction costs
of doing business and improve access to markets and services.
2. Promote regional economic diversification: Support the development of productive sectors outside
the traditional economic centers through targeted investments, industrial policy complemented by
servicification, and support for regional value chains that create stable employment and new sources
of wealth.
3. Strengthen human capital in lagging regions: Improve the quality and accessibility of education and
health services in lagging regions to raise productivity and reduce intergenerational transmission of
vulnerability.
4. Enhance local institutional capacity: Build the capacity of local governments to plan, implement and
monitor development programs effectively and to manage public resources in ways that respond to
local vulnerability profiles.
Improving Labor Market Outcomes
The analysis indicates that vulnerability is closely linked to the quality and stability of employment
rather than income levels alone. Households dependent on informal, seasonal, or low-productivity employment
face significantly higher vulnerability, even when their current incomes place them above the poverty line.
Strengthening labor market outcomes is therefore central to reducing vulnerability and enabling sustained
upward mobility. Suggested policy priorities include:
1. Expand formal and stable employment: Encourage formal job creation through regulatory reforms,
simplified business registration and incentives for firms to formalize and invest in labor-intensive
productive activities.
2. Improve income security in the labor market: Strengthen labor institutions and protections to reduce
income volatility and employment insecurity while maintaining flexibility for firms.
3. Invest in skills aligned with structural transformation: Expand workforce development and
lifelong learning systems to equip workers with skills needed for higher-productivity sectors, including
digital and green industries.
4. Support entrepreneurship and MSMEs: Improve access to finance, training, technology and
markets for micro, small and medium enterprises to foster job creation and income diversification,
particularly in rural areas.
Addressing Vulnerability Factors
Beyond income and employment, vulnerability is driven by exposure to shocks, limited access to risk
management tools, and constraints on asset accumulation. Health risks, disasters, financial exclusion, and
insecure property and livelihood assets play a central role in pushing households into poverty and preventing
40

recovery. Reducing vulnerability therefore requires targeted interventions that address these underlying risk
channels.
To reduce household vulnerability to poverty, the study recommends:
1. Expand access to financial risk management tools: Increase access to savings, credit, and insurance
to enable households to smooth consumption, manage shocks, and invest in productive activities.
2. Strengthen health systems and financial protection: Improve the quality, accessibility and financial
coverage of health services to reduce catastrophic health expenditures and health-related income
losses.
3. Enhance disaster preparedness and response systems: Invest in early warning, preparedness, and
post-disaster recovery mechanisms that protect lives, assets and livelihoods.
4. Support asset building and security: Promote policies that strengthen households asset ownership
and security, including land tenure, housing, and business capital, particularly for vulnerable and near-
poor households.
11. Conclusion
The analysis of middle-class development and vulnerability to income poverty in the Philippines reveals
a complex picture of progress and persistent challenges. While the country has made significant strides in
reducing extreme poverty and in expanding the middle-income class over the past three decades, the COVID-
19 pandemic highlighted the fragility of these gains and the vulnerability of many households to shocks. The
vision of a predominantly middle-class Philippines by 2040 remains achievable but requires sustained
commitment to policies that promote inclusive growth, reduce vulnerability and build resilience. The insights
presented in this study provides a foundation for evidence-based policy making that can help realize this vision
while ensuring that the benefits of development are shared broadly across the population.
The recovery of the share of the middle-income class to 41.3 percent by 2023 demonstrates resilience
and the effectiveness of policy responses. However, the recovery has not yet returned to pre-pandemic
baselines which indicates persistent vulnerabilities that require policy attention to build sustainable middle-
class growth. The persistent urban-rural divide in middle-class development reflects deeper structural issues
in the Philippine economy. Rural areas continue to lag in economic opportunities, infrastructure, and access to
services, limiting the potential for middle-class growth outside urban centers. The concentration of middle-
income households in specific regions particularly in NCR, CAR, and CALABARZON and the persistent
urban-rural divide point to the importance of geographic factors in determining economic opportunities, and
the need for more inclusive development strategies. This concentration, while beneficial for regional economic
growth, may exacerbate inequality and limit long-term growth potential by underutilizing human and natural
resources in other regions. The policy challenge lies in expanding middle-class opportunities while maintaining
the economic dynamism of leading regions.
The vulnerability analysis reveals that many Filipino households, including those currently classified as
middle-income, face significant risks of falling into poverty. The vulnerability rates among low-income but
non-poor and lower middle-income households suggest that recent gains may seem fragile and be susceptible
to reversal during economic downturns. The factors associated with vulnerability point to potential policy
interventions. Education emerges as a crucial factor, suggesting that investments in human capital can reduce
41

household vulnerability. Employment stability is equally important, indicating the need for policies that
promote formal employment and job security.
The social protection system has made important contributions to poverty reduction, particularly through
the 4Ps program, which have allowed households to build resilience and invest in human capital through
education and health and thereby break the intergenerational cycle of poverty among families. The analysis
reveals significant gaps in social protection coverage. Existing social protection programs, especially the 4Ps
primarily targets the poorest segments of the population, leaving the non-poor low-income and middle-class
households exposed to economic shocks. The COVID-19 pandemic highlighted these gaps, as many middle-
class families experienced significant hardship but were ineligible for 4Ps assistance, while they were provided
support through different means such as “ayuda”.
The vulnerability assessment defines the probability of a household finding itself income-poor but does
not capture its long-term economic prospects. The 4Ps program is intended to lift households out of future
poverty by investing in children’s present education and health through conditional cash transfers. It is
important for low-income households to build the assets and capabilities necessary for resilience and sustained
household upward mobility, and the 4Ps program through programmatic improvements can be instrumental in
achieving this.
The Philippines is soon to breach upper middle-income status. With this, the poverty thresholds will
have to be significantly adjusted upward. The Philippines still has a considerable share of its population who
remain vulnerable, deemed ‘poor’, at 58.7 percent, if assessed at upper-middle income country standards of
the World Bank, in which the poverty threshold is $8.30 a day. This means that we should be rethinking our
views of social protection to cover not just the poor, but the near poor, and even up to the lower middle class,
and progressively work toward universal social protection.
The findings in this study underscore that poverty in the Philippines is increasingly dynamic rather than
static. Preventing future poverty by strengthening household resilience is therefore as important as reducing
current poverty. Vulnerability analysis provides a necessary complement to traditional poverty measurement
in guiding this transition.
