A B S T R A C T 
This  study  examines  the  post-pandemic  transformation  of  labour  markets  in 
Southeast Asia, focusing on the rise  of the gig economy and its relationship to youth 
employment. Combining a survey of 1,200 young workers aged 18 to 29 across Vietnam, 
the Philippines, Indonesia, and Thailand collected between January and October 2023 
with 40 semi-structured interviews of platform workers, the study applies logistic and 
ordinary least squares regression alongside reflexive  thematic analysis to characterise 
the drivers, earnings, and lived experience of platform-mediated gig work. By 2023, an 
estimated 28.4 per cent of young urban workers in the four sampled economies engaged 
in  at  least  one  form  of  platform-mediated  gig  work,  with  full-time  participation 
concentrated  in  the  food  delivery,  ride-hailing,  and  online  retail  segments.  Logistic 
regression  indicates  that  secondary  school  completion  lowers  the  probability  of  gig 
participation  by  6.8  percentage  points  (p<0.05),  while  youth  from  lower-income 
households  and  urban  residents  are  significantly  more  likely  to  engage  in  gig  work. 
Earnings differentials are notable, with median full-time gig earnings ranging from USD 
247 in Vietnam to USD 358 in Thailand, but earnings volatility and absence of social 
protection  emerged  as  persistent  concerns.  Qualitative  analysis  identified  four 
overarching themes: the autonomy paradox, the social protection gap, opportunities for 
skills development, and intergenerational tensions over the status of platform work. The 
study  contributes  to  the  literature  on  the  gig  economy  in  emerging  Asia  and  informs 
policy debate on portable benefits, platform regulation, and the integration of platform 
work into national social protection systems.

Introduction

Southeast Asia entered the COVID-19 pandemic with one of the youngest demographic structures 
in the world. The combined population of Indonesia, the Philippines, Vietnam, Thailand, and Malaysia 
exceeds  580  million  people,  of  whom  approximately  27  per  cent  are  aged  between  15  and  29.  The 
pandemic,  which  disrupted  formal  employment  relationships  and  accelerated  digital  platform  usage, 
brought into sharp focus the structural features of regional labour markets that affect young people most 
acutely: dual-track segmentation between formal and informal employment, limited social protection 
coverage,  and  an  expanding  ecosystem  of  digital  platforms  mediating  short-term  work  assignments. 
According  to  the  International  Labour  Organisation  (ILO,  2023),  youth  unemployment  in  the  region 
peaked at 14.7 per cent in 2020 and remained at 11.2 per cent in 2022, well above pre-pandemic levels. 
Against this backdrop, the rise of the gig economy has emerged as both a coping mechanism and 
a  transformation  of  work  itself.  Platforms  such  as  Grab,  Gojek,  Foodpanda,  Lazada,  Shopee,  and 
Lalamove have rapidly scaled across the region, providing income opportunities for millions of young 
workers but also raising urgent questions about labour standards, earnings stability, and access to social 
protection.  The  ASEAN  Secretariat  (2023)  estimated  that  platform-mediated  work  accounted  for 
approximately 2.4 per cent of total regional employment by 2023, with the share substantially higher

Literature Review

Labour market theories relevant to platform work 
Labour  economics  offers  several  theoretical  frameworks  relevant  to  the  analysis  of  the  gig 
economy. The dual labour market or segmentation tradition (Doeringer and Piore, 1971; Reich et al., 
1973)  distinguishes  between  a  'primary'  segment  characterised  by  job  stability,  internal  promotion 
ladders, and relatively high wages, and a 'secondary' segment characterised by instability, low pay, and 
limited mobility. Platform work has been variously interpreted as a new manifestation of the secondary 
segment,  as  a  hybrid  that  combines  features  of  formal  and  informal  work,  or  as  a  distinct  category 
requiring  new  theoretical  apparatus  (Stanford,  2017).  Search-and-matching  models,  developed  by 
Diamond, Mortensen, and Pissarides (Mortensen and Pissarides, 1994), provide a complementary lens 
by modelling labour markets as imperfectly informed environments in which workers and firms engage 
in costly search; platform algorithms can be analysed as low-cost matching technologies that compress 
search frictions, with implications for both wages and the quality of matches.

A third relevant tradition is the literature on informality and segmentation in developing economies 
(Fields, 2005; Maloney, 2004). In low- and middle-income countries, the informal sector has historically 
been  large  and  heterogeneous,  containing  both  involuntary  low-productivity  workers  and  voluntary 
entrepreneurial  workers.  Platform  work  in  emerging  Asia  displays  features  consistent  with  each 
interpretation, complicating any simple characterisation of its welfare implications. The present study 
draws on this composite theoretical apparatus while contributing empirical evidence on which features 
are most descriptively apt for young Southeast Asian workers.

Empirical evidence on platform work and policy responses 
The empirical literature on platform work has expanded rapidly. Berg et al. (2018) provide an early 
comparative analysis of platform workers, finding considerable heterogeneity in earnings, time use, and 
satisfaction. Wood et al. (2019) examine the experiences of remote platform workers in Southeast Asia 
and  sub-Saharan  Africa,  emphasising  the  autonomy-control  paradox  in  algorithmic  management.  De 
Stefano (2016) traces the legal status of platform workers across multiple jurisdictions. For India, Surie 
(2018) documents the conditions of urban platform workers and the precarity of their livelihoods. For 
Indonesia, Nastiti (2022) provides a detailed account of Gojek and Grab drivers in Jakarta and Surabaya, 
while Soriano (2021) examines the Philippine context. Vietnamese platform work has been analysed by 
Tran et al. (2023). These studies collectively provide rich case material but rarely permit direct cross-
country comparison; the present study addresses this gap by employing a common survey instrument 
and consistent variable definitions across the four economies.

A parallel strand of literature focuses on policy responses to platform work. The European Union's 
Platform Workers Directive, adopted in 2024, established a rebuttable presumption of employment for 
platform workers, marking the most ambitious legislative intervention to date. In Southeast Asia, policy 
responses  have  been  more  incremental:  the  Philippines  has  explored  extending  the  Magna  Carta  for 
Workers  in  the  Informal  Economy  to  platform  workers,  Indonesia  has  wrestled  with  the  appropriate 
classification  of  online  drivers  (Perpres  36/2020  and  successor  regulations),  Vietnam's  2019  Labour 
Code  revision  created  space  for  non-standard  employment,  and  Thailand's  Department  of  Labour 
Protection  and  Welfare  has  issued  guidance  on  platform-mediated  work.  The  diversity  of  policy 
approaches provides natural variation for examining the relationship between regulation and outcomes, 
which the present study does not formally exploit but discusses qualitatively.

Youth employment in emerging markets 
Youth  employment  in  emerging  markets  is  shaped  by  demographic,  educational,  and 
macroeconomic dynamics. The World Bank's World Development Report on the Changing Nature of 
Work (2019) emphasised that technological change is reshaping the demand for skills and creating both 
opportunities  and  disruptions.  The  Asian  Development  Bank  (ADB,  2023)  documents  persistent 
challenges in school-to-work transitions, with informal employment absorbing a disproportionate share 
of  new  labour  market  entrants.  Pre-pandemic  regional  studies  (Choudhry et  al.,  2018;  Verick,  2014) 
identified  determinants  of  youth  unemployment  that  include  macroeconomic  growth  volatility, 
mismatches between training and labour demand, and barriers to entrepreneurship. The post-pandemic 
period brought  these challenges into sharper relief, with  platform  work emerging  as  a partial coping 
mechanism but also generating new uncertainties about long-term career trajectories.

Research gap 
Synthesising  the  literature,  three  research  gaps  motivate  the  present  study.  First,  directly 
comparable  cross-country  evidence  on  platform  participation  patterns  among  young  workers  in 
Southeast Asia is sparse. Second, the relationship between gig participation and traditional labour market 
indicators  (skills  accumulation,  earnings  differentials,  transition  to  formal  employment)  is  under-
explored in the regional context. Third, the integration of quantitative and qualitative evidence within a 
single  research  design  is  rare;  many  studies  focus  on  one  or  the  other.  The  present  mixed-methods 
comparative design addresses these gaps with a common instrument administered across four economies 
and a qualitative component nested within the same population..

The research design is a sequential explanatory mixed-methods design (Creswell and Plano Clark, 
2018). The quantitative phase produced statistical estimates of participation determinants and earnings 
differentials, while the qualitative phase deepened interpretation of mechanisms and lived experience. 
The two phases were integrated at the analytical and interpretive stages following the joint display logic 
recommended by Fetters et al. (2013). Ethics approval was obtained from the institutional review board 
of the lead author's university (Foreign Trade University, approval reference FTU/IRB/2022/053), and 
informed consent was secured from all participants.

Sampling and data collection 
The quantitative sample comprised 1,200 young workers aged 18 to 29, distributed across Vietnam 
(n = 320), the Philippines (n = 300), Indonesia (n = 320), and Thailand (n = 260). The sample was drawn 
through a multi-stage design: (i) urban areas were selected (Hanoi and Ho Chi Minh City in Vietnam; 
Manila, Cebu, and Davao in the Philippines; Jakarta, Surabaya, and Yogyakarta in Indonesia; Bangkok 
and Chiang Mai in Thailand); (ii) within each city, neighbourhoods were stratified by socioeconomic 
status; (iii) within each neighbourhood, respondents were recruited through a combination of intercept 
methods  near  platform  driver  hubs  and  online  panels  accessing  university  and  vocational  training 
networks. Quotas were set to ensure approximately equal representation of platform-engaged and non-
platform-engaged workers within each country sub-sample. The survey instrument, developed in English 
and translated into Vietnamese, Tagalog/Filipino, Bahasa Indonesia, and Thai using forward and back 
translation, covered demographic characteristics, education, employment history, current employment 
status, platform-specific experience, earnings, satisfaction, and aspirations.

The qualitative sample comprised 40 in-depth interviews (Vietnam n = 11, Philippines n = 10, 
Indonesia n = 11, Thailand n = 8) selected purposively from the survey sample to capture variation in 
platform type, intensity of platform engagement, age, gender, and education. Interviews lasted between 
60 and 95 minutes, were audio-recorded with consent, conducted in the respondent's preferred language, 
and transcribed verbatim. The topic guide structured the conversation around five themes: pathway into 
platform work, working conditions and earnings, family and community attitudes, future aspirations, 
and policy preferences.  The guide was iteratively refined  as data  accumulated, in  line  with  reflexive 
practice (Braun and Clarke, 2019).

Analytical strategy 
Quantitative analysis employed logistic regression for binary outcomes (participation in any gig 
work; participation in full-time gig work as primary income source) and ordinary least squares regression 
for continuous outcomes (logarithm of monthly earnings; self-reported job satisfaction on a Likert scale). 
Independent  variables  included  gender,  age,  education  (highest  level  completed),  household  income 
tercile, urban residence, parental education, and country fixed effects. Robust standard errors were used 
throughout. Heckman selection models were estimated as a robustness check to address the possibility 
that earnings analyses are conditioned on the participation decision. The data were weighted to align 
with national-level demographic distributions where possible.

Qualitative analysis followed Braun and Clarke's (2019) reflexive thematic approach. Transcripts 
were coded in NVivo 14. Two researchers (one a member of the research team, one an external coder) 
independently coded a 12-transcript subsample to establish an initial codebook, achieving Cohen's kappa 
of 0.79 prior to full-corpus coding. Disagreements were resolved through team discussion. Themes were 
refined  through  iterative  reading,  with  attention  to  negative  cases  that  complicated  emerging 
interpretations.  Researcher  reflexivity  was  practised  through  a  research  diary  maintained  by  each 
interviewer.

Results

Sample characteristics and participation patterns 
Table 1 (described here in prose) summarises sample characteristics. Mean age was 23.4 years, 
with no significant difference across the four countries. Females constituted 47 per cent of the sample 
overall, somewhat lower among full-time gig workers (38 per cent). Sixty-two per cent of respondents 
had completed secondary education and 24 per cent had tertiary qualifications. Across the full sample, 
38 per cent reported engaging in at least one form of platform-mediated gig work in the past 12 months, 
with the highest participation rate in Vietnam (42 per cent), followed by the Philippines (39 per cent), 
Indonesia (37 per cent), and Thailand (33 per cent). Among those engaged, 23 per cent reported platform 
work as their primary income source (full-time gig workers), 47 per cent  as a regular supplementary 
source  (15  hours  per  week  or  more),  and  30  per  cent  as  occasional  supplementary  work.  The  most 
common  platforms  were  ride-hailing  and  food  delivery  (Grab,  Gojek,  Foodpanda,  Grab  Food,  Now, 
Lineman), online retail (Shopee, Lazada, Tokopedia), and remote freelance platforms (Upwork, Fiverr).

Determinants of platform participation 
Table 2 presents logistic regression results for the probability of any gig participation. Secondary 
school completion was significantly associated with lower participation probability (β =  -0.31, OR = 
0.73, 95% CI 0.58 to 0.93, p = 0.011), equivalent to approximately a 6.8 percentage point decrease in 
participation  probability  at  sample  means.  Tertiary  education  was  associated  with  a  smaller  and  less 
precisely estimated effect (β = -0.18, p = 0.09), suggesting that the participation gradient is not strictly 
monotonic in education. Household income tercile entered negatively, with respondents from the lowest 
tercile 11.4 percentage points more likely to participate than those from the highest tercile (p < 0.001). 
Urban  residence  and  male  gender  were  also  positively  associated  with  participation.  Country  fixed 
effects were statistically significant, indicating that country-specific factors not captured by individual 
covariates account for meaningful variation in participation rates.

For  full-time  platform  participation  as  primary  income  source,  the  determinants  exhibited  a 
partially different pattern. Education remained negatively associated with full-time participation, but the 
magnitude was larger (β = -0.46, OR = 0.63, p = 0.003). Male respondents were substantially more likely 
to engage in full-time gig work, particularly in ride-hailing and delivery (β = 0.61, OR = 1.84, p < 0.001). 
Household income remained negatively associated. Vietnam and the Philippines had higher full-time 
participation rates conditional on covariates, while Thailand had the lowest.

Qualitative themes 
Reflexive thematic analysis of the 40 interviews identified four overarching themes that recurred 
across countries: (1) the autonomy paradox;  (2) the social protection gap; (3) opportunities for skills 
development; and (4) intergenerational tensions over the status of platform work. The autonomy paradox 
describes the tension between perceived control over one's schedule and the binding constraints imposed 
by  platform  algorithms,  peak-hour  incentive  structures,  and  customer  ratings.  A  24-year-old  food 
delivery driver in Hanoi described the experience as 'free in the morning to choose when to start, but by 
lunchtime  the  app  decides  for  me.'  The  social  protection  gap  surfaced  repeatedly  in  narratives  about 
illness, injury, and pregnancy, with  platform workers articulating  awareness  of and concern over  the 
absence  of  health  insurance,  paid  sick  leave,  and  maternity  protection.  A  27-year-old  e-commerce 
courier in Manila put it directly: 'When I get sick, I just stop. There is no one paying.' Opportunities for 
skills development were mentioned by a subset of workers, particularly in customer service, financial 
management,  and  digital  tool  use.  Intergenerational  tensions  surfaced  in  conversations  about  family 
expectations,  with  several  respondents  describing  parental  disappointment  that  they  had  not  entered 
'proper'  jobs,  and  others  framing  platform  work  as  a  temporary  stepping  stone  to  professional 
employment.

Discussion

The empirical  findings  paint a  complex portrait  of the post-pandemic labour market  for young 
Southeast  Asians.  Platform-mediated  gig  work  occupies  a  substantial  and  growing  share  of  youth 
employment,  with  38  per  cent  of  the  four-country  urban  sample  engaging  in  at  least  some  form  of 
platform work and 23 per cent of those reporting it as their primary income source. The participation 
pattern is concentrated among less-educated, lower-income, urban, and male workers, consistent with

Theoretically, the findings are most consistent with a hybrid characterisation of platform work as 
occupying  a  distinctive  position  between  the  formal  and  informal  sectors.  Earnings  differentials  are 
modest in magnitude but not negligible, and earnings volatility is substantially higher than in comparable 
non-platform  work.  The  qualitative  findings  on  the  autonomy  paradox  extend  Wood  et  al.'s  (2019) 
analysis to a different empirical context and confirm the generality of the tension between perceived 
flexibility and algorithmic constraint. The social protection gap finding aligns with the broader literature 
on social protection floors and the imperative to extend coverage to non-standard employment.

Policy implications  are several.  First,  the social protection gap suggests urgent  need for policy 
innovation  to  extend  health  insurance,  maternity  protection,  and  contributory  pension  schemes  to 
platform workers without forcing rigid reclassification into traditional employment categories. Portable 
benefits, contributory account structures linked to platform-mediated earnings, and public co-funding 
mechanisms  warrant  serious  experimentation.  The  Indonesian  BPJS  Ketenagakerjaan  extension  to 
platform drivers and the Vietnamese voluntary social insurance scheme represent steps in this direction; 
both could benefit from cross-country learning. Second, platform regulation needs to balance protection 
against rigidity that might suppress the legitimate flexibility benefits that workers value. Light-touch 
frameworks  that  establish  basic  minimum  standards,  transparent  algorithmic  communication,  and

Several limitations should be acknowledged. The urban focus of the sample limits generalisation

subject  to  measurement  error,  particularly  for  platform  work  where  income  is  irregular  and  often

in particular cities. Despite these limitations, the convergence of cross-country quantitative patterns and 
qualitative  themes  provides  a  coherent  account  of  the  structure  and  experience  of  platform  work  for

A  noteworthy  dimension  of  the  post-pandemic  landscape  is  the  role  of  digital  remittances  and 
platform-based  gig  earnings  in  supplementing  household  incomes  during  periods  of  macroeconomic 
shock.  Survey  respondents  reporting  full-time  platform  work  were  significantly  more  likely  to  be 
primary or co-primary household earners than would be expected from their demographic profile alone, 
suggesting that platform work substitutes for formal employment under conditions of elevated economic 
uncertainty. The intersection of platform work, household financial resilience, and the macroeconomic 
recovery merits further investigation, particularly as ASEAN governments calibrate the next phase of 
social protection extension.

Gender  dynamics  within  the  platform  economy  display  interesting  patterns.  While  male 
respondents  dominate  the  visible  ride-hailing  and  delivery  segments,  female  respondents  are  over-
represented  in  online  retail  (Shopee  Live,  TikTok  Shop),  beauty  and  wellness  services,  and  remote 
freelance work. Earnings within female-dominated segments are typically lower than in male-dominated 
segments,  but  flexibility  benefits  are  reported  as  more  salient.  The  intersection  of  gender,  platform 
segment,  and  care  responsibilities  emerged  as  a  particularly  important  theme  in  the  Philippine  and 
Indonesian interview material, where women platform workers spoke at length about juggling platform 
income with childcare and household responsibilities.

Comparative  reflections  across  the  four  countries  reveal  both  common  patterns  and  country-
specific dynamics. Vietnamese platform workers benefit from a relatively young platform ecosystem 
and rapidly expanding domestic demand, but they face constraints from a tightening labour market in 
formal  sectors  that  draws  away  potential  platform  supply.  Filipino  platform  workers  operate  in  an 
environment characterised by extensive overseas labour migration culture and a long-standing tradition 
of remittance-supported households; platform work is often discussed as a substitute for or complement 
to migration. Indonesian platform workers operate at the largest scale in absolute terms and benefit from 
the most developed domestic super-app ecosystem, but they also face complex regulatory negotiations 
as  authorities  determine  their  classification.  Thai  platform  workers  operate  in  a  relatively  older 
demographic structure where platform participation among the very young is lower than the regional 
average, and where the integration of foreign workers (Myanmar, Cambodian, Laotian) into the platform 
economy adds an additional layer of analytical complexity that future research should address.

policy  innovation  in  social  protection,  platform  regulation,  and  skills  development.  The  empirical 
patterns  suggest  that  policy  makers  should  treat  platform  work  neither  as  a  uniformly  positive  nor 
uniformly negative phenomenon but as a hybrid form of work that requires tailored responses. Future 
research could extend the analysis through longitudinal tracking, expansion to additional countries, and 
deeper engagement with platform-firm strategies and the algorithmic management practices that shape 
worker experience.

The structural context within which platform work has expanded in Southeast Asia is shaped by 
three  intersecting  forces:  rapid  digitalisation  of  consumer-facing  services,  persistent  informality  in 
labour markets, and policy frameworks designed for an earlier era of standard wage employment. Each 
of these forces  operates  with  country-specific intensity. Indonesia and the Philippines combine large 
young  populations  with  large  informal  sectors  and  active  super-app  ecosystems;  Vietnam  combines 
rapid digitalisation with a tightening formal labour market; Thailand combines an ageing demographic 
structure with a sophisticated digital economy and a significant migrant worker presence. The interaction 
between  these  forces  produces  distinct  platform  labour  landscapes  across  the  four  economies, 
contributing to the heterogeneity observed in the empirical results.

From  the  perspective  of  platform  firms,  the  post-pandemic  period  has  been  characterised  by 
intense competition, consolidation pressures, and growing scrutiny over commission structures, driver 
incentives,  and  algorithmic  management  practices.  Grab's  merger  discussions  with  regional  players, 
Gojek's integration with Tokopedia to form GoTo, Foodpanda's strategic reviews, and Lazada-Shopee 
competition  have  all  influenced  the  operational  conditions  experienced  by  platform  workers.  The 
interview  material  in  the  present  study  captured  worker  awareness  of  these  dynamics,  with  several 
respondents  noting  changes  in  commission  rates,  surge  multipliers,  and  rating  systems  that  they 
perceived as shifts in the platform-worker relationship over the past two to three years.

Skills  development  in  the platform  economy  operates through several  channels.  First,  platform 
workers acquire customer service, navigation, and self-management skills that are transferable, at least 
in principle, to other employment. Second, platform workers gain familiarity with digital tools, financial 
transactions, and customer-facing communication that have value in subsequent jobs. Third, a subset of 
platform  workers  leverage  their  earnings  to  invest  in  formal  education,  vocational  training,  or 
microenterprise  development.  Survey  responses  in  the  present  study  indicated  that  14  per  cent  of 
platform workers had used platform earnings to invest in further education or training in the previous 
two years, with the share highest in the Philippines (19 per cent) and Indonesia (17 per cent) and lowest 
in Thailand (8 per cent). These investments in human capital suggest that platform work, for at least 
some workers, functions as a productive bridge rather than a dead-end.

Mental  health  and  wellbeing  among  platform  workers  emerged  as  a  distinct  theme  in  the 
qualitative data, particularly in the post-pandemic period. Respondents articulated stress associated with 
earnings unpredictability, exposure to traffic and safety risks (in the case of ride-hailing and delivery), 
and the social isolation of solo work patterns. A 26-year-old Grab driver in Bangkok described the toll 
of seven-day work weeks during peak periods: 'I make more money, but I cannot remember the last time 
I saw my friends.' Although the present study does not include validated mental health measures, the 
qualitative material suggests an important agenda for future research on the wellbeing implications of 
platform participation, ideally combining survey measures with longitudinal designs.

of growing AI integration, with several respondents expressing concern about reduced earnings stability 
and  increased  monitoring  intensity.  The  competitive  dynamics  among  platforms  also  produce 
uncertainty: as platforms experiment with subscription models, gig-worker pooling arrangements, and 
AI-driven  dispatch,  the  experience  of  platform  work  may  shift  further  from  the  current  state.  Policy 
frameworks  should  anticipate  these  technological  dynamics  rather  than  focus  exclusively  on  current

adaptation to immediate disruption and a longer-term structural shift in the organisation of work. Young 
workers  occupy  a  particularly  visible  and  dynamic  position  within  this  shift,  both  contributing  to 
platform growth and bearing the brunt of its precarity. A policy and research agenda that takes platform 
work seriously — neither romanticising flexibility nor dismissing it as mere informalisation — will be 
essential to ensure that the gig economy delivers broad-based welfare gains.

A B S T R A C T
This  study  examines  the  post-pandemic  transformation  of  labour  markets  in
Southeast Asia, focusing on the rise  of the gig economy and its relationship to youth
employment. Combining a survey of 1,200 young workers aged 18 to 29 across Vietnam,
the Philippines, Indonesia, and Thailand collected between January and October 2023
with 40 semi-structured interviews of platform workers, the study applies logistic and
ordinary least squares regression alongside reflexive  thematic analysis to characterise
the drivers, earnings, and lived experience of platform-mediated gig work. By 2023, an
estimated 28.4 per cent of young urban workers in the four sampled economies engaged
in  at  least  one  form  of  platform-mediated  gig  work,  with  full-time  participation
concentrated  in  the  food  delivery,  ride-hailing,  and  online  retail  segments.  Logistic
regression  indicates  that  secondary  school  completion  lowers  the  probability  of  gig
participation  by  6.8  percentage  points  (p<0.05),  while  youth  from  lower-income
households  and  urban  residents  are  significantly  more  likely  to  engage  in  gig  work.
Earnings differentials are notable, with median full-time gig earnings ranging from USD
247 in Vietnam to USD 358 in Thailand, but earnings volatility and absence of social
protection  emerged  as  persistent  concerns.  Qualitative  analysis  identified  four
overarching themes: the autonomy paradox, the social protection gap, opportunities for
skills development, and intergenerational tensions over the status of platform work. The
study  contributes  to  the  literature  on  the  gig  economy  in  emerging  Asia  and  informs
policy debate on portable benefits, platform regulation, and the integration of platform
work into national social protection systems.

Introduction

Southeast Asia entered the COVID-19 pandemic with one of the youngest demographic structures
in the world. The combined population of Indonesia, the Philippines, Vietnam, Thailand, and Malaysia
exceeds  580  million  people,  of  whom  approximately  27  per  cent  are  aged  between  15  and  29.  The
pandemic,  which  disrupted  formal  employment  relationships  and  accelerated  digital  platform  usage,
brought into sharp focus the structural features of regional labour markets that affect young people most
acutely: dual-track segmentation between formal and informal employment, limited social protection
coverage,  and  an  expanding  ecosystem  of  digital  platforms  mediating  short-term  work  assignments.
According  to  the  International  Labour  Organisation  (ILO,  2023),  youth  unemployment  in  the  region
peaked at 14.7 per cent in 2020 and remained at 11.2 per cent in 2022, well above pre-pandemic levels.
Against this backdrop, the rise of the gig economy has emerged as both a coping mechanism and
a  transformation  of  work  itself.  Platforms  such  as  Grab,  Gojek,  Foodpanda,  Lazada,  Shopee,  and
Lalamove have rapidly scaled across the region, providing income opportunities for millions of young
workers but also raising urgent questions about labour standards, earnings stability, and access to social
protection.  The  ASEAN  Secretariat  (2023)  estimated  that  platform-mediated  work  accounted  for
approximately 2.4 per cent of total regional employment by 2023, with the share substantially higher

Literature Review

Labour market theories relevant to platform work
Labour  economics  offers  several  theoretical  frameworks  relevant  to  the  analysis  of  the  gig
economy. The dual labour market or segmentation tradition (Doeringer and Piore, 1971; Reich et al.,
1973)  distinguishes  between  a  'primary'  segment  characterised  by  job  stability,  internal  promotion
ladders, and relatively high wages, and a 'secondary' segment characterised by instability, low pay, and
limited mobility. Platform work has been variously interpreted as a new manifestation of the secondary
segment,  as  a  hybrid  that  combines  features  of  formal  and  informal  work,  or  as  a  distinct  category
requiring  new  theoretical  apparatus  (Stanford,  2017).  Search-and-matching  models,  developed  by
Diamond, Mortensen, and Pissarides (Mortensen and Pissarides, 1994), provide a complementary lens
by modelling labour markets as imperfectly informed environments in which workers and firms engage
in costly search; platform algorithms can be analysed as low-cost matching technologies that compress
search frictions, with implications for both wages and the quality of matches.

A third relevant tradition is the literature on informality and segmentation in developing economies
(Fields, 2005; Maloney, 2004). In low- and middle-income countries, the informal sector has historically
been  large  and  heterogeneous,  containing  both  involuntary  low-productivity  workers  and  voluntary
entrepreneurial  workers.  Platform  work  in  emerging  Asia  displays  features  consistent  with  each
interpretation, complicating any simple characterisation of its welfare implications. The present study
draws on this composite theoretical apparatus while contributing empirical evidence on which features
are most descriptively apt for young Southeast Asian workers.

Empirical evidence on platform work and policy responses
The empirical literature on platform work has expanded rapidly. Berg et al. (2018) provide an early
comparative analysis of platform workers, finding considerable heterogeneity in earnings, time use, and
satisfaction. Wood et al. (2019) examine the experiences of remote platform workers in Southeast Asia
and  sub-Saharan  Africa,  emphasising  the  autonomy-control  paradox  in  algorithmic  management.  De
Stefano (2016) traces the legal status of platform workers across multiple jurisdictions. For India, Surie
(2018) documents the conditions of urban platform workers and the precarity of their livelihoods. For
Indonesia, Nastiti (2022) provides a detailed account of Gojek and Grab drivers in Jakarta and Surabaya,
while Soriano (2021) examines the Philippine context. Vietnamese platform work has been analysed by
Tran et al. (2023). These studies collectively provide rich case material but rarely permit direct cross-
country comparison; the present study addresses this gap by employing a common survey instrument
and consistent variable definitions across the four economies.

A parallel strand of literature focuses on policy responses to platform work. The European Union's
Platform Workers Directive, adopted in 2024, established a rebuttable presumption of employment for
platform workers, marking the most ambitious legislative intervention to date. In Southeast Asia, policy
responses  have  been  more  incremental:  the  Philippines  has  explored  extending  the  Magna  Carta  for
Workers  in  the  Informal  Economy  to  platform  workers,  Indonesia  has  wrestled  with  the  appropriate
classification  of  online  drivers  (Perpres  36/2020  and  successor  regulations),  Vietnam's  2019  Labour
Code  revision  created  space  for  non-standard  employment,  and  Thailand's  Department  of  Labour
Protection  and  Welfare  has  issued  guidance  on  platform-mediated  work.  The  diversity  of  policy
approaches provides natural variation for examining the relationship between regulation and outcomes,
which the present study does not formally exploit but discusses qualitatively.

Youth employment in emerging markets
Youth  employment  in  emerging  markets  is  shaped  by  demographic,  educational,  and
macroeconomic dynamics. The World Bank's World Development Report on the Changing Nature of
Work (2019) emphasised that technological change is reshaping the demand for skills and creating both
opportunities  and  disruptions.  The  Asian  Development  Bank  (ADB,  2023)  documents  persistent
challenges in school-to-work transitions, with informal employment absorbing a disproportionate share
of  new  labour  market  entrants.  Pre-pandemic  regional  studies  (Choudhry et  al.,  2018;  Verick,  2014)
identified  determinants  of  youth  unemployment  that  include  macroeconomic  growth  volatility,
mismatches between training and labour demand, and barriers to entrepreneurship. The post-pandemic
period brought  these challenges into sharper relief, with  platform  work emerging  as  a partial coping
mechanism but also generating new uncertainties about long-term career trajectories.

Research gap
Synthesising  the  literature,  three  research  gaps  motivate  the  present  study.  First,  directly
comparable  cross-country  evidence  on  platform  participation  patterns  among  young  workers  in
Southeast Asia is sparse. Second, the relationship between gig participation and traditional labour market
indicators  (skills  accumulation,  earnings  differentials,  transition  to  formal  employment)  is  under-
explored in the regional context. Third, the integration of quantitative and qualitative evidence within a
single  research  design  is  rare;  many  studies  focus  on  one  or  the  other.  The  present  mixed-methods
comparative design addresses these gaps with a common instrument administered across four economies
and a qualitative component nested within the same population..

The research design is a sequential explanatory mixed-methods design (Creswell and Plano Clark,

The two phases were integrated at the analytical and interpretive stages following the joint display logic

Sampling and data collection
The quantitative sample comprised 1,200 young workers aged 18 to 29, distributed across Vietnam
(n = 320), the Philippines (n = 300), Indonesia (n = 320), and Thailand (n = 260). The sample was drawn
through a multi-stage design: (i) urban areas were selected (Hanoi and Ho Chi Minh City in Vietnam;
Manila, Cebu, and Davao in the Philippines; Jakarta, Surabaya, and Yogyakarta in Indonesia; Bangkok
and Chiang Mai in Thailand); (ii) within each city, neighbourhoods were stratified by socioeconomic
status; (iii) within each neighbourhood, respondents were recruited through a combination of intercept
methods  near  platform  driver  hubs  and  online  panels  accessing  university  and  vocational  training
networks. Quotas were set to ensure approximately equal representation of platform-engaged and non-
platform-engaged workers within each country sub-sample. The survey instrument, developed in English
and translated into Vietnamese, Tagalog/Filipino, Bahasa Indonesia, and Thai using forward and back
translation, covered demographic characteristics, education, employment history, current employment
status, platform-specific experience, earnings, satisfaction, and aspirations.

The qualitative sample comprised 40 in-depth interviews (Vietnam n = 11, Philippines n = 10,
Indonesia n = 11, Thailand n = 8) selected purposively from the survey sample to capture variation in
platform type, intensity of platform engagement, age, gender, and education. Interviews lasted between
60 and 95 minutes, were audio-recorded with consent, conducted in the respondent's preferred language,
and transcribed verbatim. The topic guide structured the conversation around five themes: pathway into
platform work, working conditions and earnings, family and community attitudes, future aspirations,
and policy preferences.  The guide was iteratively refined  as data  accumulated, in  line  with  reflexive
practice (Braun and Clarke, 2019).

Analytical strategy
Quantitative analysis employed logistic regression for binary outcomes (participation in any gig
work; participation in full-time gig work as primary income source) and ordinary least squares regression
for continuous outcomes (logarithm of monthly earnings; self-reported job satisfaction on a Likert scale).
Independent  variables  included  gender,  age,  education  (highest  level  completed),  household  income
tercile, urban residence, parental education, and country fixed effects. Robust standard errors were used
throughout. Heckman selection models were estimated as a robustness check to address the possibility
that earnings analyses are conditioned on the participation decision. The data were weighted to align
with national-level demographic distributions where possible.

Qualitative analysis followed Braun and Clarke's (2019) reflexive thematic approach. Transcripts
were coded in NVivo 14. Two researchers (one a member of the research team, one an external coder)
independently coded a 12-transcript subsample to establish an initial codebook, achieving Cohen's kappa
of 0.79 prior to full-corpus coding. Disagreements were resolved through team discussion. Themes were
refined  through  iterative  reading,  with  attention  to  negative  cases  that  complicated  emerging
interpretations.  Researcher  reflexivity  was  practised  through  a  research  diary  maintained  by  each
interviewer.

Results

Sample characteristics and participation patterns
Table 1 (described here in prose) summarises sample characteristics. Mean age was 23.4 years,
with no significant difference across the four countries. Females constituted 47 per cent of the sample
overall, somewhat lower among full-time gig workers (38 per cent). Sixty-two per cent of respondents
had completed secondary education and 24 per cent had tertiary qualifications. Across the full sample,
38 per cent reported engaging in at least one form of platform-mediated gig work in the past 12 months,
with the highest participation rate in Vietnam (42 per cent), followed by the Philippines (39 per cent),
Indonesia (37 per cent), and Thailand (33 per cent). Among those engaged, 23 per cent reported platform
work as their primary income source (full-time gig workers), 47 per cent  as a regular supplementary
source  (15  hours  per  week  or  more),  and  30  per  cent  as  occasional  supplementary  work.  The  most
common  platforms  were  ride-hailing  and  food  delivery  (Grab,  Gojek,  Foodpanda,  Grab  Food,  Now,
Lineman), online retail (Shopee, Lazada, Tokopedia), and remote freelance platforms (Upwork, Fiverr).

Determinants of platform participation
Table 2 presents logistic regression results for the probability of any gig participation. Secondary
school completion was significantly associated with lower participation probability (β =  -0.31, OR =
0.73, 95% CI 0.58 to 0.93, p = 0.011), equivalent to approximately a 6.8 percentage point decrease in
participation  probability  at  sample  means.  Tertiary  education  was  associated  with  a  smaller  and  less
precisely estimated effect (β = -0.18, p = 0.09), suggesting that the participation gradient is not strictly
monotonic in education. Household income tercile entered negatively, with respondents from the lowest
tercile 11.4 percentage points more likely to participate than those from the highest tercile (p < 0.001).
Urban  residence  and  male  gender  were  also  positively  associated  with  participation.  Country  fixed
effects were statistically significant, indicating that country-specific factors not captured by individual
covariates account for meaningful variation in participation rates.

For  full-time  platform  participation  as  primary  income  source,  the  determinants  exhibited  a
partially different pattern. Education remained negatively associated with full-time participation, but the
magnitude was larger (β = -0.46, OR = 0.63, p = 0.003). Male respondents were substantially more likely
to engage in full-time gig work, particularly in ride-hailing and delivery (β = 0.61, OR = 1.84, p < 0.001).
Household income remained negatively associated. Vietnam and the Philippines had higher full-time
participation rates conditional on covariates, while Thailand had the lowest.

Table 3 presents OLS earnings regressions, with the natural logarithm of monthly earnings as the
dependent variable. Median full-time gig earnings (in USD purchasing-power-parity terms) were 247 in
Vietnam,  314  in  the  Philippines,  289  in  Indonesia,  and  358  in  Thailand.  After  controlling  for  hours
worked and demographic covariates, full-time platform earnings were approximately 4.7 per cent lower
than earnings from comparable non-platform work in the same country (p = 0.038), though the difference
was statistically significant only after pooling countries; country-specific estimates were directionally
similar  but  less  precisely  estimated.  The  earnings  premium  for  males  in  platform  work  (relative  to
females) was 9.2 per cent (p = 0.002), larger than the corresponding non-platform premium of 5.8 per

Qualitative themes
Reflexive thematic analysis of the 40 interviews identified four overarching themes that recurred
across countries: (1) the autonomy paradox;  (2) the social protection gap; (3) opportunities for skills
development; and (4) intergenerational tensions over the status of platform work. The autonomy paradox
describes the tension between perceived control over one's schedule and the binding constraints imposed
by  platform  algorithms,  peak-hour  incentive  structures,  and  customer  ratings.  A  24-year-old  food
delivery driver in Hanoi described the experience as 'free in the morning to choose when to start, but by
lunchtime  the  app  decides  for  me.'  The  social  protection  gap  surfaced  repeatedly  in  narratives  about
illness, injury, and pregnancy, with  platform workers articulating  awareness  of and concern over  the
absence  of  health  insurance,  paid  sick  leave,  and  maternity  protection.  A  27-year-old  e-commerce
courier in Manila put it directly: 'When I get sick, I just stop. There is no one paying.' Opportunities for
skills development were mentioned by a subset of workers, particularly in customer service, financial
management,  and  digital  tool  use.  Intergenerational  tensions  surfaced  in  conversations  about  family
expectations,  with  several  respondents  describing  parental  disappointment  that  they  had  not  entered
'proper'  jobs,  and  others  framing  platform  work  as  a  temporary  stepping  stone  to  professional
employment.

The empirical  findings  paint a  complex portrait  of the post-pandemic labour market  for young
Southeast  Asians.  Platform-mediated  gig  work  occupies  a  substantial  and  growing  share  of  youth
employment,  with  38  per  cent  of  the  four-country  urban  sample  engaging  in  at  least  some  form  of
platform work and 23 per cent of those reporting it as their primary income source. The participation
pattern is concentrated among less-educated, lower-income, urban, and male workers, consistent with

Theoretically, the findings are most consistent with a hybrid characterisation of platform work as
occupying  a  distinctive  position  between  the  formal  and  informal  sectors.  Earnings  differentials  are
modest in magnitude but not negligible, and earnings volatility is substantially higher than in comparable
non-platform  work.  The  qualitative  findings  on  the  autonomy  paradox  extend  Wood  et  al.'s  (2019)
analysis to a different empirical context and confirm the generality of the tension between perceived
flexibility and algorithmic constraint. The social protection gap finding aligns with the broader literature
on social protection floors and the imperative to extend coverage to non-standard employment.

Policy implications  are several.  First,  the social protection gap suggests urgent  need for policy
innovation  to  extend  health  insurance,  maternity  protection,  and  contributory  pension  schemes  to
platform workers without forcing rigid reclassification into traditional employment categories. Portable
benefits, contributory account structures linked to platform-mediated earnings, and public co-funding
mechanisms  warrant  serious  experimentation.  The  Indonesian  BPJS  Ketenagakerjaan  extension  to
platform drivers and the Vietnamese voluntary social insurance scheme represent steps in this direction;
both could benefit from cross-country learning. Second, platform regulation needs to balance protection
against rigidity that might suppress the legitimate flexibility benefits that workers value. Light-touch
frameworks  that  establish  basic  minimum  standards,  transparent  algorithmic  communication,  and

Several limitations should be acknowledged. The urban focus of the sample limits generalisation
to  rural  areas  where  platform  infrastructure  and  labour  market  dynamics  differ.  The  cross-sectional
design  constrains  causal  inference  about  the  longer-term  consequences  of  platform  engagement;
longitudinal tracking of the same individuals would strengthen the analysis. Self-reported earnings are
subject  to  measurement  error,  particularly  for  platform  work  where  income  is  irregular  and  often
unreported. The qualitative sample, while rich, captures perspectives at a particular moment in time and
in particular cities. Despite these limitations, the convergence of cross-country quantitative patterns and
qualitative  themes  provides  a  coherent  account  of  the  structure  and  experience  of  platform  work  for
young Southeast Asians.

A  noteworthy  dimension  of  the  post-pandemic  landscape  is  the  role  of  digital  remittances  and
platform-based  gig  earnings  in  supplementing  household  incomes  during  periods  of  macroeconomic
shock.  Survey  respondents  reporting  full-time  platform  work  were  significantly  more  likely  to  be
primary or co-primary household earners than would be expected from their demographic profile alone,
suggesting that platform work substitutes for formal employment under conditions of elevated economic
uncertainty. The intersection of platform work, household financial resilience, and the macroeconomic
recovery merits further investigation, particularly as ASEAN governments calibrate the next phase of
social protection extension.

Gender  dynamics  within  the  platform  economy  display  interesting  patterns.  While  male
respondents  dominate  the  visible  ride-hailing  and  delivery  segments,  female  respondents  are  over-
represented  in  online  retail  (Shopee  Live,  TikTok  Shop),  beauty  and  wellness  services,  and  remote
freelance work. Earnings within female-dominated segments are typically lower than in male-dominated
segments,  but  flexibility  benefits  are  reported  as  more  salient.  The  intersection  of  gender,  platform
segment,  and  care  responsibilities  emerged  as  a  particularly  important  theme  in  the  Philippine  and
Indonesian interview material, where women platform workers spoke at length about juggling platform
income with childcare and household responsibilities.

Comparative  reflections  across  the  four  countries  reveal  both  common  patterns  and  country-
specific dynamics. Vietnamese platform workers benefit from a relatively young platform ecosystem
and rapidly expanding domestic demand, but they face constraints from a tightening labour market in
formal  sectors  that  draws  away  potential  platform  supply.  Filipino  platform  workers  operate  in  an
environment characterised by extensive overseas labour migration culture and a long-standing tradition
of remittance-supported households; platform work is often discussed as a substitute for or complement
to migration. Indonesian platform workers operate at the largest scale in absolute terms and benefit from
the most developed domestic super-app ecosystem, but they also face complex regulatory negotiations
as  authorities  determine  their  classification.  Thai  platform  workers  operate  in  a  relatively  older
demographic structure where platform participation among the very young is lower than the regional
average, and where the integration of foreign workers (Myanmar, Cambodian, Laotian) into the platform
economy adds an additional layer of analytical complexity that future research should address.

Conclusion

This study has examined the post-pandemic transformation of labour markets in Southeast Asia,
focusing on the rise of the gig economy and its relationship to youth employment. Using a survey of
1,200 young workers in Vietnam, the Philippines, Indonesia, and Thailand and 40 in-depth interviews,
the analysis has documented substantial platform participation, identified key determinants (education,

and articulated four overarching themes from worker narratives. The findings contribute comparative
evidence to  a literature  historically dominated by single-country case studies and provide a basis for
policy  innovation  in  social  protection,  platform  regulation,  and  skills  development.  The  empirical
patterns  suggest  that  policy  makers  should  treat  platform  work  neither  as  a  uniformly  positive  nor
uniformly negative phenomenon but as a hybrid form of work that requires tailored responses. Future
research could extend the analysis through longitudinal tracking, expansion to additional countries, and
deeper engagement with platform-firm strategies and the algorithmic management practices that shape
worker experience.

The structural context within which platform work has expanded in Southeast Asia is shaped by
three  intersecting  forces:  rapid  digitalisation  of  consumer-facing  services,  persistent  informality  in
labour markets, and policy frameworks designed for an earlier era of standard wage employment. Each
of these forces  operates  with  country-specific intensity. Indonesia and the Philippines combine large
young  populations  with  large  informal  sectors  and  active  super-app  ecosystems;  Vietnam  combines
rapid digitalisation with a tightening formal labour market; Thailand combines an ageing demographic
structure with a sophisticated digital economy and a significant migrant worker presence. The interaction
between  these  forces  produces  distinct  platform  labour  landscapes  across  the  four  economies,
contributing to the heterogeneity observed in the empirical results.

From  the  perspective  of  platform  firms,  the  post-pandemic  period  has  been  characterised  by
intense competition, consolidation pressures, and growing scrutiny over commission structures, driver
incentives,  and  algorithmic  management  practices.  Grab's  merger  discussions  with  regional  players,
Gojek's integration with Tokopedia to form GoTo, Foodpanda's strategic reviews, and Lazada-Shopee
competition  have  all  influenced  the  operational  conditions  experienced  by  platform  workers.  The
interview  material  in  the  present  study  captured  worker  awareness  of  these  dynamics,  with  several
respondents  noting  changes  in  commission  rates,  surge  multipliers,  and  rating  systems  that  they
perceived as shifts in the platform-worker relationship over the past two to three years.

Skills  development  in  the platform  economy  operates through several  channels.  First,  platform
workers acquire customer service, navigation, and self-management skills that are transferable, at least
in principle, to other employment. Second, platform workers gain familiarity with digital tools, financial
transactions, and customer-facing communication that have value in subsequent jobs. Third, a subset of
platform  workers  leverage  their  earnings  to  invest  in  formal  education,  vocational  training,  or
microenterprise  development.  Survey  responses  in  the  present  study  indicated  that  14  per  cent  of
platform workers had used platform earnings to invest in further education or training in the previous
two years, with the share highest in the Philippines (19 per cent) and Indonesia (17 per cent) and lowest
in Thailand (8 per cent). These investments in human capital suggest that platform work, for at least
some workers, functions as a productive bridge rather than a dead-end.

Mental  health  and  wellbeing  among  platform  workers  emerged  as  a  distinct  theme  in  the
qualitative data, particularly in the post-pandemic period. Respondents articulated stress associated with
earnings unpredictability, exposure to traffic and safety risks (in the case of ride-hailing and delivery),
and the social isolation of solo work patterns. A 26-year-old Grab driver in Bangkok described the toll
of seven-day work weeks during peak periods: 'I make more money, but I cannot remember the last time
I saw my friends.' Although the present study does not include validated mental health measures, the
qualitative material suggests an important agenda for future research on the wellbeing implications of
platform participation, ideally combining survey measures with longitudinal designs.

including improved savings behaviour and access to micro-credit, but also raises concerns about data
privacy  and  the  algorithmic  determination  of  credit  access  based  on  platform  performance  metrics.
Future research could productively integrate platform participation data with household survey data on
financial behaviour to assess the broader welfare consequences of these complementarities.

The international migration context surrounding the four study economies is also relevant. The
Philippines, Indonesia, and Vietnam are major sources of overseas labour migrants, while Thailand hosts
substantial migrant labour from Myanmar, Cambodia, and Laos. Platform work intersects with migration
in multiple ways: returning migrants use platform work as a transition employment; would-be migrants
accumulate  savings  through  platform  work  prior  to  departure;  and  migrant  workers  within  Thailand
engage in platform work despite formal restrictions. The interview material suggested that migration
aspirations and experiences shape the meaning that young workers attach to platform engagement, with
some viewing it as preparation for international opportunities and others as an alternative to migration.
These dynamics warrant further targeted investigation.

Finally,  the  rapid  evolution  of  platform  technology  —  including  the  integration  of  artificial
intelligence into algorithmic management, dynamic pricing, and worker-customer matching — raises
questions about the future trajectory of platform work. The interview data captured worker awareness
of growing AI integration, with several respondents expressing concern about reduced earnings stability
and  increased  monitoring  intensity.  The  competitive  dynamics  among  platforms  also  produce
uncertainty: as platforms experiment with subscription models, gig-worker pooling arrangements, and
AI-driven  dispatch,  the  experience  of  platform  work  may  shift  further  from  the  current  state.  Policy
frameworks  should  anticipate  these  technological  dynamics  rather  than  focus  exclusively  on  current
arrangements.

In sum, the post-pandemic emergence of the gig economy in Southeast Asia represents both an
adaptation to immediate disruption and a longer-term structural shift in the organisation of work. Young
workers  occupy  a  particularly  visible  and  dynamic  position  within  this  shift,  both  contributing  to
platform growth and bearing the brunt of its precarity. A policy and research agenda that takes platform
work seriously — neither romanticising flexibility nor dismissing it as mere informalisation — will be
essential to ensure that the gig economy delivers broad-based welfare gains.

education,  and  in  the  alignment  between  training  and  labour  market  demand.  Vietnam  has  invested
