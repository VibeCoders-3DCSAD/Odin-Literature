ABSTRACT

Research  on  the  labour  agency  of  remote‐based  online  freelancers  has  often  struggled  to  account  for  the  interplay  between 
platform dynamics, broader labour market conditions, and gendered household responsibilities. This article addresses this gap 
by proposing an integrated analytical framework that synthesises these dimensions to examine how freelancers exercise their 
labour agency in choosing between different online jobs—decisions which are vital to their platform success. Drawing on in‐ 
depth  interviews  with  25  Filipino  freelancers,  the  findings  show  that  freelancers  exercise  their  agency  by  employing  two 
overarching strategies: optimising their financial and temporal resources. These strategies are shaped by platform logics, as well 
as by constraints and opportunities linked to freelancers' wider labour market position and private‐sphere responsibilities. The 
discussion demonstrates the framework's analytical value by illustrating how these strategies produce uneven implications for 
freelancers' longer‐term platform  trajectories, adding nuance to  debates  about the uneven gains of remote  platform work.

2 | Conceptualising the Labour Agency of Online 
Freelancers

2.1 | Labour  Agency Shaped by the Platform 
Environment

On online freelance platforms, freelancers are presented with a 
vast  array  of  job  opportunities  and  must  carefully  determine 
which  ones  to  pursue  (Stephany  et  al.  2021;  Kässi  and 
Lehdonvirta 2018; Kuek et al. 2015). This selective approach is 
further  reinforced  by  measures  implemented  by  platforms  to 
lower the number of bids per job. These were introduced partly 
in  response  to  a  substantial  oversupply  of  labour  and  a  bid 
volume that far exceeds the number of available jobs (Beerepoot 
and  Lambregts  2015;  Gefen  et  al.  2016),  which  have  made  it 
increasingly difficult and time‐consuming for clients to identify 
suitable freelancers.  Platforms such as Upwork and  Freelancer 
now  require  freelancers  to  use  a  certain  number  of  tokens,  re-
ferred to respectively as ‘connects’ and ‘bids’, when bidding for 
jobs.  Each  platform  provides  a  small  number  of  tokens  free  of 
charge,  and  freelancers  must  pay  for  additional  tokens.  By 
doing so, the platforms incentivise them to carefully review job 
posts  to  ensure  they  match  their  skills  and  availability 
(Upwork n.d.‐a;  Freelancer n.d.).

While  existing  research  has  touched  on  how  freelancers  select 
the jobs they pursue, to my knowledge, no study has examined 
this decision‐making process in‐depth. Nonetheless, prior work 
suggests  that  freelancers'  choices  are  shaped  by  the  platform 
environment.  On  one  hand,  this  environment  is  structured  by

what  Jarrahi  et  al.  (2020)  term  ‘platformic  management’—the 
broad set of technological resources and rules wielded by online 
freelance  platforms  to  enable  and  manage  work  (p.  6).  A 
prominent example is freelancers' platform reputation, typically 
based  on  client  feedback  and  performance  ratings,  which  plat-
forms  actively  collect  and  prominently  display.  On  the  other 
hand,  the  platform  environment  is  structured  by  informal 
platform norms, rules, and standards, such as the considerable 
value  that  clients  give  to  freelancers'  platform  reputation 
(Author's own 2023). Consequently, research has identified the 
imperative  to  build  and  maintain  a  strong  reputation  as  a 
notable  driver  of  freelancers'  job  selection  strategies.  Particu-
larly,  new  freelancers  select  easy,  short‐term  jobs  to  quickly 
generate  client  feedback  (Anwar  and  Graham  2020;  Beerepoot 
and  Lambregts  2015;  Lehdonvirta  et  al.  2019;  Idowu  and 
Elbanna 2020; Kinder et al. 2019) and freelancers, whether new 
or experienced, are wary of bidding for challenging jobs as they 
fear  bad  reviews  (Nemkova  et  al.  2019).  Additionally,  they 
carefully  scrutinise  clients  before  bidding  for  jobs  to  avoid  dis-
agreements  that  might  hamper  their  platform  reputation 
(Anwar  and  Graham  2020;  Bucher  et  al.  2021;  Jarrahi 
et  al. 2020; Kinder  et al.  2019; Wood  and Lehdonvirta 2023).

As  illustrated,  the  platform  environment  shapes  freelancers' 
bidding  decisions and  strategies, yet  their behaviour  cannot be 
adequately  understood  through  this  lens  alone.  Freelancers' 
objectives, dependencies, interests, and actions are embedded in 
a  multiplicity  of  contexts—historical,  temporal,  institutional, 
spatial, and social—each offering opportunities and constraints 
that  are  vital  to  understanding  their  economic  behaviour  (Van 
Doorn  and  Shapiro  2023;  Bögenhold  2019;  Welter  2011).  As 
articulated within the structure‐agency debate (e.g., see Giddens 
[1979]  and  Jessop  [2001]),  their  actions  are  subject  to  multiple 
‘structures’,  that  is,  conditions  that  both  enable  and  constrain 
human  action,  which  influence  if,  how,  and  why  freelancers 
seek  to  assert  their labour  agency  (Coe  and Jordhus‐Lier  2011; 
Van Doorn and Shapiro 2023). This article argues that situating 
freelancers  within  a  wider  set  of  structures  enables  a  more 
complex  and  nuanced  understanding  of  heterogeneity  in  how 
they exercise their agency (Bögenhold 2019), thereby addressing 
critiques  of

The analytical  scope  is further  broadened by  incorporating  the 
private realm. As highlighted by feminist‐oriented scholars who 
challenge  the  notion  that  structure  is  solely  defined  by  the 
capitalist  system  (Coe  and  Jordhus‐Lier  2011),  freelancers' 
bidding decisions and strategies cannot be separated from their 
private  lives.  These  scholars  advocate  for  a  more  expansive 
understanding  of  labour  agency,  one  that  accounts  for  how 
workers  navigate  gender  ideologies,  dynamics  of  social  repro-
duction and household  livelihood  strategies (Coe  and Jordhus‐ 
Lier  2011;  Massey  1995;  Katz  2001;  Lier  2007;  Mezzadri 
et  al.  2022).  This  resonates  with  Handy's  (1989)  notion  of 
portfolio  marriages,  which  describes  how  households  balance 
work  portfolios  by  mixing  risk  and  security  and  combining 
short‐term  income  with  long‐term  gains.  Similarly,  Bögenhold 
(2019)  argues  that  shifting  the  analytical  lens  from  the  indi-
vidual  to  the  household  level  reveals  how  inner  family 
dynamics shape employment  strategies.

Inspired  by  these  studies,  this  article  adopts  an  analytical 
approach  that  foregrounds  the  role  of  gender  norms,  social 
reproduction,  and  household  livelihood  strategies  in  shaping 
freelancers'  bidding  decisions  and  strategies.  In  doing  so,  it 
contributes  to  a  small  but  growing  body  of  research  on  the 
labour  agency  of  online  freelancers  that  acknowledges  its  gen-
dered  nature  (e.g.,  Foong  et  al.  2018;  Dunn  et  al.  2021; 
Anwar  2022;  James  2022,  2024).  These  studies  generally  argue 
that  women's  agency  is  relatively  constrained  by  the  unequal 
burden  of  social  reproduction,  resulting  in  online  freelance 
labour reinforcing the gender inequalities existent in traditional

labour markets. However, with the exception of Anwar (2022), 
these studies are situated in the Global North. This geographic 
concentration  limits  our  understanding  of  how agency  unfolds 
in different socio‐economic contexts, where gendered norms of 
capitalist  production  and  social  reproduction  may  differ  subs-
tantially.  By  focusing  on  the  Philippines,  this  article  helps 
redress  this  imbalance  and  shows  that  insights  derived  from 
Global  North  contexts  are  not  always  directly  transferable  to 
Global South settings.

3 | Data  and Methodology

Figure  1 presents  the  analytical  framework  proposed  in  the 
article.  Its  analytical  value  is  examined  by  focussing  on  the 
following  research  question:  how  do  freelancers  exercise  their 
agency  in  choosing  online  jobs?  Two  sub‐questions  were 
formulated:

–  What strategies do freelancers use in choosing online jobs?

–  How  are  these  strategies  shaped  by  considerations  about 
the  platform  environment,  the  wider  labour  market,  and 
freelancers' private life?

To  answer  these  questions,  this  article  draws  on  qualitative 
data  that  constitutes  the  first  round  of  data  collection  of  a 
larger  longitudinal  research  project  on  the  labour  market 
agency  of  online  freelancers.  From  June  to  August  2023,  I 
conducted in‐depth interviews with Filipino freelancers who 
were  recruited  through  two  Facebook  groups  dedicated  to 
communally  supporting  Upwork  freelancers  from  the  Phil-
ippines.  In  these  groups,  I  posted  a  call  for  participants, 
inviting  any  freelancer  residing  in  the  Philippines  who  had 
created  an  Upwork  profile  within  the  past  6  months,  to 
respond.

I chose to recruit freelancers who were new to Upwork because, 
unlike  experienced  freelancers  with  an  established  platform 
reputation  to  whom  work  flows  more  easily,  they  have  to 
actively  search  for  and  choose  online  jobs  to  bid  for  (Wood 
et  al.  2019a;  Anwar  and  Graham  2020;  Jarrahi  et  al.  2020).  In 
the call for participants, I also noted that participants would be 
offered  a  monetary  reward  of  15  euros  (approximately  850 
Philippine  pesos),  a  common  approach  used  by  researchers  to 
recruit  online  freelancers  (e.g.,  see  Wood  et  al.  (2019b),  Wood 
and Lehdonvirta (2023), and James (2024)). Approximately 500 
people  responded,  after  which  purposive  sampling  was  used, 
aiming  for  diversity  in  terms  of  place  of  residence  (level  of  ur-
banisation),  sex,  and  the  type  of  services  offered  (see  Table  1). 
Ultimately,  25  participants  were  selected  who  broadly  reflect 
the characteristics of Filipino online freelancers. Most reside in 
highly  urbanised  cities,  are  women,  and  provide  lower‐skilled 
services  such  as  administrative  support  and  customer  service 
(Beerepoot  and  Oprins  2022;  Beerepoot  and  Lambregts  2015). 
Participants  ranged  in  age  from  22  to  40,  with  one  participant 
aged  52.  To  protect  anonymity,  pseudonyms  were  assigned. 
Importantly, all but one participant had actively applied for jobs 
on  Upwork  at  the  time  of  the  interview;  the  remaining  partic-
ipant  was  waiting  for  clients  to  reach  out.  His  inclusion 
nevertheless  proved  valuable,  as  his  approach  reflected  con-
siderations arising from the broader labour market and private 
realm.

4 | Contextual  Background

4.1 | Labour  and Gender in the Philippines

The Philippines is classified as a lower‐middle‐income country, 
with a gross national income per capita of $3950 in 2023 (World 
Bank  2024).  Over  the  past  decades,  driven  by  the  remarkable 
growth  of  the  information  technology  business  process  out-
sourcing  (IT‐BPO)  sector,  the  Philippine  economy  has  transi-
tioned from an agriculture‐based to a service‐oriented economy 
(Beerepoot  and  Oprins  2022;  World  Bank  2023).  The  country's 
well‐educated, English‐proficient workforce, has positioned the 
Philippines  as  the  second‐largest  recipient  of  internationally 
relocated service activities (Kleibert 2015; Lambregts et al. 2016; 
IBPAP  2022).  Today,  the  IT‐BPO  sector  employs  over  1.5  mil-
lion  Filipinos,  offering  not  only  substantial  employment  but 
also  relative  job  stability  and  affordable  access  to  healthcare 
insurance,  advantages  often  lacking  in  other  sectors  of  the 
economy (IBPAP 2022; Soriano and Cabañes 2019). Participants 
residing  in  highly  urbanised  areas  reported  that  such  opportu-
nities were generally within reach and therefore did not express 
concerns  about securing traditional  offline employment.

Despite  these  advantages,  there  are  also  some  challenges  asso-
ciated  with  employment  in  the  IT‐BPO  sector.  Workers  often 
face  long  and  exhausting  commutes,  along  with  limited  time‐ 
space  flexibility  and  few  opportunities  for  personal  and  career 
growth.  As  a  result,  despite  the  sector's  reputation  for  being 
‘hip’  and  prestigious,  its  popularity  has  begun  to  decline—an 
observation confirmed by the participants in this study (Soriano 
and  Cabañes  2019).  In  addition,  employment  in  the  IT‐BPO 
sector  is  primarily  accessible  to  college  graduates  in  highly  ur-
banised areas (Soriano and Cabañes 2019), while alternative job 
opportunities  for  less‐educated  workers  and  those  in  rural 
regions  remain  limited  (World  Bank  2023).  Participants’  ac-
counts echoed these structural divides. The Philippines has also 
been  struggling  with  a  substantial  number  of  working  poor. 
Despite  having  an  income,  they  are  unable  to  lift  themselves 
and  their  families  out  of  poverty  (World  Bank  2023)  and  this 
problem  has  likely  worsened  over  the  past  few  years,  wherein

annual  inflation  was  high  (around  6%  in  2022  and  2023),  but

Resulting  from  these  challenges  and  following  a  decade‐long 
government policy which promotes labour emigration, millions 
of  Filipinos  choose  to  work  abroad  (Opiniano  and  Ang  2024). 
Labour migration is so ingrained in Philippine society that 7% of 
households  are  home  to  overseas  Filipino  workers  (OFW)  and 
20%  of  Filipinos  are  hoping  to  live  abroad  (Valmonte  2023). 
However,  only  two  participants  considered  overseas  work  a 
viable  option  in  the  near  future.  Most  were  reluctant  to  leave 
their families or lacked  the required financial resources.

Another  challenge  is  the  relatively  low  female  labour  force 
participation rate (World Bank 2021). Just over half of Filipino 
women is formally1 part of the labour force versus nearly 75% of 
men  (PSA  2024b).  The  gap  is  largest  among  married  men  and 
women  aged  between  20  and  39,  with  nearly  90%  of  women 
attributing  their  absence  to  social  reproductive  responsibilities 
(World Bank 2021). As for women who are formally employed, 
particularly  those  at  peak  reproductive  ages  between  25  and 
39  years  old,  they  often  work  fewer  hours  than  men  and  are 
more likely to be self‐employed. This suggests that women more 
frequently  choose  employment  that  provides  the  flexibility 
needed  to  balance  waged‐  with  social  reproductive  labour 
(World Bank 2021). These patterns were reflected in this study's 
interviews,  where  many  women  emphasised  the  need  to  com-
bine waged labour  with caregiving tasks.

In  an  effort  to  mitigate  these  labour  market  challenges,  the 
Philippine  government  has  initiated  road  shows  and  technical 
training  programmes  aimed  at  promoting  and  supporting 
online  freelance  work  as  an  alternative  livelihood  for  socio‐ 
economically  disadvantaged  groups,  including  rural  workers, 
non‐college graduates, and women (Beerepoot and Oprins 2022; 
DOST  2015;  Peña  and  Yao  2022;  PIA  2025).  Although  the 
impact of these initiatives remains unclear and comprehensive 
statistics  on  Filipino  participation  in  online  freelancing  are 
lacking,  existing  evidence  shows  that  Filipinos  are  dis-
proportionally  active  on  online  freelance  platforms  (Stephany 
et  al.  2021;  Upwork  2020).  Their  engagement  is  driven  by 
opportunities  for  upward  career  mobility,  autonomy  and  flexi-
bility,  higher  wages,  and  more  quality  time  with  their  families 
(Beerepoot and Oprins 2022;  Soriano and Cabañes  2019).

Clients check for applications just minutes after their job

guarantee  that  a  client  views  the  application.

tories  illustrate  the  central  premise  of  this  article's  analytical 
framework:  that  freelancers'  labour  agency  on  Upwork  takes 
shape  through  the  combined  influence  of  the  platform  en-
vironment,  their  labour‑market  position,  and  the  gendered  or-
ganisation of their private lives.

6 | Discussion  and Conclusion

Online  freelance  platforms  are  known  to  fragment  work  into 
smaller,  short‐term  tasks,  reflecting  a  broader  transformation 
in  the  world  of  work  (Graham  et  al.  2017).  This  article  ad-
vances  our  understanding  of  how  online  freelancers  navigate 
this  shift  by  introducing  a  novel  analytical  framework  that 
situates  freelancers'  labour  agency  within  three  interrelated 
dimensions:  the  platform  environment,  freelancers'  broader 
labour  market  position,  and  their  gendered  roles  in  the 
domestic sphere. Focussing on freelancers' agency in choosing 
between online jobs, the findings show that they draw on two 
overarching  sets  of  strategies—optimising  their  financial 
resources  and  managing  their  temporal  resources—when 
deciding  which  jobs  to  pursue.  Their  ability  and  willingness 
to  follow  the  financial  and  temporal  logics  embedded  in  Up-
work's  platform  environment  are  shaped  by  constraints  and 
opportunities  in  the  wider  labour  market  and  their  private 
lives.  Namely,  these  contexts  condition  freelancers'  access  to 
time and money, shaping the very strategies they rely on when 
navigating  the  platform  environment.  These  structurally  con-
ditioned  strategies,  while  rational  within  their  broader 
socio‑economic  context,  often  lead  them  to  deprioritise  Up-
work, limiting their ability to accumulate platform reputation 
on  which  future  access  to  work  depends.  Prior  research  has 
shown  that  such  reputation  and  the  on‐platform  experience 
that  underpins  it  is  essential  for  generating  a  stable  flow  of 
work  and  income  from  the  platform  over  time  (Pallais  2014; 
Demirel et al. 2021; Graham et al. 2017).

Building  on  these  findings,  the  analytical  framework  demon-
strates its value by offering new insights into why some workers 
succeed  on  online  freelance  platforms  while  others  do  not 
(Oprins  2025).  The  findings  reveal  gendered  inner  family 
dynamics that shape freelancers' capacity to adhere to platform 
logics.  Many  female  participants  pursued  online  freelancing  to 
gain  the temporal  flexibility needed  to  balance paid work with 
social  reproductive  labour,  which  they  perceived  as  their  pri-
mary  responsibility.  Unlike  women  in  the  Global  North,  who 
often struggle to adhere to temporal platform logics due to time 
constraints  imposed  by  caregiving  duties  (James  2022,  2024; 
Dunn et al. 2021), women in this study experienced greater time 
flexibility, needed to effectively select and land jobs and build a 
solid  platform  reputation.  This  was  facilitated  by  support  from 
extended family and the ability to manage caregiving alongside 
online  freelance  work.  In  contrast,  male  participants  in  urban 
settings who identified as primary breadwinners often adopted 
moonlighting  strategies  (Shevchuk  et  al.  2024)  or  hybrid  en-
trepreneurial  models  (Bögenhold  2019a),  combining  full‐time, 
local  employment  with  online  freelancing.  Their  limited  avail-
ability  and  inability  to  invest  flexible  time  in  securing  online 
freelance  jobs  placed  them  at  a  disadvantage  in  building  their 
Upwork career.

The  implications  for  gender  inequality  in  the  Philippines  are 
complex and remain ambiguous. On one hand, online freelance 
labour  reinforces  existing  inequalities  by  pushing  women  into 
precarious  platform  work  due  to  prevailing  gender  norms 
around  caregiving  (Anwar  2022).  Online  freelance  platforms 
may  also  perpetuate  gendered  constructions  of  care  by  confin-
ing  women  to  the  domestic  sphere  rather  than  challenging 
these  norms  (James  2024).  On  the  other  hand,  prior  research 
shows  that  online  freelancing  does  not  necessarily  result  in 
income  precarity  (Elbanna  and  Idowu  2022)  and  there  are 
numerous  examples  of  freelancers  in  low‐wage  countries 
earning incomes that are relatively high compared to traditional 
employment  (Elbanna  and  Idowu  2022;  Soriano  et  al.  2021; 
D'Cruz  and  Noronha  2016;  Anwar  and  Graham  2021;  Graham 
et  al. 2017).

The framework also sheds light on how freelancers strategically 
source  digital  jobs.  While  platforms  like  Upwork  are  designed 
as  self‐contained  digital  ecosystems  with  lock‐in  effects—such 
as  non‐transferable  reputation  systems  (Kinder  et  al.  2019; 
Jarrahi et al. 2020; Sutherland et al. 2020; Rahman 2021; Bucher 
et  al.  2021)—freelancers often  navigate a broader  ecosystem  of 
multiple  digital  (labour)  platforms  and  personal  networks  (see 
also  Kinder  et  al.  (2019),  Rahman  (2021),  Anwar  and  Graham 
(2020), and Malik et al. (2021)). Securing jobs elsewhere reduces 
their  adherence  to  the  financial  and  temporal  logics  of  any 
single  platform.  These  findings  suggest  that  online  freelance 
platforms  should  be  understood  as  one  component  within  a 
broader  portfolio  of  income‐generating  strategies  (see  also 
Shevchuk et  al. 2024).

To  conclude,  this  article  demonstrates  how  broadening  the 
analytical scope enriches debates on the uneven gains of remote 
platform  work.  While  the  empirical  focus  is  on  Upwork  free-
lancers  in  the  Philippines,  the  findings  offer  valuable  insights 
into  online  freelance  labour  in  the  Global  South  and  illustrate 
how  freelancers’  lived  experiences  diverge  from  those  in  the 
Global  North.  The  proposed  analytical  framework  provides  a 
useful  tool  for  future  research  to  deepen  our  understanding  of

ABSTRACT
Research on the labour agency of remote‐based online freelancers has often struggled to account for the interplay between
platform dynamics, broader labour market conditions, and gendered household responsibilities. This article addresses this gap
by proposing an integrated analytical framework that synthesises these dimensions to examine how freelancers exercise their
labour agency in choosing between different online jobs—decisions which are vital to their platform success. Drawing on in‐
depth interviews with 25 Filipino freelancers, the findings show that freelancers exercise their agency by employing two
overarching strategies: optimising their financial and temporal resources. These strategies are shaped by platform logics, as well
as by constraints and opportunities linked to freelancers' wider labour market position and private‐sphere responsibilities. The
discussion demonstrates the framework's analytical value by illustrating how these strategies produce uneven implications for
freelancers' longer‐term platform trajectories, adding nuance to debates about the uneven gains of remote platform work.
