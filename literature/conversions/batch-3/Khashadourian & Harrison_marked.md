I N T R O D U C T I O N

Abstract
This article creates a typology to assess four categories of financial well-being
based on a combination of household financial ratios. Most financial well-

being scales are based on subjective measures (i.e., perceptions), as objective
markers have not reliably encapsulated financial well-being. We define a con-

ceptual model, the equilibrium model of the household (EMH), and use dis-
criminant analysis to extract categories of financial well-being. The continuum

of these categories is financially distressed (lowest), financially fragile, finan-

cially stable, and financially flourishing (highest). Our results demonstrate
these categories are consistent with the Consumer Financial Protection

Bureau's (CFPB) Financial Well-Being Scale, a subjective scale. Higher CFPB
scores were associated with higher category ranks. Our results provide addi-

tional evidence to support construct validity of the CFPB scale and may offer

more actionability to the CFPB scores because specific financial outcomes/
behaviors associated with our categories of financial well-being correspond to

ranges of the CFPB scale. However, we argue that the claim whereby the CFPB
scale measures a concept beyond traditional financial measures is imprecise

and may even reflect the existence of noise in the CFPB's data, raising ques-
tions about its reliability.

The CFPB scale does not measure financial well-
being based on financial data. Respondents are not asked
about their level of income, assets, and liabilities or rate

in this article as objective measures). In fact, the devel-

financial status could potentially measure. To make their
point, researchers refer to the high standard deviation of
the distribution of scores in the national sample to claim
that none of the objective measures can independently
determine financial well-being (CFPB, 2017). In other
words, since any objective measure, such as income,
expenses, savings, and so forth, does not perfectly corre-
late to financial well-being, the CFPB scale offers a better,
or more complete, picture of financial well-being. This
idea implies that individuals or households with identical
objective characteristics may have different perceptions
regarding their level of financial well-being (Nanda &
Banerjee, 2021). Among other things, in this article, we
attempted to evaluate this claim.

| LITERATURE R EVIEW

Differences between perceptions and behavior, the latter
primarily measured through objective indicators, are well
recognized in the literature related to financial well-
being. Financial well-being is often defined as a compos-
ite measure that includes both subjective (perceptive)
and objective (behavioral) factors (CFPB, 2015; Gutman
et al., 2015; Gutter & Copur, 2011; Joo, 2008; Robb &
Woodyard, 2011). There are some minor differences in
existing definitions. For example, Joo (2008) prefered to
use the term financial wellness rather than financial
well-being because the former encompasses a more func-
tional rather than cognitive definition and implied a mul-
tidisciplinary application. On the other hand, the Center
for Financial Services Innovation (CFSI) used the term
financial health in describing a financial behavior that
increases the financial resilience and opportunity of indi-
viduals and households (Gutman et al., 2015).

Yet, while defined as a measure that includes both
objective and subjective components, when actually ana-
lyzed, almost all existing scales or tools are based solely
on subjective measures (Netemeyer et al., 2018; Prawitz
et al., 2006; Sorgente & Lanz, 2019). One exception is the
CFSI's Financial Health Segments where objective

measures are included to some extent. However, these
variables are measured categorically and are not
extracted from detailed household-level financial data
(Gutman et al., 2015). Other studies appear to adopt a
similar approach. Robb and Woodyard (2011), studied
the relationship between financial knowledge and finan-
cial satisfaction in predicting best practice financial
behavior based on Joo's financial wellness framework.
Gutter and Copur (2011) introduced a subjective scale,
the In Charge Financial Distress/Financial Well-Being
(IFDFW) for college students by including objective mea-
sures of financial behavior in the form of a few binary
variables.

To the authors' knowledge, since the publication of
the aforementioned CFPB (2017) report, with a few nota-
ble exceptions, neither the CFPB's Financial Well-Being
Scale nor any other financial well-being scale, directly
compares or contrasts to a complete set of household
financial ratios. A recent study, heavily influenced by the
CFPB scale, combined self-reported measures of subjec-
tive financial well-being with data from bank records to
create a separate “observed” scale of financial well-being
for Australian households (Comerton-Forde et al., 2022).
The study concluded that both the self-reported subjec-
tive scale and the “observed” financial well-being scale
maintained a high level of consistency in discriminating
between a wide range of financial outcomes. Most nota-
bly, the study identified saving behavior as the single,
most important predictor of financial well-being on both
scales. That being said,
there were some differences
between the two scales. For example, the observed scale
incorporated temporal aspects of financial well-being,
such as considering the number of months within a
12-month period in which household expenses exceeded
80% of incoming contributions. The subjective measure,
on the other hand, did not include temporal dimensions
of financial well-being. The study also found some differ-
ences between the self-reported vs. observed scales in
terms of their association with certain predictors of finan-
cial well-being (Comerton-Forde et al., 2022, p. 147).

Using the CFPB's Financial Well-Being Scale's
national dataset, another recent study used structural
equations modeling to estimate the effect of indicators of
financial capability including savings, financial shocks,
income volatility, as well as demographic variables on a
score on the CFPB scale (Anvari-Clark & Ansong, 2022).
The authors concluded that factors within the control of
the household, such as saving behavior, had a substantial
predictive influence on financial well-being as compared
with structural or environmental variables. In particular,
results from their study indicated that when saving
increased by one standard deviation, a CFPB score
increased by 0.437 standard deviations.

2.1

| Research questions

Our study explored the following three research ques-
tions. First, to what extent could a composite measure of
household financial ratios be employed to delineate dis-
tinct states of financial well-being? This was the central
question in our study. Existing research measuring
household finances utilizing income, expenditures, sav-
ings, and so forth, are unable to independently explain a
significant portion of the variance of financial well-being.
We intend to explore whether a combination of such
measures in the form of simple financial ratios allow us
to define financial well-being in a consistent manner.
More details provided in the next section will explain the
conceptual model utilized.

Second, what level of congruence exists between the
identified typologies of financial well-being, which we
have named the equilibrium model of the household
(EMH), and the CFPB's Financial Well-Being Scale?
Once the EMH identified typologies using household
financial ratios, we evaluated these objective measures in
terms of their correspondence to the CFPB scale. This
was made possible since participants provided household
financial data and completed the CFPB scale. Finally,
can a systematic mapping of the stages of financial well-
being onto the CFPB's Financial Well-Being Scale be
achieved to establish mutually exclusive and comprehen-
sive intervals of CFPB scores?

2.2

| Conceptual model

We defined typologies of household financial well-being
using self-reported data from a small random sample of
individuals in the United States. Using this dataset, we
calculated four critical financial ratios. These ratios mea-
sure households' success in managing a budget, generat-
ing a positive cash flow, servicing household debt, and
maintaining adequate levels of liquidity. By combining
these ratios, we created an ordinal ranking of stages of
financial well-being. Defining stages along the proverbial
financial well-being continuum could be a valuable tool
in the toolbox of any financial coach or counselor. These
categories can typify common financial
issues. Then
counselors can offer options to help families move along
the financial well-being continuum toward higher levels
of financial well-being and upward mobility. Key aspects
of our conceptual model, along with details regarding the

2.2.1

| Equilibrium model of the household

Households that attain financial success often do so
through the gradual accumulation of wealth. This accu-
mulation process typically hinges on the judicious access
to and utilization of credit. However, for the majority of
households, the ability to access credit and build wealth
is contingent upon the generation of income and the
establishment of a surplus of income over expenses. This
framework, termed by the authors as the EMH, suggests
that the journey toward the apex of financial well-being
commences with the establishment of a consistent
income stream and the capacity to maintain a flexible
monthly budget without being unduly burdened by
excessive debt. This combination creates the environment
for households to increase their savings rate, a factor
already determined to be imperative to financial well-
being.

Within the EMH framework, a household can flourish
by generating a surplus of cash flow, which, when coupled
with access to credit, serves to bolster the household's net
asset position. Moreover, the success of households in
accumulating wealth is contingent upon their adeptness in
managing financial uncertainties by maintaining sufficient
liquidity to mitigate potential risks. The EMH framework
delineates four pivotal financial ratios and establishes a
hierarchy among these ratios to define various stages of
household financial well-being.

Household financial ratios
Financial ratios can measure certain aspects of the finan-
cial health of the household. A study by Griffith (1985) is
one of the early writings on this topic. Subsequent efforts
by other scholars, including a widely referenced article
by Greninger et al. (1996), identified several important
financial ratios and benchmarks for household financial
well-being. DeVaney (1994), argued that household
financial ratio analysis serves three purposes; to objec-
tively measure family finance, to track progress in the
financial situation through time, and to help financial
educators, counselors, and planners guide families.

Benchmarks
Utilization of household financial ratios provides a con-
venient method to evaluate household financial well-
being. However, in building a new financial well-being
scale using these ratios, complications may arise unless
these ratios are complemented by proper benchmarks or
reference values. With few exceptions, there is no a prior
knowledge of how different ranges of values of these
ratios may relate to household financial well-being. Early
studies looked at central measures, such as averages and
medians of sample data. Different datasets were used to
collect the necessary information for such analyses. In
one example, the financial ratios recommended by Grif-
fith (1985) were calculated for 3824 households from the
1983 Survey of Consumer Finances using a multistage
probability sampling (Prather, 1990). A correlation analy-
sis examined the relationships between age, income, and
financial ratios. Another approach used expert opinion to
create such benchmarks (Greninger et al., 1996). Using
the Delphi method, Greninger et al. (1996) collected
information from a panel of 156 financial planners and
educators to identify reference values for household
financial ratios. Later studies used correlational methods,
including logistic regression and other multivariate clas-
sification models, to evaluate relevant benchmarks to
explain one or more aspects of household financial well-
being (DeVaney, 1994). Common measures or definitions
of well-being included financial fragility, financial insol-
vency, and household financial satisfaction. Having a net

Financial ratios defining financial well-being
Previous studies looked at
the relationship between
aspects of financial well-being and household financial
ratios. For example, Garrett and James III (2013), looked
at the relationship between household financial satisfac-
tion and financial ratios. A study by Brunetti et al. (2012)
looked at the role of household portfolio composition on
financial fragility in Italy. One interesting aspect of this
study is the classification of households into four nomi-
nal
conditions:
(1) whether income exceeded or was equal to planned
household expenses, and (2) whether liquid household

categories based on two general

assets were sufficient
to meet potential unexpected
expenses. The resulting categories were good financial
position,
financially fragile, dissaving, and financially
constrained. Households were assumed to be in a good
financial position if they met both conditions. The house-
hold was considered financially fragile if it met the first
condition but not the second. If the household did not
meet the first condition but met the second, the house-
hold was labeled as dissaving, over-consuming but liquid.
Finally, the household was considered financially con-
strained if they did not meet either condition (Brunetti
et al., 2012). The study used a multinominal logit model
to associate household characteristics with the above-
referenced classifications.

Another notable effort in this regard was the CFSI's
Financial Health Segmentation Study which defined
seven distinct segments within three overall
tiers of
financial health based on patterns of responses to a set
of 24 objective and subjective questions
(Gutman
et al., 2015). Some financial ratios were included among
these questions. The idea of creating categories of finan-
cial well-being was also suggested by Khashadourian
(2016). He identified seven distinct categories of financial
well-being. These ranged from financial distressed at the
lowest level to upwardly mobile at the highest level. Kha-
shadourian used a combination of values of household
financial ratios within a data-assisted financial coaching
network.

2.3

| Significance of the study

The significance of this study is twofold. First, developing
stages of financial well-being using financial ratios can
make a strong case for content validity of the CFPB scale
if the stages are shown to be consistent with the CFPB
scale. Second, the study also examines whether stages of
financial well-being may correspond to distinct intervals
of
the CFPB scale. Identifying such intervals would
enhance the use of the CFBP measure by making it more
actionable.

As an example of being more actionable, employers
could use their employee CFPB scores, in combination
with other types of employee data, such as salary records,
and so forth, to identify necessary actions to improve
employees' financial well-being (Bailey, 2019). In this
context, stages of financial well-being may help identify
areas of financial strength or weakness facing the house-
hold. Proper courses of action can then be taken to
address these, thereby improving the effectiveness of
financial capability programs offered by employers, uni-
versities, and community-based organizations. Finally,
our approach can also help financial institutions in creat-
ing new products to improve the financial capability of
their clients.

process

To collect data, we developed an online survey consisting
of two filtering variables, age and income. The survey
also consisted of 40 multicomponent questions covering
categories of a typical household budget and their assets
and liabilities. Each participant also completed the CFPB
survey. Capturing reliable and detailed household finan-
cial data through a survey proved to be complicated. We
had to balance the amount of time needed to complete
the survey against both the reliability of the collected
data and the minimum amount of data elements required
for a complete data analysis. Before collecting data for
the main study,
the research team spent nearly

changing responses to certain data fields from “required”

financial well-being had average CFPB scores that were
significantly different from other categories. Results, not
reported here, indicated that differences between all cate-
gories were statistically significant, at least at α = 0.1,
except for the pairwise comparison between Financially
Fragile and Financially Distressed states.

Estimated marginal means and their corresponding
95% confidence intervals in Table 8 presented how the
intervals for the three lower categories of financial well-
being overlap. While partially overlapping categories,
such as the overlap between Financially Stable and
Financially Fragile, was not a significant concern in
terms of practicality and identification of states of finan-

the same cannot be claimed for the

category was greater than the upper bound of the Finan-
cially Fragile category,
thus completely masking the
Financial Fragile category.

Part of the issue of overlapping categories could be
attributed to large random effects in the sample data
(Seltman, 2012). We ran the ANOVA model again by
including the random effects of the survey participants.
Inclusion of the random effects lowered the within-group
variability and improved the group-level effects, resulting
in significant improvements in pairwise comparisons. All
pairwise comparisons in the model that included the ran-
dom effects were statistically significant at the 5% level
except for the last two groups. In addition, the updated

model provided nonoverlapping categories for the first
three groups (see Table 8). In reference to research ques-
tion three, our study was not successful in identifying an
exhaustive and nonoverlapping ranges of financial well-
being categories along the CFPB scale.

| DISCUSSION

Our results suggest that financial ratios, considered an
objective measure, can effectively categorize financial
well-being. In addition, these categories are consistent
with the CFPB scale, which we consider to be a subjec-
tive measure. This implies the CFPB scale might capture
objective financial behavior to a greater extent than pre-
viously thought. We contend that our results enhance the
validity of the CFPB survey as a simple and practical way
to measure financial well-being. We arrived at this con-
clusion because the EMH objective measure of financial
well-being introduced in our study was developed inde-
pendently from the CFPB scores. Statistical tests not only
implied that significant differences existed across catego-
ries of financial well-being, but that declining CFPB aver-
age scores in each category were found to be in line with
the expected ordering of such categories along the EMH
financial well-being continuum.

A second objective of the study, defining nonoverlap-
ping and exhaustive categories of financial well-being,
was not supported since results were inconclusive across
the entire domain of possible CFPB scores. On the one
hand, confidence intervals for the two categories of
Financially Distressed and Financially Fragile had signif-
icant overlaps. On the other hand, the intervals for the
two middle categories, Financially Fragile and Finan-
cially Distressed, while not overlapping, appeared to be
too narrowly defined given the high variability of the
CFPB scores. Several factors may contribute to this prob-
lem, but four obvious ones include (i) the multifaceted
nature of financial ratios in influencing financial well-
being, (ii) measurement and calculation errors, (iii) small
sample size, and (iv) high standard deviation of the

| C ON C L U S I ON

We conclude this article by recommending that further
research look at ways to reduce the noise in the CFPB
data. We also believe that any future attempt to explore
the research questions in our study should be carried out
using separate datasets. Defining stages of financial well-
being, as in the EMH, would benefit from having a larger
data sample to improve the classification and categoriza-
tion of financial well-being. At the same time, contrasting
CFPB scores to the EMH categories of financial well-being
and attempting to define nonoverlapping ranges of CFPB
scores requires strict adherence to a minimum acceptable
effect size, which limits the desired sample size.

In addition, a significant contribution of this research
is not only to support prior studies results related to the
importance of household savings rates on financial well-
being, but also the progressive interaction of the four
financial ratios on the movement along the financial
well-being continuum. The initial building block to
financial well-being is a positive savings rate followed by
no more than 65% of a household budget composed of
fixed expenses. Add to these first two, a household with
<15% of their income dedicated to repaying nonmortgage
debt, moves that household to a higher stage of financial
well-being. The last factor, maintaining at least 200% of
monthly expenses in liquid assets, moves the household
to the Financially Flourishing stage. Also of note is the
congruence between perceived financial well-being and
the behaviors that either precede or follow that percep-
tion. Knowledge of this progressive interaction, combined
with the CFPB score, can enhance the effectiveness of
financial counseling efforts in the United States.
