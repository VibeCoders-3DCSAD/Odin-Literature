ABSTRACT In the context of advancing intelligent accounting systems within the broader field of
computational methods and data processing, this study focuses on overcoming critical limitations inherent in
conventional accounting information classification methodologies. These traditional systems often exhibit
low adaptability to rapidly evolving data characteristics and a heavy dependence on manually crafted
feature engineering pipelines. Existing approaches predominantly rely on predefined statistical heuristics
or shallow machine learning models, which are insufficient for capturing the intricate, non-linear, and
high-dimensional relationships embedded in modern financial datasets—especially those generated by large-
scale enterprise resource planning systems. To address these constraints, we propose a novel deep feature
extraction framework grounded in convolutional autoencoder architectures. This framework is designed
to autonomously learn multi-level hierarchical feature representations directly from raw journal entries,
eliminating the need for domain-specific feature selection. Our approach incorporates a dual-objective
design that simultaneously performs input reconstruction and classification, allowing the network to preserve
input fidelity while promoting discriminative learning. Furthermore, an adversarial training component is
introduced to enhance generalization performance, especially under conditions of class imbalance and input
noise—both common issues in financial transaction data. We evaluate our method on a comprehensive real-
world accounting dataset composed of annotated journal logs across multiple business units. Experimental
results demonstrate substantial improvements in both classification accuracy and anomaly detection F1-score
when compared to traditional baseline models such as logistic regression and decision trees. Our findings
illustrate the potential of deep learning-based architectures for intelligent accounting systems and contribute
to the growing body of research on adaptive, scalable, and automated solutions for financial data analysis
and auditing in computational finance.

anomaly detection, adversarial representation learning.

Initial efforts to address these challenges introduced
rule-oriented frameworks that formalized domain expertise
into structured processing routines. These systems were
built on deterministic rules and heuristic decision trees to

flexibility and scalability [6], [7]. As financial records diver-

and invoices—these static systems struggled to maintain
performance. The hard-coded rules failed to accommodate
edge cases, inconsistencies, and emerging transaction types
that deviated from their predefined templates. Moreover, the
maintenance of such systems required continuous involve-
ment of domain experts, who needed to update rules and logic
in response to policy changes, system migrations, and market
innovations. This led to a bottleneck in scalability, as the
cost and complexity of upkeep escalated with the volume
and variety of data. In dynamic enterprise settings, where

foundation for automating accounting classification, they
highlighted the need for more intelligent systems capable of
evolving with data and minimizing dependence on manual
rule curation. These limitations ultimately catalyzed the
exploration of more flexible computational strategies that
could adapt to new data distributions and capture subtle
patterns beyond the reach of hand-coded logic [9], [10].

To improve adaptability and reduce dependence on rigid
rule structures, statistical learning models were developed
to identify classification patterns from annotated financial
logs. By utilizing supervised algorithms that learned decision
boundaries from examples, these models achieved better
generalization across various scenarios, especially when
the transaction data exhibited variability in structure and
semantics. Techniques such as logistic regression, decision
trees, support vector machines, and ensemble methods
like random forests became widely adopted in accounting
information classification tasks [11], [12]. These approaches
offered notable improvements over rule-based systems by
allowing models to infer data-label relationships directly
from historical observations, rather than relying solely on
expert-crafted rules. For instance, models could learn to
associate specific account codes with textual descriptions
or identify fraud-prone behavior from numerical attributes.
This shift introduced a more data-centric paradigm that was
faster to deploy across similar task domains and moderately
resilient to noise or inconsistencies in input. Nevertheless,
despite these advancements, statistical learning models still
required significant human intervention in the form of
handcrafted features. Designing effective input variables—
such as transaction frequency, monetary thresholds, counter-
party identifiers, categorical tags, and semantic patterns—
demanded deep domain knowledge and iterative refinement.
These features often captured only surface-level relationships
and failed to reflect deeper contextual interactions among
financial entities or temporal sequences [2], [13]. As financial
datasets became more complex, encompassing multimodal
records like textual justifications, invoice images, and tem-

engineering approaches struggled to keep pace. Moreover,
the fixed feature representations limited model flexibility
when applied to new institutions or accounting standards,
often necessitating manual redesign and retraining. Models
also became vulnerable to changes in data distribution or
previously unseen transaction types, resulting in performance
degradation under real-world deployment conditions. The

II. RELATED WORK
A. DEEP LEARNING IN ACCOUNTING
Recent advancements in deep learning have significantly
transformed the landscape of accounting information sys-
tems by enabling automatic classification and intelligent
processing of complex financial data. One of the most
influential applications has been the utilization of neural
networks, particularly convolutional neural networks (CNNs)
and recurrent neural networks (RNNs), to extract semantic
and structural features from large volumes of unstructured or
semi-structured accounting data. These models can process
various forms of financial inputs, such as invoices, ledgers,
balance sheets, and narrative reports, identifying patterns that
are imperceptible to traditional statistical methods [1], [19].
The integration of word embeddings like Word2Vec, GloVe,
and BERT within these frameworks has allowed models to

and semantics of accounting texts. Unlike general-purpose
document classification tasks, accounting documents often
contain heterogeneous data types including numerical tables,
textual narratives, and structured metadata. This complexity

statements where spatial positioning conveys semantic roles.
Graph neural networks (GNNs) have also emerged as a

significant hurdle due to the diversity in document formats,
accounting standards, and terminologies across organizations

document analysis is also gaining momentum, with attention
visualization and concept attribution being employed to
enhance model transparency and trustworthiness for auditors
and regulators [1], [30].

C. AUTOMATED ACCOUNTING CLASSIFICATION
Automated classification of accounting entries and transac-
tions is a foundational component of intelligent financial
systems, and recent deep learning techniques have markedly
improved performance in this domain by enabling sophis-
ticated feature extraction from raw and semi-structured
inputs. Traditional accounting systems rely heavily on
manually crafted rules and extensive domain knowledge,
which are both labor-intensive and inflexible to evolving
data landscapes [19]. In contrast, modern systems utilize
end-to-end deep learning pipelines that learn hierarchical
representations of transactions directly from data, cap-
turing intricate dependencies between attributes such as
date, amount, category, vendor, and narrative description.
Transformer-based models, particularly those adapted for
tabular and sequence data, have demonstrated exceptional
capabilities in capturing both local syntactic features and
global semantic patterns in accounting records. Sequence-to-
sequence models have been applied to tasks such as account
mapping, VAT code classification, and regulatory tagging,
while attention mechanisms ensure that critical transaction
features receive higher importance during classification [20],
[31]. Transfer learning and domain adaptation techniques
further enhance these models by incorporating financial

B. EXPERIMENTAL DETAILS
All experiments are conducted using PyTorch on NVIDIA
A100 GPUs. We adopt the Adam optimizer with a learning
rate of 1e-4 and apply a cosine annealing schedule for
dynamic adjustment. Batch size is fixed at 64 for all datasets
to ensure consistency. For training stability, we employ gradi-
ent clipping with a threshold of 1.0 and use mixed-precision
training via AMP. Dropout with a rate of 0.1 is applied in
all feedforward and attention layers to mitigate overfitting.
Models are trained for 100 epochs with early stopping
based on validation loss to prevent overfitting. We employ
a standard train/validation/test split of 70%/15%/15% for
all datasets. For temporal datasets such as Compustat and
CSMAR, we strictly follow chronological order to avoid
data leakage. Input sequences of length 12 (corresponding
to 12 months or quarters, depending on the dataset) are used
to predict the next step. For sequence modeling, we adopt a
Transformer-based architecture with four encoder layers and
a model dimension of 256. Positional encodings are learned
rather than fixed to accommodate variable-length sequences.
Layer normalization is applied after each sublayer, and
residual connections are maintained throughout the network.
Multi-head attention uses 8 heads with dimensionality
32 each. For classification tasks within the EDGAR dataset,
we convert the task into a multi-class setting where each class
corresponds to a transaction category. Cross-entropy loss is
used as the objective function. In contrast, for regression
tasks such as revenue or budget forecasting, we employ
Mean Squared Error (MSE) loss. Mean Absolute Percentage
Error (MAPE) and Root Mean Square Error (RMSE) are

V. CONCLUSION AND FUTURE WORK
In this study, we address the persistent challenges of adapt-
ability and reliance on manual feature design in traditional
accounting information classification systems. To overcome
these issues, we propose a deep feature extraction frame-
work based on convolutional autoencoders. This model is
designed to learn hierarchical representations directly from
raw accounting data, eliminating the need for handcrafted
features. Our framework integrates joint objectives for data
reconstruction and classification, enhanced by adversarial
training to promote robustness against noisy and imbalanced
entries. Experimental results on real-world accounting logs
show that our method significantly improves classification

accuracy by 12 percentage points and anomaly detection
F1-score by 15 percentage points compared to conventional
shallow models. These findings highlight the efficacy of deep,
automated feature learning in capturing complex patterns
within accounting datasets, aligning with the growing trend
of scalable, intelligent financial systems.

limitations. First, the convolutional-autoencoder architecture,
while effective, may still struggle with extremely sparse

mitigate overfitting risks, especially in low-resource settings.

the robustness and generalization of our framework across
